MÉNIÈRE'S DISEASE

an ear that fills and fades ✎

1Idiopathic endolymphatic hydrops

episodic vertigofluctuating SNHLtinnitusfullness
  • Onset usually 35–60 years · starts in one ear; the other joins in a substantial minority
  • Disease = idiopathic · syndrome (secondary hydrops) = head injury, viral labyrinthitis, syphilis, Cogan, autoimmune, delayed hydrops after a profound loss

2Too much endolymph

schematic: Reissner's membrane (coloured line) bows into the scala vestibuli
duct + saccule distend→small ruptures: K⁺-rich endolymph meets perilymph→hair cells poisoned = attack→heals, partial recovery
Theories: poor sac absorption (small, poorly pneumatised sac / duct) · vasomotor spasm · allergy, autoimmunity · salt + water retention · hypothyroidism · virus

3The attack and the interval

  • Vertigo: sudden, rotatory, 20 minutes to several hours, nausea, vomiting, sweating · clusters, remissions of weeks–months · lies still · no loss of consciousness
  • Hearing: fluctuating SNHL, worse in attacks, low frequencies first → permanent + flat over years · distortion, diplacusis, loud-sound intolerance
  • Tinnitus low, roaring, louder in attacks · fullness may change before one = warning
  • Drum normal · attack: horizontal-rotatory nystagmus (Dhingra: to healthy ear; Lee: changes with phase) · Tullio: sound-induced vertigo

4Criteria (Bárány / AAO-HNS 2015)

Needs
Definite≥2 spontaneous vertigo episodes, 20 min – 12 h · audiometric low–mid SNHL in that ear at least once · fluctuating aural symptoms · not better explained
Probable≥2 vertigo / dizziness episodes, 20 min – 24 h · fluctuating aural symptoms · not better explained
12-hour limit separates it from vestibular migraine: about ten times commoner, overlaps, commonest misdiagnosis
1995 categories replaced · Stage by mean 0.5, 1, 2, 3 kHz (worst audiogram, last six months): 1 ≤25 · 2 26–40 · 3 41–70 · 4 >70 dB

5Tests

  • PTA: low-frequency SNHL, rising curve → flattens later
  • Speech 55–85% between attacks · recruitment = cochlear
  • ECochG: ↑ SP/AP (Dhingra >30%, Lee >0.35)
  • Glycerol 1.5 mL/kg: positive = 10 dB at two adjacent frequencies, or 10% discrimination
  • Caloric reduced on affected side in ~three-quarters
  • Contrast MRI to exclude schwannoma · delayed contrast MRI shows hydrops (specialist)
DDx: schwannoma · vestibular migraine · recurrent vestibulopathy · SCD · autoimmune / otosyphilis · vertebrobasilar TIA

6Attack & medical control

reassure, lie still→neuro + head impulse→prochlorperazine / dimenhydrinate / diazepam→IV fluids if vomiting
New neurological signs → stroke imaging, not sedatives · keep sedatives short: they slow compensation
  • Lifestyle: salt ~1.5–2 g/day, water, ↓ caffeine + alcohol, stop smoking, stress · no heights / driving in active periods
  • Betahistine (caution in asthma) + thiazide widely used, but BEMED 2016 (221 pts, 48 or 144 mg): no better than placebo → don't hold frequent attackers on it
  • Treat allergy, hypothyroidism, migraine overlap · most eventually controlled (partly natural course)

8The options in detail

  • IT steroid usually first: = gentamicin for vertigo, no hearing risk (Patel 2016: 90% vs 87% fewer attacks at two years)
  • IT gentamicin titrated: vertigo control 80–90%, hearing loss 4–30%
  • Meniett pressure pulses: no clear benefit; US guideline advises against
  • Sac surgery (decompression = shunt): ≥75% control after failed medical (Sood 2014), but no placebo proof
  • Neurectomy: high control, hearing kept, needs craniotomy

9Before you destroy

Before destroying one ear, check the other: up to half get the second ear over twenty years; bilateral ablation → oscillopsia
Recruitment makes aids hard to fit · sacculotomy, Cody tack = historical · US guideline 2020: IT steroid for active disease, gentamicin / labyrinthectomy after failure

10Exam traps ✗

  • Every dizzy patient called Ménière's without documented hearing loss
  • No MRI to exclude schwannoma
  • Vestibular sedatives for months
  • Gentamicin when the other ear may be affected
