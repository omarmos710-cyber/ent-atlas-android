@stand Vertigo is the illusion of movement, usually spinning, and in most patients it comes from the inner ear. Peripheral causes account for about 85%, but the duty of the doctor is to catch the dangerous central minority. Four questions sort most cases: how long the attacks last, what triggers them, whether hearing is affected, and whether there are neurological signs.

# Overview | A symptom, not a diagnosis.
## Definitions
### Vertigo
A false sense of rotation or movement of oneself or the surroundings, from asymmetry in the vestibular system.
### Other kinds of dizziness
- **Disequilibrium:** unsteadiness without a sense of rotation.
- **Oscillopsia:** the visual world jumps with head movement, typical of bilateral vestibular loss.
- **Presyncope:** light-headedness from reduced cerebral perfusion (cardiac, orthostatic, hypoglycaemic).
- **Physiological:** motion sickness and height vertigo.
## Peripheral or central
!tree Causes of vertigo
Peripheral | BPPV, vestibular neuritis | labyrinthitis, Ménière's disease, perilymph fistula, superior canal dehiscence, ototoxicity, trauma, syphilis
Central | Posterior circulation stroke | vertebrobasilar insufficiency, Wallenberg syndrome, vestibular migraine, multiple sclerosis, cerebellar and brainstem tumours, epilepsy
Other | Cervical | ocular, psychogenic, cardiovascular
!!

## Triggers
Head position (BPPV), loud sound or pressure (superior canal dehiscence, fistula), straining, neck movement, standing up (orthostatic hypotension), and migraine triggers.
## Hearing
Hearing loss or tinnitus points to the labyrinth (labyrinthitis, Ménière's disease, fistula) or the cerebellopontine angle, or to an AICA stroke.
## Neurological symptoms
Double vision, slurred speech, numbness, weakness, difficulty swallowing, new severe headache: central until proven otherwise.

# Examination | Eyes, head and gait.
## Nystagmus
Look in the primary position and on gaze, with and without fixation (Frenzel glasses). Peripheral nystagmus is horizontal-torsional, beats away from the affected side and is suppressed by fixation; it is stronger looking towards the fast phase (*Alexander's law*).
## Head impulse test
A rapid head turn: a catch-up saccade means the vestibulo-ocular reflex on that side has failed, a peripheral lesion.
## HINTS
In acute continuous vertigo with nystagmus: a *normal* head impulse, direction-changing nystagmus, or a vertical skew deviation points to a stroke.
## Positional tests
The Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre for posterior canal BPPV, and the supine roll test for the lateral canal.
## Other bedside tests
Romberg and tandem gait, head shake, dynamic visual acuity (losing more than five lines suggests bilateral loss), fistula test, and a full neurological and cranial nerve examination.
!table Peripheral or central findings
Finding | Acute peripheral | Central
Nystagmus | Mixed horizontal-torsional | Pure vertical or torsional, direction-changing

Head impulse | Abnormal | Normal
Pursuit | Normal | Saccadic
Saccades | Normal | Dysmetric
Gait | Can walk | Often cannot stand
Other CNS signs | Absent | Often present

# Peripheral causes | The common ones.
## Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo
### Mechanism
Otoconia dislodged from the utricle settle in a semicircular canal, most often the posterior canal, and move with gravity when the head changes position (*canalolithiasis*). It often follows head injury or ear infection, or occurs in older people without cause.
!anat s79_14 s81_18 | The crista ampullaris with its cupula, and the macula of the utricle and saccule whose otoconia cause BPPV.
### Picture
Brief vertigo, under a minute, on lying down, rolling over or looking up. No hearing loss.
### Dix-Hallpike nystagmus
Rotatory and geotropic, after a latency of a few seconds, rising then fading within a minute, reversing on sitting up, and fatiguing on repetition.
### Treatment
The *Epley manoeuvre* repositions the debris into the utricle; about 80% are cured by one manoeuvre. Semont manoeuvre and Brandt-Daroff exercises are alternatives. Most cases resolve in months anyway. Surgery (posterior canal occlusion or singular neurectomy) is rarely needed.
!fig d7-1 | The Epley manoeuvre for posterior canal BPPV, with the position of the patient and of the otolith debris in the canal at each step.
!table Positional vertigo: peripheral or central
Feature | Peripheral | Central
Latency | Present | Often none
Duration | Under a minute | Persists
Fatigue | Yes | No
Direction | Fixed, torsional | Changing or vertical
Vertigo | Marked | Often mild
!!
## Vestibular neuritis
### Mechanism
Probably viral inflammation of the vestibular nerve or ganglion.
### Picture
Sudden, severe, continuous vertigo with nausea and vomiting, lasting days and settling over two to three weeks, without hearing loss. Nystagmus beats away from the affected side; the head impulse is abnormal towards it; calorics are reduced. BPPV follows in up to 15%.
### Treatment
Short-course vestibular sedatives, possibly steroids early, then early vestibular rehabilitation. Failure to improve at two to three weeks means a central lesion, such as a cerebellar infarct, must be excluded.
## Labyrinthitis
As neuritis, but with hearing loss, from viral or bacterial infection of the labyrinth itself.
## Ménière's disease
Attacks of vertigo lasting hours with fluctuating hearing loss, tinnitus and fullness.
## Recurrent vestibulopathy
Ménière-like spells without hearing symptoms. About 60% remit; a minority become Ménière's disease or BPPV.
## Perilymph fistula
Episodic vertigo and fluctuating hearing after stapes surgery, barotrauma, heavy lifting or coughing. Bed rest with the head up and laxatives; explore if worsening.
## Superior canal dehiscence
Vertigo provoked by sound and pressure, autophony and a low-frequency conductive loss with intact reflexes (see the separate topic).
## Bilateral vestibular loss
Oscillopsia and unsteadiness in the dark, without true vertigo, most often from aminoglycosides. Cerebellar ataxia with bilateral vestibular loss is called *CABV*, or *CANVAS* when sensory neuropathy is added.

# Central causes | The ones not to miss.
## Posterior circulation stroke
!table Posterior circulation stroke syndromes
Feature | PICA | AICA | SCA
Vertigo, nystagmus, ataxia | Yes | Yes | Yes
Hearing loss, tinnitus | No | Yes | No
Facial palsy | Sometimes | Yes | No
Dysphagia, hoarseness | Yes | No | No
Contralateral loss of vibration and position | No | No | Yes
!!
!fig d7-2 | Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome from occlusion of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery.
### Wallenberg syndrome
Violent vertigo with hoarseness, dysphagia, ipsilateral Horner syndrome, ipsilateral facial and contralateral body loss of pain and temperature, and ataxia.
## Vertebrobasilar insufficiency
Attacks lasting minutes in patients over 50, with visual disturbance, diplopia or drop attacks, sometimes on neck movement.
## Vestibular migraine
Attacks of vertigo, minutes to hours, with or near migraine headache, often in young women with a family history; response to migraine treatment supports the diagnosis.
## Multiple sclerosis
Young adults with vertigo and pendular or dissociated nystagmus, and other neurological episodes.
## Other central causes
Cerebellar and brainstem tumours, Chiari malformation, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, encephalitis and epilepsy.

# Other causes | When it isn't the ear or the brain.
## Cervical vertigo
Vertigo with neck movement after a neck injury; controversial and probably over-diagnosed.
## Cardiovascular
Presyncope from arrhythmia, orthostatic hypotension or hypoglycaemia; not true rotatory vertigo.
## Psychogenic
Vague floating or swaying, with anxiety, no nystagmus and normal tests. Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness often follows an acute vestibular event.

# Management | Principles.
!ward Acute vertigo on call
1. Ask about timing, triggers, hearing and neurological symptoms.
2. Examine nystagmus, head impulse, skew and gait (HINTS).
3. Any focal sign, new severe headache, inability to stand, or a normal head impulse with nystagmus: treat as stroke until proven otherwise.
4. Dix-Hallpike only once the neck and the patient's condition allow it.
5. Give antiemetics or vestibular sedatives for a few days only.
!!
## Vestibular rehabilitation
Head and eye exercises that drive central compensation, the mainstay after any peripheral loss.
## Avoid long-term sedatives
They slow compensation.
!pearl Pearls
- An acoustic neuroma usually causes unsteadiness, not severe vertigo.
- Fatigable, delayed, torsional positional nystagmus is peripheral; immediate, persistent or vertical nystagmus is central.
- A positive fistula test: perilymph fistula, cholesteatoma with a fistula, Ménière's disease, superior canal dehiscence, or syphilis.
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Peripheral versus central** vertigo and nystagmus.
- **BPPV:** mechanism, Dix-Hallpike findings, Epley manoeuvre.
- **Vestibular neuritis** versus labyrinthitis.
- **HINTS** in acute vertigo.
- **Wallenberg syndrome.**
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Labelling a stroke as neuritis because the patient is vomiting.
- Missing a normal head impulse in continuous vertigo.
- Keeping patients on vestibular sedatives for weeks.
- Diagnosing BPPV without seeing the nystagmus.
!!
