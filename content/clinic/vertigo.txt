@stand Vertigo is the illusion of movement, usually spinning, and in most patients it comes from the inner ear. Peripheral causes account for about 85%, but the duty of the doctor is to catch the dangerous central minority. Four questions sort most cases: how long the attacks last, what triggers them, whether hearing is affected, and whether there are neurological signs.

# Summary | A symptom, not a diagnosis.
*Vertigo* is a false sense of rotation or movement of oneself or the surroundings, from asymmetry in the vestibular system. Peripheral causes account for about 85%. The history (duration, triggers, hearing, neurological symptoms) and the bedside examination (nystagmus, head impulse, HINTS, Dix-Hallpike) sort most cases. The general approach comes first; each cause then has its own chapter.
!tree Causes of vertigo
Peripheral | [[@Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo|BPPV]], [[@Vestibular neuritis]] | labyrinthitis, Ménière's disease, perilymph fistula, superior canal dehiscence, ototoxicity, trauma, syphilis ([[@Other peripheral causes]])
Central | [[@Posterior circulation stroke]] | vertebrobasilar insufficiency, Wallenberg syndrome, [[@Vestibular migraine]], multiple sclerosis, cerebellar and brainstem tumours, epilepsy
Other | [[@Other causes|Cervical]] | ocular, psychogenic, cardiovascular
!!
## Other kinds of dizziness
- **Disequilibrium:** unsteadiness without a sense of rotation.
- **Oscillopsia:** the visual world jumps with head movement, typical of bilateral vestibular loss.
- **Presyncope:** light-headedness from reduced cerebral perfusion (cardiac, orthostatic, hypoglycaemic).
- **Physiological:** motion sickness and height vertigo.

# Diagnosis | Timing is everything; then eyes, head and gait.
## History
### Duration
!table Duration of attacks
Duration | Typical causes
Seconds | Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo
Minutes | Vertebrobasilar ischaemia, vestibular migraine
Minutes to hours | Ménière's disease, recurrent vestibulopathy, vestibular migraine
Days to weeks | Vestibular neuritis, labyrinthitis, stroke
Variable | Perilymph fistula, superior canal dehiscence
!!
### Triggers
Head position (BPPV), loud sound or pressure (superior canal dehiscence, fistula), straining, neck movement, standing up (orthostatic hypotension), and migraine triggers.
### Hearing
Hearing loss or tinnitus points to the labyrinth (labyrinthitis, Ménière's disease, fistula) or the cerebellopontine angle, or to an AICA stroke.
### Neurological symptoms
Double vision, slurred speech, numbness, weakness, difficulty swallowing, new severe headache: central until proven otherwise.
## Examination
### Nystagmus
Look in the primary position and on gaze, with and without fixation (Frenzel glasses). Peripheral nystagmus is horizontal-torsional, beats away from the affected side and is suppressed by fixation; it is stronger looking towards the fast phase (*Alexander's law*).
### Head impulse test
A rapid head turn: a catch-up saccade means the vestibulo-ocular reflex on that side has failed, a peripheral lesion.
### HINTS
In acute continuous vertigo with nystagmus (the acute vestibular syndrome): a *normal* head impulse, direction-changing nystagmus, or a vertical skew deviation points to a stroke. New hearing loss with acute vertigo ("HINTS plus") also raises concern for an anterior inferior cerebellar artery stroke. HINTS is more sensitive than early MRI, which misses up to 1 in 5 posterior circulation strokes in the first 48 hours, but it is reliable only in the hands of someone trained to do it, and only in patients with continuous vertigo and nystagmus.
!update Current evidence
In acute vestibular syndrome, HINTS had a pooled sensitivity of 95.5% and specificity of 71% for posterior circulation stroke (Krishnan et al., *Eur Stroke J* 2019).
!!
### Positional tests
The Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre for posterior canal BPPV, and the supine roll test for the lateral canal.
### Other bedside tests
Romberg and tandem gait, head shake, dynamic visual acuity (losing more than five lines suggests bilateral loss), fistula test, and a full neurological and cranial nerve examination. A positive fistula test: perilymph fistula, cholesteatoma with a fistula, Ménière's disease, superior canal dehiscence, or syphilis. Formal vestibular tests are covered in [[t:vest]].
## Peripheral or central?
!table Peripheral or central findings
Finding | Acute peripheral | Central
Nystagmus | Mixed horizontal-torsional | Pure vertical or torsional, direction-changing
Fixation | Suppresses | Does not suppress
Head impulse | Abnormal | Normal
Pursuit | Normal | Saccadic
Saccades | Normal | Dysmetric
Gait | Can walk | Often cannot stand
Other CNS signs | Absent | Often present
!!

# Management | Principles.
## Approach
!ward On call: acute vertigo
1. Ask about timing, triggers, hearing and neurological symptoms.
2. Examine nystagmus, head impulse, skew and gait (HINTS).
3. Any focal sign, new severe headache, inability to stand, or a normal head impulse with nystagmus: treat as stroke until proven otherwise.
4. Dix-Hallpike only once the neck and the patient's condition allow it.
5. Give antiemetics or vestibular sedatives for a few days only.
!!
## Vestibular rehabilitation
Head and eye exercises that drive central compensation, the mainstay after any peripheral loss.
!update Current evidence
A Cochrane review found moderate to strong evidence that vestibular rehabilitation is safe and effective for unilateral peripheral vestibular loss (McDonnell and Hillier, 2015).
!!
## Avoid long-term sedatives
They slow compensation.
!ops

# Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo {dz} | Seconds, on lying down.
## Etiology
It often follows head injury or ear infection, or occurs in older people without cause.
## Pathophysiology
Otoconia dislodged from the utricle settle in a semicircular canal, most often the posterior canal, and move with gravity when the head changes position (*canalolithiasis*).
!anat s79_14 s81_18 | The crista ampullaris with its cupula, and the macula of the utricle and saccule whose otoconia cause BPPV.
## Clinical features
Brief vertigo, under a minute, on lying down, rolling over or looking up. No hearing loss.
## Diagnosis
### Dix-Hallpike nystagmus
Rotatory and geotropic, after a latency of a few seconds, rising then fading within a minute, reversing on sitting up, and fatiguing on repetition.
### No routine tests
No routine imaging or vestibular testing. Atypical nystagmus, failure after several manoeuvres and frequent recurrence call for imaging.
### Investigations: what and when
!table BPPV: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Diagnosis (gold standard, posterior canal) | Dix–Hallpike test | Torsional upbeating nystagmus with latency, lasting under a minute
Horizontal canal | Supine roll test | Geotropic or apogeotropic horizontal nystagmus
Not routine | Imaging, audiometry, vestibular function tests | Only if the nystagmus is atypical, manoeuvres fail, or there are other neurological or hearing features
!!
## Differential diagnosis
!table Positional vertigo: peripheral or central
Feature | Peripheral | Central
Latency | Present | Often none
Duration | Under a minute | Persists
Fatigue | Yes | No
Direction | Fixed, torsional | Changing or vertical
Vertigo | Marked | Often mild
!!
## Subtypes and variants: which canal
The **posterior canal** causes about 90 to 95% of BPPV because it is the lowest canal when lying down; the **horizontal (lateral) canal** causes 5 to 15%, and the anterior canal very few. The occasional Epley manoeuvre converts posterior into horizontal canal BPPV.
### Horizontal canal
Diagnosed with the supine roll test, which produces horizontal nystagmus on turning to either side. In the commoner *geotropic* form (beating towards the ground), the side with the stronger nystagmus is affected; in the *apogeotropic* form (beating away from the ground, thought to be cupulolithiasis), the weaker side is affected. Treat with the **Lempert 360 degree (barbecue) roll** towards the healthy side, the **Gufoni manoeuvre**, or prolonged lying on one side; evidence for the apogeotropic form is weak.
### Anterior canal
Down-beating nystagmus with a torsional component, treated by the **Yacovino (deep head-hanging) manoeuvre**. Down-beating positional nystagmus that does not settle must be imaged to exclude a cerebellar lesion.
## Management
### Repositioning
The *Epley manoeuvre* repositions the debris into the utricle; about 80% are cured by one manoeuvre and over 90% after up to four. The Semont manoeuvre is an equal alternative; Brandt-Daroff exercises are less effective. Most cases resolve in months anyway.
!fig d7-1 | The Epley manoeuvre for posterior canal BPPV, with the position of the patient and of the otolith debris in the canal at each step.
### After repositioning
No postural restrictions are needed. Patients can be taught to treat themselves.
### No sedatives
Vestibular sedatives and benzodiazepines have no role in BPPV: they slow compensation and increase falls in the elderly.
### Surgery
Posterior canal occlusion or singular neurectomy is rarely needed.
!update Current evidence
The American BPPV guideline recommends diagnosis with the Dix-Hallpike and supine roll tests, repositioning manoeuvres as treatment, and no routine imaging, vestibular testing or vestibular suppressants (Bhattacharyya et al., *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2017). A Cochrane review found the Epley manoeuvre resolved vertigo in 56% compared with 21% after a sham manoeuvre (Hilton and Pinder, 2014).
!!
### Treatment: what and when
!table BPPV: treatment
Step | Treatment | Course and notes
First line, posterior canal | Epley manoeuvre (or Semont) | About 80% cured by one; repeat up to four times; recheck in 1 month
Horizontal canal | Barbecue (Lempert) roll or Gufoni manoeuvre | By the side and type of nystagmus
Self-treatment and recurrence | Home Epley taught to the patient; Brandt–Daroff exercises are a weaker option | 
Not recommended | Vestibular sedatives (betahistine, prochlorperazine, cinnarizine, benzodiazepines); postural restrictions after the manoeuvre | Slow compensation and cause falls; at most a single antiemetic dose for severe nausea during the manoeuvre
Intractable (rare) | Posterior canal occlusion | After a year of failed manoeuvres
!!
Based on the American guideline (Bhattacharyya et al., *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2017).
## Prognosis
Recurrence is common, in up to a third of patients within four years.

# Vestibular neuritis {dz} | Days of continuous vertigo, hearing intact.
## Etiology
Probably viral inflammation of the vestibular nerve or ganglion.
## Clinical features
Sudden, severe, continuous vertigo with nausea and vomiting, lasting days and settling over two to three weeks, without hearing loss.
## Diagnosis
Nystagmus beats away from the affected side; the head impulse is abnormal towards it; calorics are reduced.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Vestibular neuritis: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
First (bedside) | HINTS: head impulse, nystagmus, test of skew | Abnormal head impulse, unidirectional nystagmus and no skew favour neuritis
Confirmation | Video head impulse test; caloric test | Reduced function of the affected side
Every patient | Audiogram | Normal in neuritis; loss means labyrinthitis (or AICA stroke)
Any central sign, or no improvement at 2 to 3 weeks | MRI with diffusion | Cerebellar or brainstem stroke
!!
## Differential diagnosis
- **Labyrinthitis:** as neuritis, but with hearing loss ([[@Other peripheral causes/Labyrinthitis]]).
- **Cerebellar infarct:** failure to improve at two to three weeks means a central lesion must be excluded ([[@Posterior circulation stroke]]).
## Management
Short-course vestibular sedatives for the first few days, then early mobilisation and vestibular rehabilitation. Steroids started early speed recovery of caloric function in the short term, but do not clearly improve long-term symptoms.
!update Current evidence
Steroids for vestibular neuritis improved caloric recovery at one month but not at 12 months (Fishman et al., 2011).
!!
### Drugs and doses
!table Vestibular neuritis: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Acute vomiting | Prochlorperazine 12.5 mg by deep intramuscular injection, or buccal 3 to 6 mg twice a day | First day or two
Acute, oral | Prochlorperazine 5 mg three times a day; or cyclizine 50 mg up to three times a day | No more than about 3 days
Steroid (optional, within 3 days of onset) | Methylprednisolone 100 mg daily, reduced by 20 mg every 3 days (or prednisolone 1 mg/kg tapered) | Speeds caloric recovery; long-term benefit unproved; check glucose
Every patient | Early mobilisation and vestibular rehabilitation exercises | Start as soon as vomiting settles
Not recommended | Antivirals; prolonged sedatives | 
!!
Doses from the BNF and the steroid trial (Strupp et al., *N Engl J Med* 2004).
## Complications
BPPV follows in up to 15%.

# Other peripheral causes {dz} | The rest of the inner ear.
## Labyrinthitis
As neuritis, but with hearing loss, from viral or bacterial infection of the labyrinth itself ([[t:fistula]]).
## Ménière's disease
Attacks of vertigo lasting hours with fluctuating hearing loss, tinnitus and fullness ([[t:meniere]]).
## Recurrent vestibulopathy
Ménière-like spells without hearing symptoms. About 60% remit; a minority become Ménière's disease or BPPV.
## Perilymph fistula
Episodic vertigo and fluctuating hearing after stapes surgery, barotrauma, heavy lifting or coughing. Bed rest with the head up and laxatives; explore if worsening ([[t:fistula]]).
## Superior canal dehiscence
Vertigo provoked by sound and pressure, autophony and a low-frequency conductive loss with intact reflexes ([[t:scd]]).
## Bilateral vestibular loss
Oscillopsia and unsteadiness in the dark, without true vertigo, most often from aminoglycosides ([[t:sensori]]). Cerebellar ataxia with bilateral vestibular loss is called *CABV*, or *CANVAS* when sensory neuropathy is added.
## Vestibular schwannoma
An acoustic neuroma ([[t:vs]]) usually causes unsteadiness, not severe vertigo.

# Posterior circulation stroke {dz} | The one not to miss.
## Clinical features
!table Posterior circulation stroke syndromes
Feature | PICA | AICA | SCA
Vertigo, nystagmus, ataxia | Yes | Yes | Yes
Hearing loss, tinnitus | No | Yes | No
Facial palsy | Sometimes | Yes | No
Dysphagia, hoarseness | Yes | No | No
Contralateral loss of vibration and position | No | No | Yes
!!
!fig d7-2 | Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome from occlusion of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery.
## Wallenberg syndrome
Violent vertigo with hoarseness, dysphagia, ipsilateral Horner syndrome, ipsilateral facial and contralateral body loss of pain and temperature, and ataxia.
## Diagnosis
HINTS at the bedside ([[@Diagnosis/Examination/HINTS]]); early MRI misses up to 1 in 5 posterior circulation strokes in the first 48 hours.
## Vertebrobasilar insufficiency
Attacks lasting minutes in patients over 50, with visual disturbance, diplopia or drop attacks, sometimes on neck movement.
## Treatment and investigations
!table Posterior circulation stroke: investigations and acute treatment
Role | Test or treatment | When and what it shows
Bedside screen | HINTS plus hearing (HINTS+) and gait | A central pattern is more sensitive than early MRI
First imaging | Non-contrast CT with CT angiography | Haemorrhage; vertebral or basilar occlusion
Gold standard | MRI with diffusion | Infarct; may miss up to 1 in 5 in the first 48 hours, so repeat if doubt remains
Treatment, eligible within 4.5 hours | Thrombolysis by the stroke team (alteplase 0.9 mg/kg, maximum 90 mg; or tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg, maximum 25 mg); thrombectomy for basilar occlusion | Stroke protocol
Treatment, after thrombolysis is excluded or 24 hours after it | Aspirin 300 mg daily for 2 weeks, then long-term antiplatelet | Secondary prevention
!!
Based on NICE NG128 Stroke and TIA in over 16s, and the NICE appraisal of tenecteplase (TA990, 2024).

# Vestibular migraine {dz} | Vertigo with migraine.
## Clinical features
Attacks of vertigo, minutes to hours, with or near migraine headache, often in young women with a family history.
## Diagnosis
Response to migraine treatment supports the diagnosis. It is the commonest misdiagnosis of Ménière's disease ([[t:meniere]]).
## Drugs and doses
!table Vestibular migraine: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Every patient | Regular sleep and meals, hydration, trigger diary; avoid medication overuse | Long term
Acute attack | Antiemetic (prochlorperazine 5 mg or metoclopramide 10 mg) with simple analgesia; a triptan if headache dominates (for example sumatriptan 50 to 100 mg) | Triptans: not in vascular disease
Prevention (frequent attacks) | Propranolol 80 to 160 mg a day; or amitriptyline 10 mg at night, rising to 25 to 75 mg; or topiramate 25 mg rising to 50 mg twice a day | Trial each for 2 to 3 months; topiramate is avoided in women who could become pregnant unless a pregnancy prevention programme is in place
Every patient | Vestibular rehabilitation | 
!!
Doses from NICE CG150 (migraine) and the BNF; the evidence base for vestibular migraine itself is small.

# Other central causes {dz} | Rarer, but central.
## Multiple sclerosis
Young adults with vertigo and pendular or dissociated nystagmus, and other neurological episodes.
## Others
Cerebellar and brainstem tumours, Chiari malformation, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, encephalitis and epilepsy.

# Other causes {dz} | When it isn't the ear or the brain.
## Cervical vertigo
Vertigo with neck movement after a neck injury; controversial and probably over-diagnosed.
## Cardiovascular
Presyncope from arrhythmia, orthostatic hypotension or hypoglycaemia; not true rotatory vertigo.
## Psychogenic
Vague floating or swaying, with anxiety, no nystagmus and normal tests. Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness often follows an acute vestibular event.

# Tips and links | For the examination and the ward.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Peripheral versus central** vertigo and nystagmus.
- **BPPV:** mechanism, Dix-Hallpike findings, Epley manoeuvre.
- **Vestibular neuritis** versus labyrinthitis.
- **HINTS** in acute vertigo.
- **Wallenberg syndrome.**
!!
## Pearls
!pearl Pearls
- An acoustic neuroma ([[t:vs]]) usually causes unsteadiness, not severe vertigo.
- Fatigable, delayed, torsional positional nystagmus is peripheral; immediate, persistent or vertical nystagmus is central.
- A positive fistula test: perilymph fistula, cholesteatoma with a fistula, Ménière's disease, superior canal dehiscence, or syphilis.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Labelling a stroke as neuritis because the patient is vomiting.
- Missing a normal head impulse in continuous vertigo.
- Keeping patients on vestibular sedatives for weeks.
- Diagnosing BPPV without seeing the nystagmus.
!!
