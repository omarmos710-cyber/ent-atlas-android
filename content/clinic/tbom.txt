# Summary | The chronic ear that refuses to heal.
This topic covers two granulomatous infections of the middle ear, each in its own chapter. [[@Tuberculous otitis media]] is infection of the middle ear cleft by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*: a painless, thin discharge, multiple perforations, pale granulation and early facial palsy; it is proved by PCR, histology and culture, and treated with antituberculous drugs. [[@Syphilitic otitis]], much rarer, is included because it shares the picture of bone destruction and a disproportionate hearing loss.

# Tuberculous otitis media {dz} | Painless, thin, and resistant.
## Epidemiology
Uncommon in low-burden countries, but important where tuberculosis is endemic, and rising with drug-resistant strains and immunosuppression, particularly HIV.
## Etiology: routes of infection
- Through the Eustachian tube, from pulmonary disease, classically present in most patients, although the chest film is normal in many.
- Through the blood, from a distant focus.
- In infants, through the tube from infected milk.
## Clinical features
### Symptoms
- **Discharge:** painless, thin and cloudy, persisting despite treatment.
- **Hearing loss:** out of proportion to the symptoms, often mixed, from early ossicular and cochlear involvement.
### Signs
- A thickened drum early on, then *multiple perforations* that merge into one large defect.
- Pale, thick mucosa and abundant pale granulation.
- Bone sequestra in the mastoid.
- Non-tender enlarged cervical and preauricular nodes.
- Facial palsy in about 10%, sometimes the presenting feature in a child.
!pearl Suspect it when
A chronic ear fails ordinary treatment, a mastoidectomy fails, granulation is pale, or facial palsy appears early in a painless ear.
!!
## Diagnosis
### From the ear
- **PCR of the discharge or tissue:** gives an answer within days.
- **Histology:** caseating granulomas with Langhans giant cells.
- **Acid-fast smear and culture:** a negative smear does not exclude it.
!update Current evidence
**Diagnosis.** Tuberculous otitis is easily missed: biopsy of granulations with histology, culture and a rapid nucleic acid test (Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra) on tissue gives the best yield.
!!
### General
Chest imaging, tuberculin skin test or interferon-gamma release assay, and HIV testing.
### CT
Often looks like ordinary chronic otitis media, with more bone erosion and sequestra.
!imaging | Otomastoiditis on CT.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Tuberculous otitis media: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Gold standard | Biopsy of granulations: histology, mycobacterial culture with sensitivities | Caseating granulomas; culture takes weeks
Rapid test | Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (nucleic acid test) on tissue or discharge | Diagnosis and rifampicin resistance in days
Adjunct | Acid-fast smear of discharge | A negative smear does not exclude it
Every patient | Chest X-ray; HIV test; liver and renal function | Pulmonary disease; co-infection; baseline for treatment
Supportive | Interferon-gamma release assay or tuberculin skin test | Infection, not active disease
Imaging | High-resolution CT temporal bone | Extensive erosion, sequestra
!!
## Differential diagnosis
Ordinary [[t:csom|chronic suppurative otitis media]], carcinoma of the middle ear ([[t:tumear]]), granulomatosis with polyangiitis ([[t:gpa]]), and fungal infection.
## Management
### Antituberculous chemotherapy
Standard multidrug therapy, guided by sensitivities, for at least six months: two months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, then four of isoniazid and rifampicin (the WHO regimen for most extrapulmonary tuberculosis), with longer courses for bone or central nervous system involvement.
### Surgery
Limited to biopsy, removal of sequestra and drainage of abscesses or complications. Reconstruction is delayed until treatment is complete, because healing is slow and fistulae are common during active disease; afterwards, repair succeeds in most.
!ops
### Drugs and doses
!table Tuberculous otitis media: drugs and doses
Phase | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Intensive phase | Isoniazid 5 mg/kg (maximum 300 mg), rifampicin 10 mg/kg (450 mg under 50 kg, 600 mg from 50 kg), pyrazinamide (1.5 g under 50 kg, 2 g from 50 kg) and ethambutol 15 mg/kg, once daily | 2 months; fixed-dose combinations are used
Continuation phase | Isoniazid and rifampicin, same doses | 4 months (6 months in total); longer with extensive bone disease or CNS spread, on specialist advice
Children | Isoniazid 10 mg/kg, rifampicin 15 mg/kg, pyrazinamide 35 mg/kg, ethambutol 20 mg/kg daily (WHO weight bands) | Same phases
Every patient | Pyridoxine 10 mg daily with isoniazid (where there is a risk of neuropathy); baseline liver and renal function, visual acuity and colour vision | Directly observed therapy where needed
Drug resistance | Regimen from sensitivities and the TB service | 
Ear | Aural toilet; quinolone drops for secondary infection | Surgery only for sequestra or complications
!!
Doses from NICE NG33 (tuberculosis), WHO consolidated guidelines on tuberculosis treatment and the BNF.
## Complications
- **Facial palsy:** usually recovers well with medical treatment ([[t:facial]]).
- Sequestrum, postauricular fistula, Bezold abscess and labyrinthitis.

# Syphilitic otitis {dz} | Rare, destructive.
## Clinical features
Congenital or acquired syphilis can cause osteitis of the temporal bone with necrosis, and labyrinthitis with profound, often fluctuating sensorineural loss and vertigo.
## Diagnosis
Treponemal tests (FTA-ABS).
### Investigations: what and when
!table Syphilitic otitis: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Screening | Treponemal enzyme immunoassay (EIA) | Positive in past or present infection
Activity | RPR or VDRL titre | Activity and response to treatment
Confirmation | A second treponemal test (TPPA) | 
With otological or neurological signs | Lumbar puncture (with genitourinary medicine); HIV test | Neurosyphilis; co-infection
Ear | Audiogram; CT temporal bone | Fluctuating sensorineural loss; bone erosion
!!
## Management
Penicillin and steroids. Syphilitic labyrinthitis is covered in [[t:fistula]].
### Drugs and doses
!table Syphilitic otitis: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
First line (treat as neurosyphilis) | Procaine penicillin 1.8 to 2.4 million units IM once daily with probenecid 500 mg four times a day | 14 days; with genitourinary medicine
Alternative | Benzylpenicillin 1.8 to 2.4 g IV every 4 hours | 14 days
Penicillin allergy | Doxycycline 200 mg twice a day | 28 days
Jarisch–Herxheimer prevention | Prednisolone 40 to 60 mg daily for 3 days, starting 24 hours before the antibiotic | A longer course may be considered for hearing loss
!!
Doses from the BASHH UK guideline on syphilis (Kingston et al., *Int J STD AIDS* 2024) and the NHS West of Scotland neurosyphilis guidance.

# Tips and links | For the examination and the ward.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **The features:** painless discharge, multiple perforations, pale granulation, early facial palsy, disproportionate hearing loss.
- **Diagnosis** by PCR, histology and culture.
- **Treatment is medical;** surgery for complications only.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Operating on a chronic ear without sending the tissue for tuberculosis.
- Being reassured by a negative acid-fast smear.
- Reconstructing during active treatment.
!!
