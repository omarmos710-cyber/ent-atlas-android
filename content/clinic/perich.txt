@stand A red, hot, swollen pinna that spares the lobule is a disease of the cartilage, because the lobule has none. Perichondritis needs anti-pseudomonal cover and early drainage of any pus; otherwise the cartilage dies and the ear crumples. Vesicles in the concha with facial weakness are a different emergency: Ramsay Hunt syndrome.

# Applied anatomy | Why the lobule is spared.
The cartilage of the pinna has no blood supply of its own and lives on its perichondrium. When pus or blood separates the two, the cartilage starves and necroses. The lobule has no cartilage, so a cartilage disease spares it.
!anat s30_29 | The auricular cartilage: everything except the lobule.

# Management | The first steps.
!ward On call: a red, swollen ear
1. Does it spare the lobule? Then it is cartilage.
2. Fluctuant: drain under anaesthesia and bolster.
3. Start anti-pseudomonal antibiotics.
4. Vesicles with facial weakness: steroids, antivirals and eye protection.
5. Eye symptoms: check the cornea.
!!

# Differential diagnosis | Other red or nodular pinnae.
Gout (tophi on the helix), lymphoma and skin tumours ([[t:extca]]).

# Perichondritis {dz} | Cartilage under threat.
## Etiology
Blunt trauma with an infected haematoma ([[t:earminor]]), lacerations and burns, high (cartilage) piercing, ear surgery, and spread from [[t:oe|otitis externa]]. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is the usual organism, with staphylococci.
## Clinical features
### Early
Pain and a red, hot, tender, thickened pinna, sparing the lobule.
### Late
Fluctuant swelling as pus collects between perichondrium and cartilage, then necrosis.
!fig d8-10 | Perichondritis of the pinna: the whole pinna is red and swollen.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Perichondritis: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Diagnosis | Clinical: red, hot, tender pinna sparing the lobule | 
Pus or discharge | Swab or aspirate for culture | Usually *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*; also *Staphylococcus aureus*
Fluctuance uncertain | Ultrasound | Collection needing drainage
Recurrent, bilateral, or nose and eye involved | Inflammatory markers, rheumatology review | Relapsing polychondritis
Recurrent infection | Blood glucose | Diabetes
!!
## Management
### Early, without abscess
An oral fluoroquinolone such as ciprofloxacin, or intravenous anti-pseudomonal antibiotics for severe infection; cool aluminium acetate compresses.
### Abscess
Incise along a natural fold, remove dead cartilage, irrigate, and apply a bolster dressing for several days to keep the skin against the cartilage. Antibiotics continue for two to four weeks.
!update Current evidence
**Perichondritis.** The link between *Pseudomonas* and high (cartilage) piercing is why oral ciprofloxacin is first line.
!!
### Drugs and doses
!table Perichondritis: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
First line, adults | Ciprofloxacin 750 mg by mouth every 12 hours (500 mg for milder infection) | 7 to 10 days, longer (2 to 4 weeks) after abscess drainage; MHRA quinolone warnings apply
Children | Ciprofloxacin 10 to 15 mg/kg (maximum 500 mg) every 12 hours | Accepted for this indication despite quinolone cautions
Severe, spreading or failing | Intravenous anti-pseudomonal: ciprofloxacin 400 mg every 8 to 12 hours, or piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g every 8 hours | Admit; drain any abscess
Abscess | Incision, removal of dead cartilage, bolster dressing | Send pus for culture
Every patient | Analgesia; remove the piercing | 
!!
Doses from NHS Fife hospital ENT antimicrobial guidance, the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital perichondritis guideline (children) and the BNF.
## Complications
Loss of cartilage leaves a shrivelled, deformed *cauliflower ear*.
!fig d8-7 | A cauliflower ear, the end result of cartilage necrosis after haematoma or perichondritis.

# Cellulitis and erysipelas {dz} | Skin infections.
## Cellulitis
Spreading skin infection, usually staphylococcal or streptococcal, often after a scratch, piercing or otitis externa. Unlike perichondritis, it may involve the lobule.
## Erysipelas
Streptococcal infection with a sharply defined, raised red edge, which can spread onto the face. Treated with penicillin.
## Drugs and doses
!table Cellulitis and erysipelas of the pinna: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
First line, adults | Flucloxacillin 500 mg to 1 g four times a day | 5 to 7 days
Children | Flucloxacillin: 1 month to 1 year 62.5 to 125 mg, 2 to 9 years 125 to 250 mg, 10 to 17 years 250 to 500 mg, four times a day | 5 to 7 days
Penicillin allergy | Clarithromycin 500 mg twice a day (adults); or erythromycin in pregnancy | 5 to 7 days
Severe | Flucloxacillin 1 to 2 g IV every 6 hours | Review at 48 hours
Lobule spared and cartilage involved | Treat as perichondritis with ciprofloxacin | 
!!
Doses from NICE NG141 (cellulitis and erysipelas), NHS Fife ENT guidance and the BNF.

# Herpes zoster oticus {dz} | Ramsay Hunt syndrome.
## Etiology
Reactivation of varicella-zoster virus in the geniculate ganglion.
## Clinical features
Severe, burning ear pain, followed by vesicles in the concha, canal and sometimes the palate and tongue, then facial palsy. The eighth nerve is often involved, with tinnitus, sensorineural hearing loss and vertigo.
!fig d14-9 | Ramsay Hunt syndrome: facial palsy with small vesicles in the right concha.
## Management
Prednisolone 50 to 60 mg daily (1 mg/kg up to 60 mg) for 10 days in total, as for Bell's palsy, with an antiviral such as valaciclovir for 7 days, analgesia, and eye protection. Gabapentin for post-herpetic neuralgia. Surgical decompression is not advised, because the nerve damage is diffuse.
!ops
!update Current evidence
**Ramsay Hunt syndrome.** Cochrane reviews found no randomised evidence that antivirals improve facial recovery (Uscategui et al., 2008) and insufficient evidence on steroids, so combined steroid and antiviral treatment, started early, rests on observational studies and on the analogy with zoster elsewhere.
!!
### Drugs and doses
!table Herpes zoster oticus: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Antiviral | Aciclovir 800 mg five times a day; or valaciclovir 1 g three times a day; or famciclovir 500 mg three times a day | 7 days; continue until 48 hours after crusting if immunocompromised
Steroid with facial palsy | Prednisolone as for Bell's palsy (50 to 60 mg daily; 1 mg/kg up to 60 mg) | 10 days in total
Neuralgia | Amitriptyline 10 mg at night or gabapentin, titrated | 
Eye | Lubricating drops and night ointment and tape | 
!!
Doses from NHS Fife ENT guidance and the BNF (herpes zoster).
## Prognosis
Worse than Bell's palsy: even with treatment only about 60% regain normal facial function, and without it only about 20% recover completely. Facial palsy in general is covered in [[t:facial]].

# Relapsing polychondritis {dz} | Autoimmune disease of cartilage.
## Clinical features
Recurrent painful red swelling of the pinna sparing the lobule, often both ears, with nasal (saddle nose), laryngeal and tracheal cartilage involvement that can threaten the airway.
## Management
High-dose steroids and immunosuppression; it needs rheumatological assessment because airway cartilage may also be involved.
### Drugs and doses
!table Relapsing polychondritis: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Flare | Prednisolone, commonly 0.5 to 1 mg/kg a day, then tapered | Rheumatology care
Steroid-sparing | Methotrexate (and other immunosuppressants or biologics) | Specialist choice
Airway involvement | Urgent airway assessment: flexible laryngoscopy, CT of the trachea, lung function | Can need tracheostomy or stents
!!

# Chondrodermatitis nodularis helicis {dz} | A painful nodule on the helix.
## Clinical features
A painful, tender nodule on the rim of the helix, usually in older men who sleep on that side.
## Management
Relieve pressure; excise if persistent, and send for histology to exclude skin cancer ([[t:extca]]).

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Using flucloxacillin alone for perichondritis.
- Leaving pus under the perichondrium.
- Calling Ramsay Hunt syndrome Bell's palsy because nobody looked in the ear.
!!
