@stand Wax and foreign bodies are among the commonest problems in the ear clinic, and among the easiest to make worse. Ask about a previous perforation before syringing, never chase a smooth round object deeper with forceps, kill a live insect before removing it, and treat a button battery as an emergency.

# Overview | The canal and what gets stuck in it.
## Applied anatomy
The external auditory canal is about 24 mm long. Its outer third is cartilaginous, with hairs and sebaceous and ceruminous glands; its inner two-thirds is bony, with thin, sensitive skin. The canal narrows at the *isthmus*, about 6 mm from the drum: objects pushed past it become impacted. The *anterior recess*, in front of and below the drum, collects debris.
!fig d1-2 | The anterior recess of the canal, where discharge and debris collect.
!anat s32_33 d188_10 | Skin of the cartilaginous canal, and the direction of the canal.
## Pulling the pinna
In adults, pull the pinna up and back to straighten the canal; in infants, down and back.

# Wax | A useful secretion.
## What it is
Wax (cerumen) is a mixture of secretions of the ceruminous and sebaceous glands with shed keratin. It is acidic, water-repellent and antibacterial, and the canal normally clears it by epithelial migration.
## Why it impacts
A narrow or tortuous canal, stiff hairs, exostoses, overactive glands, and pushing wax deeper with cotton buds or hearing aid moulds.
## Clinical picture
A blocked ear and hearing loss, sometimes tinnitus or giddiness, and occasionally a reflex cough through the auricular branch of the vagus (*Arnold's nerve*). Sudden blockage after bathing happens when water swells dry wax.
## Removal
### Softening
Hard wax is softened first with sodium bicarbonate glycerine drops, olive or almond oil, or hydrogen peroxide for a few days.
### Syringing (irrigation)
Pull the pinna up and back and direct a stream of water at body temperature along the posterosuperior canal wall, so that it flows behind the wax and pushes it out. Dry the ear and inspect the drum afterwards.
!fig d8-13 | (A) Irrigation of the ear canal. (B) The jet passes behind the wax or foreign body and pushes it out.
!flag Do not syringe when
- There is a perforation, or a history of discharge or ear surgery.
- The patient has had a previous perforation that may be thin.
- The foreign body is vegetable matter that will swell.
- The patient has an only hearing ear (relative).
!!
### Under the microscope
Suction or instruments (a wax hook, Jobson-Horne probe or crocodile forceps) under direct vision, when syringing is unsuitable.
!fig d8-14 | Other methods of removal: (A) suction and (B) forceps.
## Complications of removal
Perforation, canal laceration and bleeding, otitis externa, and vertigo if the water is not at body temperature.

# Foreign bodies | What children and insects leave behind.
## Types
### Non-living
Beads, stones, paper, cotton, seeds, peas and beans, pencil tips, and button batteries. Vegetable matter swells with moisture and can cause infection.
### Living
Insects (flies, cockroaches, ants), which cause intense discomfort and noise, and maggots, from flies laying eggs in a foul-discharging ear.
!fig d8-15 | An insect in the ear canal, seen with an endoscope (arrow).
## Removal
### General principles
Use good light, the microscope and the right instrument. A cooperative patient, or a general anaesthetic for children and for impacted objects or a failed first attempt.
### Smooth, round objects
Do not grasp with forceps: they slip and are pushed deeper past the isthmus. Use a hook passed beyond the object, suction, or syringing (if not vegetable).
### Insects
Kill first by filling the canal with oil or spirit, then remove by forceps or syringing.
### Maggots
Instil chloroform water or turpentine to kill them, then remove with forceps over several sittings; do not syringe. Treat the underlying ear disease.
### Button batteries
Remove urgently: they leak alkali and cause rapid tissue necrosis. Do not instil drops or water.
### Impacted deep objects
A post-aural approach may be needed for objects medial to the isthmus.
!pearl Pearls
- Most foreign bodies can wait for the right setting; a bad first attempt makes the second harder.
- Always inspect the drum and the other ear (and the nostrils in a child) afterwards.
!!

# Canal stenosis | Narrowing after injury.
## Causes
Acquired from chronic otitis externa, trauma, burns, surgery or radiation; congenital stenosis and atresia are covered separately.
!imaging | Canal stenosis and exostoses.
## Treatment
Meatoplasty with excision of the fibrous tissue and skin flaps or grafts, and canal stenting while it heals.
!update Exostoses, osteomas and foreign bodies (Oxford Textbook)
**Exostoses** are broad-based, usually multiple and bilateral bony swellings deep in the bony canal, caused by repeated exposure to cold water and wind ("surfer's ear"). **Osteomas** are single, pedunculated lesions arising at the tympanomastoid or tympanosquamous suture lines, unrelated to cold water. Both are usually symptomless but can trap wax and debris, causing recurrent otitis externa, a conductive loss and, rarely, canal cholesteatoma. Symptomatic patients are managed with regular microsuction and drops, and surgery is offered when that fails: a postauricular or endaural approach with a posterior meatal skin flap and removal of bone with a drill or chisel, protecting the canal skin, the drum and, posteriorly, the facial nerve. Cold-water ear protection reduces recurrence. In a child, an apparent bony stenosis may be a mild congenital atresia, so CT is advised before operating.

**Foreign bodies** in the canal are removed under direct vision. Graspable objects are taken with forceps; round or smooth objects are hooked from behind with an angled hook, curette or suction rather than grasped, because forceps push them towards the drum. Irrigation is not recommended in the ENT clinic, and must never be used for vegetable matter, which swells, or for button batteries, which need urgent removal because they cause liquefactive necrosis.
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## On call
!ward Something stuck in the ear
1. Button battery: remove urgently, no drops.
2. Live insect: kill it first with oil or spirit, then remove.
3. Smooth round object: hook or suction, never forceps.
4. Impacted object or failed attempt: microscope, and consider general anaesthesia.
5. Inspect the drum afterwards.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Anatomy:** cartilaginous and bony parts, the isthmus, the anterior recess.
- **Technique of syringing** and its contraindications.
- **Removal of foreign bodies,** living and non-living.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Syringing an ear with a perforation.
- Grasping a bead with forceps.
- Syringing vegetable matter.
- Delaying removal of a button battery.
!!
