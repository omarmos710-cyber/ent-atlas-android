# Summary | The canal and what gets stuck in it.
This topic covers four problems of the external canal, each in its own chapter: [[@Wax]], [[@Foreign bodies]], [[@Acquired canal stenosis]], and [[@Exostoses and osteoma]].

# Applied anatomy | The canal.
The external auditory canal is about 24 mm long. Its outer third is cartilaginous, with hairs and sebaceous and ceruminous glands; its inner two-thirds is bony, with thin, sensitive skin. The canal narrows at the *isthmus*, about 6 mm from the drum: objects pushed past it become impacted. The *anterior recess*, in front of and below the drum, collects debris.
!fig d1-2 | The anterior recess of the canal, where discharge and debris collect.
!anat s32_33 d188_10 | Skin of the cartilaginous canal, and the direction of the canal.
## Pulling the pinna
In adults, pull the pinna up and back to straighten the canal; in infants, down and back.

# Wax {dz} | A useful secretion.
## Pathophysiology
Wax (cerumen) is a mixture of secretions of the ceruminous and sebaceous glands with shed keratin. It is acidic, water-repellent and antibacterial, and the canal normally clears it by epithelial migration.
## Etiology: why it impacts
A narrow or tortuous canal, stiff hairs, exostoses, overactive glands, and pushing wax deeper with cotton buds or hearing aid moulds.
## Clinical features
A blocked ear and hearing loss, sometimes tinnitus or giddiness, and occasionally a reflex cough through the auricular branch of the vagus (*Arnold's nerve*). Sudden blockage after bathing happens when water swells dry wax.
## Management
### When to remove it
Treat wax only when it causes symptoms or hides the drum that needs to be seen; wax that does neither is left alone. Cotton buds and ear candling are not recommended.
### Softening drops
Hard wax is softened first, for a few days, with sodium bicarbonate glycerine drops, olive or almond oil, or hydrogen peroxide. Any drops, even water or saline, work better than none, with no clear winner among them.
### Irrigation (syringing)
Irrigation is the usual method in primary care and community services. Pull the pinna up and back and direct a stream of water at body temperature along the posterosuperior canal wall, so that it flows behind the wax and pushes it out. Dry the ear and inspect the drum afterwards.
!fig d8-13 | (A) Irrigation of the ear canal. (B) The jet passes behind the wax or foreign body and pushes it out.
!flag Do not irrigate when
- There is a perforation, a grommet, or a history of discharge or ear surgery.
- The patient has had a previous perforation that may be thin.
- There has been a complication from irrigation before.
- The patient has an only hearing ear.
- The canal holds vegetable matter, which swells, or a button battery.
!!
### Under the microscope
In the ENT clinic, wax is removed under direct vision with the microscope: suction, or instruments such as a wax hook, Jobson-Horne probe or crocodile forceps. This is the method whenever irrigation is unsuitable.
!fig d8-14 | Other methods of removal: (A) suction and (B) forceps.
!update Current evidence
**Earwax.** The American guideline recommends treating wax only when it causes symptoms or blocks the examination, using drops, irrigation or manual removal (including microsuction), and advises against cotton buds, ear candling and routine removal in asymptomatic patients (Schwartz et al., *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2017). NICE (NG98, 2018) advises against cotton buds and recommends microsuction or irrigation, in primary care or community services, when wax causes hearing loss or blocks the view. A Cochrane review found that any drops, including water or saline, are better than none, with no clear winner among them (Aaron et al., 2018).
!!
## Complications of removal
Perforation, canal laceration and bleeding, otitis externa, and vertigo if the water is not at body temperature.

# Foreign bodies {dz} | What children and insects leave behind.
## Types
### Non-living
Beads, stones, paper, cotton, seeds, peas and beans, pencil tips, and button batteries. Vegetable matter swells with moisture and can cause infection.
### Living
Insects (flies, cockroaches, ants), which cause intense discomfort and noise, and maggots, from flies laying eggs in a foul-discharging ear.
!fig d8-15 | An insect in the ear canal, seen with an endoscope (arrow).
## Management
### Approach
!ward On call: something stuck in the ear
1. Button battery: remove urgently, no drops.
2. Live insect: kill it first with oil or spirit, then remove.
3. Smooth round object: hook or suction, never forceps.
4. Impacted object or failed attempt: microscope, and consider general anaesthesia.
5. Inspect the drum afterwards.
!!
### General principles
Remove under direct vision, with good light, the microscope and the right instrument. A cooperative patient is essential; use a general anaesthetic for children, for impacted objects, and after a failed first attempt. Most foreign bodies can wait for the right setting, because a bad first attempt makes the second harder.
### Graspable objects
Paper, cotton and other soft or irregular objects are taken with crocodile forceps.
### Smooth, round objects
Never grasp a bead, pea or stone with forceps: it slips and is pushed past the isthmus towards the drum. Pass an angled hook or curette beyond the object and roll it out from behind, or use suction.
!differ Irrigation for a foreign body
Dhingra: Syringing is an option for a smooth object that is not vegetable matter.
Oxford texts: Irrigation is not recommended in the ENT clinic; hook the object out from behind instead.
In practice: Use the microscope and a hook or suction in the ENT clinic. Never irrigate vegetable matter or a button battery, or an ear with a perforation.
!!
### Insects
Kill the insect first by filling the canal with oil or spirit, then remove it with forceps or suction.
### Maggots
Instil chloroform water or turpentine to kill them, then remove them with forceps over several sittings; do not irrigate. Treat the underlying ear disease.
### Button batteries
Remove urgently: they leak alkali and cause rapid liquefactive necrosis. Do not instil drops or water.
### Impacted deep objects
An object pushed medial to the isthmus may need a postaural approach.
!pearl After any removal
Inspect the drum, then the other ear, and in a child the nostrils as well.
!!

# Acquired canal stenosis {dz} | Fibrous narrowing.
## Etiology
Fibrous narrowing after chronic otitis externa, trauma, burns, surgery or radiation; congenital stenosis and atresia are covered in [[t:atresia]].
!imaging | Canal stenosis and exostoses.
## Management
Meatoplasty, excising the fibrous tissue and resurfacing with skin flaps or grafts, with a stent while the canal heals.

# Exostoses and osteoma {dz} | Bony growths of the canal.
## Differential diagnosis
Both are bony swellings of the bony canal, and they are told apart by number, shape, site and cause.
!table Exostoses or osteoma?
 | Exostoses | Osteoma
Number and sides | Multiple, usually both ears | Single, one ear
Shape | Broad-based (sessile) | Pedunculated
Site | Deep in the bony canal, near the drum, from the anterior and posterior walls | Outer bony canal, near the bony-cartilaginous junction, at a suture line (tympanosquamous or tympanomastoid)
Cause | Repeated cold water and wind ("surfer's ear") | A true benign tumour, unrelated to cold water
!!
## Clinical features
Both are usually symptomless, but they can trap wax and debris, causing recurrent otitis externa, a conductive loss and, rarely, canal cholesteatoma.
## Diagnosis
In a child, an apparent bony stenosis may be a mild congenital atresia, so obtain a CT before operating.
## Management
Regular microsuction and drops first. Surgery when that fails: a postauricular or endaural approach, a posterior meatal skin flap, and removal of bone with a drill, protecting the canal skin, the drum and, posteriorly, the facial nerve. An osteoma can often be removed by fracturing its pedicle. Cold-water ear protection reduces the recurrence of exostoses, which return in surfers who keep swimming without it. Tumours of the canal skin and glands are covered in [[t:extca]].
