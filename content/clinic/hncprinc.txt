@stand Head and neck squamous carcinoma is several diseases that share a microscope slide. One is tobacco- and alcohol-driven cancer of the mouth, larynx and hypopharynx in older smokers. Another is HPV-driven cancer of the tonsil and tongue base in younger non-smokers, which carries a far better prognosis. A third is EBV-driven nasopharyngeal carcinoma. The principles here apply across sites: biology, staging by the eighth edition of the AJCC system, the work-up, the multidisciplinary decision, and life after treatment.

# Who and why | Epidemiology and causes.
## Burden
- **Global picture:** head and neck cancer is among the commonest cancers worldwide, about the seventh or eighth commonest. In India it is among the leading cancers in men, mostly *oral cavity* cancer linked to smokeless tobacco and betel quid.
- **Pathology:** over 90% of tumours of the mucosa of the upper aerodigestive tract are squamous cell carcinomas.
- **Late presentation:** in many low-resource settings more than 70% present at stage III or IV.
## Risk factors
- **Tobacco:**
  - Smoked: cigarettes, bidis, hookah.
  - Smokeless: chewing tobacco, gutka, *betel quid with areca nut*, snuff, and *reverse smoking* (palate cancer).
- **Alcohol:** acts *synergistically* with tobacco. The combined risk is multiplicative.
- **Human papillomavirus (HPV-16):** oropharynx, especially the tonsil and tongue base. Linked to oral sex and the number of partners. Incidence is rising in high-income countries.
- **Epstein–Barr virus:** undifferentiated nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Salted fish and preserved foods, and genetic susceptibility in southern China.
- **Other factors:**
  - Poor oral hygiene and chronic dental trauma.
  - Submucous fibrosis (areca nut).
  - Plummer–Vinson syndrome (postcricoid cancer).
  - Immunosuppression.
  - Radiation.
  - Occupational exposures: wood dust, leather and nickel (sinonasal adenocarcinoma and squamous carcinoma).
  - Fanconi anaemia and dyskeratosis congenita.
  - Diets poor in fruit and vegetables.
## Field cancerisation and second primaries
Carcinogens bathe the whole mucosa. Genetically altered fields surround a cancer and give rise to new ones.
- **Second primary tumours:** about 3 to 5% a year, in the head and neck, lung and oesophagus. Second primaries are a leading cause of death after successful treatment of an early cancer.
- **Consequences:**
  - Panendoscopy at diagnosis.
  - Stopping smoking.
  - Lifelong surveillance.
## Premalignant lesions
- Leukoplakia and erythroplakia. Erythroplakia carries the highest risk.
- Submucous fibrosis.
- Lichen planus (erosive).
- Dysplasia of the larynx.

Graded dysplasia (mild, moderate, severe, carcinoma in situ) predicts the risk of invasion.

# Tumour biology | Two diseases.
## HPV-negative carcinoma
Driven by tobacco carcinogens, which cause accumulated mutations.
- **Tumour suppressors inactivated:**
  - *TP53*: mutated in most (about 80%).
  - *CDKN2A* (p16): lost by mutation, deletion of 9p21 or methylation.
  - *FAT1*, *NOTCH1*, *CASP8*.
- **Oncogenes activated:**
  - *Cyclin D1* (11q13 amplification).
  - *PIK3CA* (3q26).
  - *EGFR* (overexpressed in about 90%, amplified in some).
  - *FGFR1*, *MYC*, *HRAS*.
- **Progression model (Califano):** loss of 9p21 (p16), then 3p, then 17p (p53), then further changes, as normal mucosa progresses through dysplasia to invasive cancer.
## HPV-positive carcinoma
- **Mechanism:**
  - The viral oncoprotein *E6* degrades *p53*.
  - *E7* inactivates *Rb*.
  - With Rb gone, *p16 is strongly over-expressed*. This is why *p16 immunohistochemistry* is the surrogate marker for HPV in oropharyngeal cancer.
- **Genetics:** p53 is usually *wild-type*, mutational burden is lower, and *PIK3CA* activation is frequent. *TRAF3* and *CYLD* changes are linked to immune escape.
- **Clinical features:**
  - Younger patients, often non-smokers.
  - Small primaries in the tonsil or tongue base.
  - Early, often *cystic* nodes.
  - Much better response to radiotherapy and chemotherapy.
  - About 80% three-year survival versus 55% for HPV-negative tumours (RTOG 0129).
  - Smoking over 10 pack-years worsens the outlook.
## Prognostic factors
- **Tumour factors:**
  - Stage, especially nodes and *extranodal extension*.
  - *Depth of invasion* in oral cancer.
  - Perineural and lymphovascular invasion.
  - Margins.
  - HPV status.
- **Patient factors:** comorbidity, performance status, continued smoking.
- **Treatment factors:** total treatment time and gaps.

# Staging | AJCC eighth edition.
## Principles
- **T:** describes the primary, mostly by size. Exceptions:
  - *Vocal fold mobility* and subsite spread in the larynx.
  - *Depth of invasion* in the oral cavity.
  - Extension in the nasopharynx and sinuses.
- **N:** regional nodes, by size, number and side, and now *extranodal extension* (ENE).
- **M:** distant metastases.
- **Clinical versus pathological:** clinical (cTNM) staging comes from examination and imaging. Pathological (pTNM) staging comes from the resected specimen.
- **Subdivisions of T4:**
  - *T4a* (moderately advanced): resectable.
  - *T4b* (very advanced): usually unresectable. Examples: masticator space, pterygoid plates, skull base, prevertebral fascia, mediastinum, or carotid encasement.
!table T stage: oral cavity (including lip)
T | Definition
T1 | 2 cm or less, depth of invasion (DOI) 5 mm or less
T2 | 2 cm or less with DOI over 5 mm, or over 2 to 4 cm with DOI 10 mm or less
T3 | Over 4 cm, or DOI over 10 mm
T4a | Invades through cortical bone of mandible or maxilla, the maxillary sinus or facial skin (lip: through bone, inferior alveolar nerve, floor of mouth or skin)
T4b | Masticator space, pterygoid plates, skull base, or encases the internal carotid
!!
!table T stage: larynx (glottis)
T | Definition
T1a / T1b | One vocal fold / both vocal folds, normal mobility
T2 | Extends to supraglottis or subglottis, or impaired mobility
T3 | Fixed vocal fold, paraglottic space or inner cortex of thyroid cartilage
T4a | Through the outer cortex of the thyroid cartilage or beyond the larynx (trachea, strap muscles, thyroid, oesophagus, deep tongue muscles)
T4b | Prevertebral space, encases carotid, or mediastinum
!!
- **Supraglottis:**
  - T1: one subsite.
  - T2: more than one subsite, or the glottis or adjacent mucosa.
  - T3: fixed fold, postcricoid area, pre-epiglottic or paraglottic space, or minor cartilage erosion.
  - T4 as for the glottis.
- **Hypopharynx:**
  - T1: 2 cm or less and one subsite.
  - T2: over 2 to 4 cm, or more than one subsite.
  - T3: over 4 cm, fixed hemilarynx, or oesophageal mucosa.
  - T4a: cartilage, hyoid, thyroid, oesophageal muscle or central compartment.
- **Oropharynx (both HPV types):** T1 is 2 cm or less, T2 is 2 to 4 cm, T3 is over 4 cm or reaches the lingual surface of the epiglottis. HPV-positive tumours have no T4b.
- **Nasopharynx:**
  - T1: confined to the nasopharynx, or extending to the oropharynx or nasal cavity.
  - T2: parapharyngeal space or adjacent muscles.
  - T3: bone of the skull base, cervical vertebrae, pterygoid structures or sinuses.
  - T4: intracranial, cranial nerves, hypopharynx, orbit, parotid or soft tissue beyond the lateral pterygoid.
## Nodal staging
!table N stage: clinical (oral cavity, larynx, hypopharynx, HPV-negative oropharynx)
N | Definition
N1 | Single ipsilateral node 3 cm or less, ENE negative
N2a | Single ipsilateral node over 3 to 6 cm, ENE negative
N2b | Multiple ipsilateral nodes, none over 6 cm, ENE negative
N2c | Bilateral or contralateral nodes, none over 6 cm, ENE negative
N3a | Any node over 6 cm, ENE negative
N3b | Clinically overt ENE (skin involvement, fixation, cranial nerve or muscle invasion)
!!
- **Pathological N stage:**
  - A single ipsilateral node 3 cm or less *with* ENE becomes pN2a.
  - Larger nodes or multiple nodes with ENE become pN3b.
- **HPV-positive oropharynx:**
  - Clinical: N1 is ipsilateral nodes 6 cm or less. N2 is contralateral or bilateral 6 cm or less. N3 is over 6 cm.
  - Pathological: *pN1 is 1 to 4 nodes, pN2 is 5 or more*.
- **Nasopharynx:**
  - N1: unilateral cervical, or unilateral or bilateral retropharyngeal nodes, 6 cm or less, above the cricoid.
  - N2: bilateral cervical, 6 cm or less, above the cricoid.
  - N3: over 6 cm or below the caudal border of the cricoid.
## Stage groups
!table Stage groups (oral cavity, larynx, hypopharynx, HPV-negative oropharynx, sinuses, salivary)
Stage | TNM
I | T1 N0 M0
II | T2 N0 M0
III | T3 N0, or T1 to T3 N1, M0
IVA | T4a N0 to N1, or T1 to T4a N2, M0
IVB | Any T N3, or T4b any N, M0
IVC | Any M1
!!
- **HPV-positive oropharynx (clinical stage groups):**
  - Stage I: T0 to T2 with N0 to N1.
  - Stage II: T0 to T2 with N2, or T3 with N0 to N2.
  - Stage III: T4 or N3.
  - Stage IV: M1.
  - Many patients are downstaged compared with the seventh edition. This reflects their better prognosis.
!update The eighth edition changes
The 2017 AJCC eighth edition introduced:
- *Depth of invasion* for oral cancer.
- *Extranodal extension* in nodal staging.
- A separate staging system for *p16-positive oropharyngeal cancer*.
- Separate staging for unknown primaries by EBV and HPV status.

Older texts and exam questions based on the seventh edition will give different stages for the same tumour.
!!

# Work-up | Before the decision.
## Assessment
- **History:** symptoms, risk factors, comorbidity, performance status (ECOG or Karnofsky), nutrition, social support.
- **Examination:**
  - The full head and neck, with palpation of the tongue base and floor of mouth.
  - Flexible nasendoscopy.
  - Cranial nerves.
  - The neck, by level.
- **Tissue diagnosis:**
  - Biopsy of the primary.
  - FNA of nodes.
  - p16/HPV for the oropharynx and unknown primaries. EBV for the nasopharynx.
## Imaging
- **CT and/or MRI** of the primary and neck. MRI is better for the tongue, nasopharynx, skull base and perineural spread. CT is better for bone and the larynx.
- **Distant staging:**
  - CT chest for advanced disease.
  - *PET-CT* for stage III and IV disease, unknown primaries and recurrence.
- **Ultrasound with FNA** for the neck.
## Panendoscopy
Examination under anaesthesia with laryngoscopy, pharyngoscopy, oesophagoscopy and sometimes bronchoscopy.
- Maps the extent of the tumour: tongue base, vallecula, postcricoid area, subglottis.
- Takes biopsies.
- Looks for synchronous second primaries.
- Tonsillectomy and tongue base mucosectomy for unknown primaries.
## Before treatment
- **Dental assessment:** extract teeth before radiotherapy.
- **Nutrition:** dietitian review, and consider a prophylactic gastrostomy.
- **Speech and swallowing therapy:** baseline assessment and *prehabilitation*.
- **Smoking and alcohol cessation.**
- **Anaesthetic and airway planning:** some patients need a tracheostomy under local anaesthesia before treatment.
- **Psychosocial support and the clinical nurse specialist.**

# Treatment | The multidisciplinary decision.
## The team
Every patient is discussed at a *multidisciplinary team (tumour board)*:
- Head and neck and reconstructive surgeons.
- Clinical and medical oncologists.
- Radiologist and pathologist.
- Restorative dentist.
- Speech and language therapist and dietitian.
- Specialist nurse.
- Palliative care.

The patient's own priorities are central: cure, voice, swallowing, appearance and time.
## General principles
- **Early disease (stage I to II): a single modality.** Surgery *or* radiotherapy, with similar cure rates. The choice is driven by function, access, morbidity, the patient's wishes, and keeping options for second primaries. For example:
  - Transoral laser surgery or radiotherapy for T1 glottic cancer.
  - Surgery for a small oral tongue cancer.
- **Advanced disease (stage III to IV): combined modality.**
  - *Surgery followed by radiotherapy*, or chemoradiotherapy for positive margins or extranodal extension. This is preferred for oral cavity cancer and for cancers destroying cartilage or bone (T4a larynx, mandible).
  - Or *primary concurrent chemoradiotherapy* with surgical salvage. This is the organ-preservation approach for the larynx, hypopharynx and oropharynx, and the standard for the nasopharynx.
- **Unresectable (T4b) disease:** chemoradiotherapy, or palliation.
- **Recurrent or metastatic disease:**
  - Salvage surgery or re-irradiation for local and regional recurrence.
  - Systemic therapy (pembrolizumab with or without platinum and 5-FU; cetuximab-based regimens).
  - Palliative care.
## The neck
- **Clinically N0 neck:** treat electively (selective neck dissection or elective radiotherapy) when the risk of occult nodes is above about 15 to 20%.
  - In the oral cavity this means depth of invasion over 3 to 4 mm. The D'Cruz trial showed a survival advantage for elective neck dissection in early oral cancer.
  - Sentinel node biopsy is an alternative in early oral cancer.
- **Clinically positive neck:** therapeutic neck dissection (modified radical or comprehensive), or chemoradiotherapy with PET-CT at 12 weeks deciding on dissection.
- **Midline and bilateral drainage:** tumours of the tongue base, soft palate, supraglottis and the floor of mouth crossing the midline drain to *both* sides. Both necks are treated.
## Surgery
- **Aim:** complete resection with clear margins, generally *5 mm or more* on histology, and close margins under 5 mm. Frozen sections help.
- **Approaches:**
  - Transoral: laser microsurgery, transoral robotic surgery.
  - Mandibulotomy (lip-split).
  - Lateral pharyngotomy.
  - Total laryngectomy and pharyngolaryngectomy.
- **Reconstruction:** usually at the same operation, often with free flaps.
- **Airway:** a tracheostomy protects the airway after major oral and pharyngeal resections.

# After treatment | Survival and survivorship.
## Follow-up
- **Visits:** every 1 to 3 months in year 1, every 2 to 4 months in year 2, every 3 to 6 months in years 3 to 5, then yearly. Most recurrences occur in the first *two years*.
- **What to check:**
  - Examination and nasendoscopy.
  - A PET-CT at about 12 weeks after radiotherapy.
  - Further imaging as indicated.
  - *TSH* every 6 to 12 months after neck radiotherapy.
  - Dental review.
  - Smoking status.
  - Screening for second primaries: chest symptoms and examination of the whole upper aerodigestive tract.
## Rehabilitation and quality of life
- **Swallowing:** exercises, dilatation of strictures, and a gastrostomy for those who cannot eat safely.
- **Voice after laryngectomy:** the *tracheo-oesophageal valve*, the electrolarynx, or oesophageal speech.
- **Other late effects:** shoulder dysfunction after accessory nerve injury (physiotherapy), lymphoedema (manual drainage), trismus, xerostomia, hearing loss, osteoradionecrosis.
- **Wellbeing:** depression, fear of recurrence, alcohol relapse and suicide risk are high in this group. Screen for them and refer.
- **Palliative care:** symptom control, managing a fungating tumour, and planning for *carotid blow-out*, airway obstruction and bleeding. Have a crisis plan with dark towels and sedation.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Risk factors:** tobacco (including smokeless) and alcohol synergy; HPV; EBV.
- **Field cancerisation** and the second primary rate.
- **HPV-positive versus HPV-negative cancer:**
  - E6 degrades p53, E7 inactivates Rb, so p16 is over-expressed.
  - Prognosis.
  - A separate AJCC staging system.
- **The eighth edition changes:** depth of invasion, ENE, p16 staging.
- **Oral cavity and glottic T staging;** the clinical N stages; the stage groups.
- **The work-up:** imaging, PET-CT, panendoscopy, the dental and nutrition review.
- **Early versus advanced disease:** single versus combined modality; when to treat the N0 neck; margins.
- **Follow-up schedule** and late effects.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Staging an oropharyngeal cancer without p16.
- Using seventh-edition definitions in an eighth-edition world.
- Forgetting the contralateral neck in midline tumours.
- Neglecting dental assessment and smoking cessation before treatment.
- Discharging patients without thyroid and second primary surveillance.
!!
