@stand Most slowly progressive sensorineural loss in both ears comes from age, noise or drugs. Ask about each specifically, stop whatever is still doing harm, and do not miss the rarer autoimmune inner ear disease, because it can respond to steroids.

# Summary | Four causes of acquired sensorineural loss.
Much of this hearing loss is preventable, by monitoring drugs and protecting ears from noise, and some is reversible. Each cause has its own chapter:
- [[@Ototoxicity]]: hearing or balance damaged by drugs.
- [[@Noise-induced hearing loss]]: from sustained or sudden loud sound.
- [[@Presbycusis]]: age-related hearing loss.
- [[@Immune-mediated inner ear disease]]: primary or as part of systemic disease.

# Differential diagnosis | Others to exclude.
- [[t:vs|Vestibular schwannoma]].
- [[t:meniere|Ménière's disease]].
- Syphilis ([[t:fistula]]).
- Multiple sclerosis.
- Familial progressive sensorineural loss ([[t:deaf]]).

# Ototoxicity {dz} | Drugs that damage the ear.
## Etiology: the drugs
!table Ototoxic drugs
Drug | Main target | Reversible?
Streptomycin, gentamicin | Vestibular | Often permanent
Neomycin, kanamycin, amikacin | Cochlea | Often permanent
Loop diuretics | Stria vascularis | Usually
Salicylates | Cochlea | Yes
Quinine, chloroquine | Cochlear vessels | Usually; high doses may not be
Cisplatin, carboplatin | Outer hair cells | Often permanent
!!
Others include deferoxamine, some ear drops (aminoglycosides and polymyxin) through a perforation, and some topical antiseptics.
!differ Tobramycin
Dhingra: Mainly vestibulotoxic.
Lee: Mainly cochleotoxic.
In practice: Treat tobramycin as able to damage both, and monitor hearing and balance.
!!
!anat s78_13 | The organ of Corti: outer hair cells are the first casualties of noise and ototoxic drugs.
## Aminoglycosides
Hair cells are damaged from the basal turn upwards, so high frequencies go first. Hearing loss may appear during or after treatment. Risk is higher with renal impairment, age over 65, high blood levels, long courses, previous exposure, loop diuretics, and genetic susceptibility: mitochondrial 12S rRNA mutations make even a single dose dangerous, so ask about family members who went deaf after these drugs.
## Cisplatin
Bilateral, permanent high-frequency loss, worse in young children under five, the elderly, and with noise or radiotherapy.
## Prevention and monitoring
Avoid ototoxic drugs where alternatives exist, monitor levels and renal function, and obtain baseline and serial audiograms, including high-frequency audiometry. **Sodium thiosulfate**, given six hours after each cisplatin dose, roughly halves hearing loss in children with localised tumours, and is now licensed for this. Rapid genetic testing for the *MT-RNR1* m.1555A>G variant before aminoglycosides is being introduced in neonatal units, so that carriers receive another antibiotic.
!flag On the ward
New tinnitus, hearing loss or imbalance on an aminoglycoside or cisplatin: alert the treating team, check levels and renal function, and test hearing.
!!
!update Current evidence
**Cisplatin.** In the SIOPEL 6 trial in children with standard-risk hepatoblastoma, sodium thiosulfate after cisplatin cut hearing loss from 63% to 33% without reducing survival (Brock et al., *NEJM* 2018); the American ACCL0431 trial found a similar reduction (Freyer et al., *Lancet Oncol* 2017). **Aminoglycosides.** The m.1555A>G variant, carried by about 1 in 500 people, can cause profound deafness after a single dose; a point-of-care test in neonatal intensive care identified carriers within about 26 minutes without delaying antibiotics (McDermott et al., *JAMA Pediatr* 2022), and NICE has recommended it (2023).
!!
## Deliberate use
Gentamicin's vestibular toxicity is used therapeutically in [[t:meniere|Ménière's disease]].

# Noise-induced hearing loss {dz} | Loud sound over time.
## Epidemiology
Recreational noise, from personal music players, concerts and shooting, is an increasingly important cause in young people.
## Pathophysiology
Noise first stiffens and then kills the **outer hair cells** of the basal turn, around the region that responds to 4 kHz; 3, 4 and 6 kHz are affected first.
### Temporary threshold shift
Hearing dulls after loud exposure and recovers.
!differ Recovery from a temporary shift
Dhingra: Up to two weeks.
Lee: Within 24 hours.
In practice: Most temporary shifts recover within a day, but allow up to two weeks before calling a loss permanent.
!!
### Permanent threshold shift
Repeated exposure turns the temporary shift into irreversible loss.
## Clinical features
High-pitched tinnitus and difficulty hearing in noise.
## Diagnosis
The audiogram shows a symmetrical *notch at 4 kHz*, which deepens and widens into the speech frequencies with continued exposure.
!fig d5-7 | Early noise-induced hearing loss, with a dip at 4000 Hz.
### Medicolegal diagnosis
The **Coles, Lutman and Buffin (CLB) guidelines** require high-frequency loss beyond that expected for age, a cumulative noise immission level of at least 100 dB (roughly 85 dB(A) daily for many years), and a notch or bulge at 3 to 6 kHz at least 10 dB deeper than the thresholds at 1 or 2 kHz and at 6 or 8 kHz.
## Subtypes and variants
### Acute acoustic trauma
A single blast or impulse (around 140 dB or more) causes immediate loss without a preceding temporary shift, often with tinnitus. It can rupture the drum, disrupt the ossicles and damage the cochlea at once. Treat the inner ear loss like sudden sensorineural loss, with early steroids ([[t:ssnhl]]).
### Hidden hearing loss
Animal work showed that noise can destroy the synapses between inner hair cells and the cochlear nerve while thresholds recover (**cochlear synaptopathy**), which may explain difficulty hearing in noise with a normal audiogram; in humans the effect appears variable and is still debated.
## Prevention: safe limits
Limits differ by country, so check local regulations.
!differ Exposure limits and the exchange rate
Dhingra: Occupational limit of 90 dBA for 8 hours; no continuous exposure over 115 dBA; no impulse over 140 dBA. The *5 dB rule*: each 5 dB rise halves the safe duration. Hearing conservation (protection and audiometry) starts at 85 dBA.
Oxford: The UK Control of Noise at Work Regulations 2005 set a lower action value of 80 dB(A), when protection must be offered and information given, and an upper action value of 85 dB(A), when protection is compulsory and hearing surveillance required, with an absolute limit of 87 dB(A) at the ear allowing for protection. European and UK practice uses a 3 dB exchange rate (each 3 dB doubles the energy and halves the safe time); the 5 dB rule is the older American occupational convention.
In practice: In the UK, offer protection from 80 dB(A) and make it compulsory, with hearing surveillance, from 85 dB(A). Know the 90 dBA limit and the 5 dB rule for the examination.
!!
## Management
Remove from exposure, reduce noise at source, and use hearing protection: earplugs give about 15 to 30 dB, earmuffs 30 to 40 dB, and both together more. Protectors work better at high than low frequencies. Pre-employment and annual audiograms. Hearing aids for established loss.
!update Current evidence
**Noise.** Kujawa and Liberman showed in mice that noise causing only a temporary threshold shift destroys cochlear synapses permanently (*J Neurosci* 2009). The World Health Organization estimates that more than a billion young people are at risk from unsafe listening, and its global standard for safe listening venues (2022) sets an average limit of 100 dB(A) over 15 minutes.
!!

# Presbycusis {dz} | Hearing and age.
## Clinical features
Gradual, bilateral, symmetrical high-frequency loss. The patient hears speech but cannot understand it, especially in noise, and is intolerant of loud sound because of recruitment.
## Subtypes and variants (Schuknecht)
!table Types of presbycusis
Type | Damage | Audiogram | Discrimination
Sensory | Hair cells, basal turn | Steep high-tone loss | Good
Neural | Cochlear neurones | Variable | Poor, out of proportion
Strial (metabolic) | Stria vascularis | Flat | Good
Cochlear conductive | Stiff basilar membrane | Gently sloping | Moderate
!!
## Management
Hearing aids, assistive listening devices, speech reading and communication tactics; cochlear implants for severe loss ([[t:rehab]]).
!ops
## Complications
Untreated hearing loss is linked with isolation and cognitive decline.

# Immune-mediated inner ear disease {dz} | The treatable one.
## Epidemiology
Usually in middle age (40 to 50). About 15% have another autoimmune disorder.
## Clinical features
Rapidly progressive, bilateral, often asymmetric sensorineural loss over weeks to months, with vestibular symptoms in about half.
## Diagnosis
Bilateral loss of 30 dB or more with progression on serial audiograms within three months. Blood tests: ESR, rheumatoid factor, ANA, complement; anti-Hsp70 antibodies correlate with activity but are not specific. Contrast MRI to exclude other causes. A response to steroids supports the diagnosis.
## Subtypes and variants
### Primary autoimmune inner ear disease
Confined to the ear, as above.
### Secondary
Part of systemic disease: *Cogan syndrome* (interstitial keratitis with audiovestibular loss), granulomatosis with polyangiitis ([[t:gpa]]), systemic lupus, antiphospholipid syndrome, and others.
## Management
Prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day, up to 60 mg, for four weeks. Responders (an improvement of 15 dB at one frequency or 10 dB at consecutive frequencies, or better discrimination) continue to a plateau and taper to the lowest maintenance dose over months. For steroid intolerance or dependence: methotrexate, cyclophosphamide (more toxic), biological agents or intratympanic steroids; note that in a randomised trial methotrexate was no better than placebo at maintaining the hearing gained with steroids (Harris et al., *JAMA* 2003), so steroid-sparing drugs are given on specialist advice only. Cochlear implantation if hearing is lost.
