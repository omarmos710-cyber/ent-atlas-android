@stand Nasopharyngeal carcinoma hides in the one part of the throat no one sees without an endoscope. It shows itself instead through its neighbours: a lump high in the neck, a blocked ear with fluid in an adult, a bloodstained nose, a squint from a sixth nerve palsy. It is linked to Epstein–Barr virus, common in southern China and South-East Asia, and treated not with the knife but with radiation and chemotherapy, to which it responds well.

# Summary | A cancer of geography and virus.
Nasopharyngeal carcinoma is the commonest malignancy of the nasopharynx. It is linked to Epstein–Barr virus, genetics and salted fish, and is commonest in southern China and South-East Asia. It presents through its neighbours: a neck node, a one-sided effusion in an adult, a bloodstained nose or a sixth nerve palsy. Endoscopy and biopsy make the diagnosis, MRI stages it, and radiotherapy, with concurrent cisplatin for non-metastatic disease beyond T1 N0 (eighth-edition stage II to IVA), is the treatment. Angiofibroma is covered in [[t:juv]].

# Epidemiology | Geography, sex and age.
The highest incidence in the world is in southern China, Hong Kong and Taiwan, with high rates in South-East Asia, North Africa, the Mediterranean and among the Inuit and Aleut peoples. It is uncommon in India except in the North-East. Chinese born in North America have a lower risk than those born in China but still higher than other Americans, showing the interplay of genes and environment. Males outnumber females about three to one; the peak is in the fifth and sixth decades, but about one in five patients is under thirty, and these younger patients usually have undifferentiated tumours.

# Etiology | Genes, virus and diet.
- **Genetic:** HLA haplotypes confer susceptibility; the risk persists after migration.
- **Epstein–Barr virus:** EBV DNA and EBV-encoded RNA are found in virtually every tumour cell of the non-keratinising types; clonal EBV in premalignant lesions suggests infection is an early, possibly initiating, event.
- **Environmental:** nitrosamines in salted, preserved fish eaten from childhood, with a diet low in vitamin C; smoke from burning incense and wood; tobacco, opium and occupational fumes.
- **Tobacco and alcohol:** linked to the keratinising (sporadic) type.

# Pathophysiology | Pathology and spread.
## WHO classification
!table WHO types of nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Type | Description | North American frequency | EBV and radiosensitivity
I | Keratinising squamous cell carcinoma | About 25% | Weaker association; least radiosensitive
II | Non-keratinising, differentiated | About 12% | Strong association
III | Non-keratinising, undifferentiated (lymphoepithelioma; Regaud and Schmincke types) | About 63% | Strongest association; most radiosensitive
!!
The current WHO scheme groups types II and III as non-keratinising carcinoma (differentiated and undifferentiated). Under the electron microscope all are squamous; immunohistochemistry for epithelial (cytokeratin) and lymphoid markers separates undifferentiated carcinoma from lymphoma. In endemic areas the non-keratinising types predominate even more; non-keratinising, EBV-associated carcinoma is the commonest type everywhere, and EBV is shown in tissue by EBER in-situ hybridisation.
## Gross forms
Proliferative (a polypoid mass causing nasal obstruction), ulcerative (bleeding), and infiltrative (submucosal, easily missed).
## Local
The commonest site of origin is the *fossa of Rosenmüller* in the lateral wall.
- **Forwards:** into the choanae and nasal cavity.
- **Laterally:** through the sinus of Morgagni into the parapharyngeal space and infratemporal fossa; into the pterygoid muscles (trismus).
- **Upwards:** through the foramen lacerum and foramen ovale into the middle cranial fossa and cavernous sinus (cranial nerves III, IV, V and VI); the *sixth nerve* is usually the first affected; into the sphenoid sinus and orbit (proptosis, optic nerve).
- **Backwards:** to the jugular foramen (IX, X, XI) and hypoglossal canal (XII), and the cervical sympathetic chain (Horner syndrome), directly or through enlarged retropharyngeal nodes.
- **Downwards:** to the oropharynx.

## Lymphatic
The nasopharynx is rich in lymphatics and nodal spread is early and often bilateral: to the lateral retropharyngeal node (of Rouvière), the upper jugular (level II) nodes high under the mastoid, and the spinal accessory chain in the posterior triangle (level V). Retropharyngeal nodes cause neck stiffness and press on the lower cranial nerves.
## Distant
Bone, lung and liver. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma has the highest rate of distant metastasis of any head and neck cancer: present in about 4% at diagnosis, and developing later, over follow-up, in about a quarter of patients treated with IMRT (a quarter to a third in older, pre-IMRT series).

# Clinical features | The four groups of symptoms.
## Cervical nodes
The commonest presentation (in 60 to 90%): a painless lump between the angle of the jaw and the mastoid, or in the posterior triangle. Nodes are present in about three-quarters at diagnosis, half of them bilateral; often the node is the only complaint.
## Ear
Eustachian tube obstruction causes conductive hearing loss, aural fullness and *otitis media with effusion*; tinnitus; rarely tumour grows up the tube.
!flag Unilateral effusion in an adult
An adult with fluid behind one drum and no obvious cause has a nasopharyngeal examination, and a biopsy if anything is seen. Inserting a grommet without looking at the nasopharynx misses cancers.
!!
## Nose
Nasal obstruction, bloodstained discharge or epistaxis, and hyponasal speech.
## Eye and nerves
Diplopia and squint from a sixth nerve palsy; ophthalmoplegia (III, IV, VI); facial pain and numbness with loss of the corneal reflex (V); proptosis and visual loss; the jugular foramen syndrome (IX, X, XI); deviation of the tongue (XII); Horner syndrome; headache from skull base erosion.
!pearl Trotter's triad
Conductive deafness from tubal blockage, ipsilateral temporoparietal neuralgia from the fifth nerve, and immobility of the soft palate on the same side from the vagus: the classic signs of a tumour invading the lateral nasopharynx and parapharyngeal space.
!!
## Others
Trismus, neck pain from retropharyngeal nodes, weight loss, and the effects of distant metastases.
!table Presenting features in order of frequency
Feature | Notes
Neck mass | Most common; often the only symptom
Hearing loss and aural fullness | Effusion from tubal obstruction
Nasal obstruction and epistaxis | Bloodstained post-nasal discharge
Cranial nerve palsy | Sixth nerve most often
Headache, earache, neck pain | Skull base and retropharyngeal involvement
!!

# Diagnosis | See it, sample it, map it.
## Endoscopy and biopsy
Rigid or flexible nasendoscopy under local anaesthesia shows a proliferative, ulcerative or submucosal lesion, and allows biopsy. If nodes suggest the diagnosis but nothing is seen, take biopsies from the fossa of Rosenmüller and posterior wall under general anaesthesia, sometimes through a transpalatal exposure. Fine-needle aspiration of a neck node, with EBV testing of the cells, points to a nasopharyngeal primary.
## Imaging
- **MRI:** the best study for soft tissue extent, the parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal spaces, perineural spread, skull base marrow, intracranial extension and nodes.
- **CT:** bone erosion of the skull base, clivus and pterygoid plates.
- **PET-CT:** distant metastases and detection of residual or recurrent disease after treatment; chest and abdominal imaging if PET is unavailable.
## Other tests
A baseline *audiogram* (radiation and cisplatin both damage hearing), dental assessment before radiotherapy, and *EBV serology*: IgA antibodies to the early antigen are highly specific and those to the viral capsid antigen more sensitive, so both are used; plasma EBV DNA load is now used for screening high-risk populations, for risk stratification and to monitor for recurrence, although assays still need standardisation.
!imaging | Nasopharyngeal carcinoma and a Thornwaldt cyst on imaging.
!update Current evidence
Screening 20,174 men in Hong Kong with plasma EBV DNA detected nasopharyngeal carcinoma earlier, with a sensitivity of 97.1% and specificity of 98.6% (Chan et al., *N Engl J Med* 2017).
!!

Hairy polyp, teratoma (congenital) | Melanoma, haemangiopericytoma

Angiofibroma is covered in [[t:juv]]; this topic is about carcinoma.
## Other malignancies
- **Lymphoma:** a bulky submucosal mass, usually B-cell non-Hodgkin, often with disease elsewhere in Waldeyer's ring; sinonasal NK/T-cell lymphoma in Asian patients.
- **Rhabdomyosarcoma:** the embryonal type in children, as a polypoid mass with rapid growth and cranial nerve involvement; chemotherapy and radiotherapy.
- **Chordoma:** from notochordal remnants in the clivus, destroying bone and presenting in the nasopharynx; surgery and proton therapy.
- **Plasmacytoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma and melanoma** are rare.

# Subtypes and variants | Staging.
!table TNM staging of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (AJCC version 9 and UICC TNM 9, from 2025)
Category | Definition
T0 | No primary seen, but EBV-positive cervical node metastasis
T1 | Confined to the nasopharynx, or extending to the oropharynx or nasal cavity (including the septum) without parapharyngeal involvement
T2 | Parapharyngeal extension, or involvement of the medial or lateral pterygoid or prevertebral muscles
T3 | Unequivocal invasion of the bony skull base (including pterygoid structures), paranasal sinuses or cervical vertebrae
T4 | Intracranial extension, unequivocal cranial nerve involvement, hypopharynx, orbit (including the inferior orbital fissure), parotid, or soft tissue beyond the anterolateral surface of the lateral pterygoid
N1 | Unilateral cervical, or unilateral or bilateral retropharyngeal, nodes: all 6 cm or less, above the caudal border of the cricoid, and without advanced ENE
N2 | Bilateral cervical nodes: all 6 cm or less, above the caudal border of the cricoid, and without advanced ENE
N3 | Any of: node over 6 cm; extension below the caudal border of the cricoid; advanced radiological or clinical ENE (invading muscle, skin or the neurovascular bundle)
M1a | Three or fewer metastatic lesions, in one or more organs
M1b | More than three metastatic lesions

Version 9 keeps the T criteria but clarifies them, adds advanced ENE to N3, reserves stage IV for metastatic disease and divides it by the number of metastases (Pan et al., *JAMA Oncol* 2024).
Older AJCC editions defined T1 as confined to the nasopharynx and N3b as any node in the supraclavicular fossa (Ho's triangle, bounded by the medial and lateral ends of the clavicle and the point where neck meets shoulder). In every edition the nodal staging differs from other head and neck sites: low neck nodes carry the worst prognosis whatever their size.

# Management | Radiation first.
## Primary treatment
The nasopharynx is hard to reach surgically, and these tumours are radiosensitive, so *radiotherapy is the treatment of choice*. The trials behind it used eighth-edition stage groups; their version 9 equivalents are given alongside (see the stage-group table above).
- **T1 N0 (eighth-edition stage I; part of version 9 stage IA):** radiotherapy alone to the nasopharynx and both sides of the neck, about 70 Gy.
- **Non-metastatic disease beyond T1 N0 (eighth-edition stage II to IVA; version 9 stage IA with T2 N0, IB, II and III):** concurrent chemoradiation with cisplatin, which improves survival over radiotherapy alone; the cumulative cisplatin dose matters more than the schedule, with about 200 mg/m² as the effective threshold (160 mg/m² after induction). **Induction gemcitabine and cisplatin** before chemoradiotherapy improves survival in high-risk patients, and **adjuvant metronomic capecitabine** improves failure-free survival in high-risk disease.
- **Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT)** delivers a high dose to the tumour while sparing the brainstem, spinal cord, optic apparatus, temporal lobes, cochleae and parotids. Proton and carbon-ion therapy have dosimetric advantages without proven better outcomes yet.
- **Metastatic disease (version 9 stage IVA and IVB):** platinum-based chemotherapy, now with immunotherapy, and local treatment in selected cases.
### Drugs and doses

Stage | Treatment and dose | Notes
T1 N0 (eighth-edition stage I; version 9 IA) | IMRT alone, about 70 Gy in 33 to 35 fractions to the primary, with elective neck irradiation | 
Eighth-edition stage II (selected) to IVA (version 9 IA with T2 N0, IB, II and III) | IMRT 70 Gy with concurrent cisplatin 100 mg/m² every 3 weeks (or 40 mg/m² weekly) | Aim for a cumulative cisplatin dose of at least 200 mg/m²
Locoregionally advanced (eighth-edition III to IVA; version 9 II and III) | Induction gemcitabine 1 g/m² on days 1 and 8 with cisplatin 80 mg/m² on day 1, every 3 weeks for 3 cycles, then chemoradiotherapy | Better survival in a phase 3 trial (Zhang et al., *N Engl J Med* 2019)

## Residual and recurrent disease
- **Neck:** persistent or recurrent nodes after radiation are treated by neck dissection. Older texts advise a radical dissection because disease is often more extensive than it seems; current practice is a comprehensive (modified radical) dissection, sacrificing structures only where tumour involves them.
- **Nasopharynx:** after restaging with MRI and PET-CT, resectable recurrences are treated by *nasopharyngectomy*; in a randomised trial from China, endoscopic nasopharyngectomy gave better overall survival and fewer severe late effects than re-irradiation with IMRT (Liu et al., *Lancet Oncol* 2021). Approaches are endoscopic for limited central tumours, or open through a maxillary swing, transpalatal, Le Fort I, midfacial degloving or infratemporal fossa approach. Extension around the internal carotid, intracranially or widely into the parapharyngeal space contraindicates surgery.
- **Re-irradiation:** IMRT, proton therapy or brachytherapy (interstitial implants, gold grains), at greater risk of necrosis of the skull base and temporal lobe, cranial nerve palsies and carotid rupture.
!ops | Salvage nasopharyngectomy approaches.
!update Current evidence
In a phase 3 trial of 480 patients with locoregionally advanced disease, induction gemcitabine and cisplatin before chemoradiotherapy improved three-year recurrence-free survival (85.3% vs 76.5%) and overall survival (Zhang et al., *N Engl J Med* 2019). Adjuvant metronomic capecitabine for a year after chemoradiotherapy improved failure-free survival in high-risk patients (Chen et al., *Lancet* 2021).
!!
## Follow-up
Every three to four months for two years, six-monthly to five years, then yearly, with yearly CT or MRI for five years.

# Complications | Effects of treatment.
Xerostomia and dental caries, otitis media with effusion and sensorineural hearing loss, trismus, hypothyroidism and pituitary failure, osteoradionecrosis of the skull base, temporal lobe necrosis, cranial neuropathy, carotid stenosis, and second cancers. Grommets in irradiated ears often discharge chronically.

# Prognosis | Distant failure.
With concurrent chemoradiation, local and regional control is about 70 to 80% and five-year survival about 70 to 80%; distant metastasis is now the commonest mode of failure.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Inserting a grommet in an adult with a one-sided effusion without examining the nasopharynx.
- Excising a high level II neck node before endoscopy and needle aspiration.
- Missing a sixth nerve palsy as the sign of skull base invasion.
