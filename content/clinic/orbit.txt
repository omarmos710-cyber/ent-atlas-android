@stand The orbit shares thin walls with three sinuses, so the rhinologist meets its diseases often: the eye pushed out by a mucocele, a sinus infection or a tumour, the protruding eyes of thyroid disease that the nose can decompress, and the watering eye that a dacryocystorhinostomy can drain. Proptosis is a sign; its direction, speed and sidedness point to the cause.

# Overview | The eye pushed forward.
## Definitions
*Proptosis* is forward displacement of the eyeball; *exophthalmos* is often used for proptosis from thyroid eye disease. Measured with an exophthalmometer (Hertel): a difference of more than 2 mm between the eyes, or a reading above about 20 mm, is abnormal. *Pseudoproptosis* (a large eye in myopia, lid retraction, or a small opposite eye) must be excluded.
## Applied anatomy
The orbit is a four-sided pyramid. Its medial wall (the lamina papyracea) separates it from the ethmoids, its floor from the maxillary sinus, its roof from the frontal sinus and anterior cranial fossa; the sphenoid lies at the apex beside the optic canal. The orbital periosteum and septum limit the spread of infection; the valveless ophthalmic veins drain to the cavernous sinus.

## Why it matters for ENT
Sinus infections, mucoceles, fungal disease, polyps, angiofibroma, inverted papilloma and sinonasal cancers all invade the orbit; endoscopic surgery can injure it; and the orbit can be decompressed and drained through the nose.

## From the sinuses
Mucoceles of the frontal and ethmoid sinuses, allergic fungal polyposis, inverted papilloma, angiofibroma, olfactory neuroblastoma and sinus carcinoma, and orbital complications of sinusitis (see those topics).

## Clues from the history and examination
- **Unilateral or bilateral:** thyroid eye disease is the commonest cause of both bilateral and unilateral proptosis in adults; orbital cellulitis is the commonest cause in children.
- **Speed:** sudden (haemorrhage, cellulitis), weeks (inflammation, malignancy), years (benign tumours, mucoceles, fibrous dysplasia).
- **Direction:** *axial* proptosis suggests a lesion inside the muscle cone (haemangioma, optic nerve tumour, thyroid eye disease); *non-axial* displacement points away from the lesion: down and out with a frontoethmoid mucocele, laterally with an ethmoid lesion, upwards with a maxillary tumour.
- **Variable proptosis** with straining or bending: venous varix.
- **Pulsatile proptosis** with a bruit and arterialised conjunctival vessels: carotid-cavernous fistula.
- **Pain:** inflammation, infection or malignancy.

# Important conditions | Inside and around the orbit.
## Thyroid eye disease
The commonest cause of proptosis in adults, bilateral or one-sided, in hyperthyroid, euthyroid or hypothyroid patients. Lid retraction, lid lag, chemosis, restricted movement and exposure keratopathy; CT shows enlargement of the muscle bellies with sparing of their tendons. Optic nerve compression at the apex threatens vision.
## Idiopathic orbital inflammation
Orbital pseudotumour: painful proptosis, worse on eye movement; may involve the muscles (myositis, including the tendons, unlike thyroid disease), lacrimal gland, sclera or optic nerve. Biopsy shows non-specific inflammation; treated with steroids, immunosuppression or radiotherapy.
## Vascular lesions
- **Cavernous haemangioma:** the commonest benign orbital tumour of adults; painless, slowly progressive axial proptosis from a well-defined, enhancing intraconal mass; excised by lateral orbitotomy or endoscopically.
- **Capillary haemangioma:** the commonest orbital tumour of infants; usually involutes by about seven; propranolol or steroids.
- **Venous varix:** proptosis that comes and goes with straining.
- **Carotid-cavernous fistula:** pulsatile proptosis, bruit, chemosis and arterialised vessels; endovascular closure.
## Tumours
- **Lymphoma:** the commonest malignant orbital tumour of adults, often anterior and palpable; biopsy, radiotherapy and chemotherapy.
- **Rhabdomyosarcoma:** the commonest primary orbital malignancy of children (around six or seven years), with rapid proptosis; chemoradiation.
- **Dermoid cyst:** the commonest benign orbital tumour of children, at the frontozygomatic suture or deeper; excised.
- **Optic nerve glioma** (children, neurofibromatosis) and **optic nerve sheath meningioma** (adults).
- **Lacrimal gland tumours:** pleomorphic adenoma and adenoid cystic carcinoma in the upper outer orbit.
- **Metastases:** breast most often, then lung, prostate and kidney; neuroblastoma in children.

# Evaluation | A joint ENT and eye assessment.
## Examination
Visual acuity, colour vision and pupils (a relative afferent pupillary defect means optic nerve compromise), eye movements and diplopia, the degree and direction of proptosis, lids, conjunctiva and cornea, fundus, and nasal endoscopy.
## Investigations
- **CT** of the orbits and sinuses: bone, sinus disease, fractures, muscle size.
- **MRI** for soft tissue, the optic nerve, apex and intracranial extension.
- Thyroid function and antibodies; blood tests for vasculitis and lymphoma as indicated.
- Biopsy for undiagnosed masses, endoscopically or by orbitotomy.

# Procedures | What the nose can do for the eye.
## Endoscopic orbital decompression
Removal of the medial wall (lamina papyracea) and the medial part of the floor, opening the periorbita so orbital fat can prolapse into the ethmoid and maxillary spaces. For thyroid eye disease with optic neuropathy, severe exposure or disfiguring proptosis. Diplopia may follow.
## Optic nerve decompression
Removal of the bone of the optic canal through the sphenoid in traumatic or compressive optic neuropathy; its value in trauma is debated.
## Dacryocystorhinostomy
For epiphora from obstruction of the nasolacrimal duct: a window is made from the lacrimal sac into the middle meatus, through the lacrimal bone and frontal process of the maxilla, endoscopically or through an external skin incision, sometimes with silicone tubes.
!anat s101_33 nd96_4 | The lacrimal drainage system, and the lacrimal apparatus.
## Drainage of orbital abscess
Endoscopic or external drainage of a subperiosteal or orbital abscess complicating sinusitis.
## Lateral canthotomy and cantholysis
An emergency release for retrobulbar haemorrhage after trauma or sinus surgery: a tense, proptosed eye with rising pressure and falling vision. Cut the lateral canthal tendon and its inferior crus within minutes.
!flag Retrobulbar haemorrhage
Pain, tense proptosis, a hard globe, ophthalmoplegia and loss of vision after injury or sinus surgery. Do not wait for imaging: perform lateral canthotomy and cantholysis at once, then call ophthalmology.
!!
## Orbital exenteration
Removal of the orbital contents for malignancy invading beyond the periorbita.

!update Who needs drainage in children
Most children with orbital complications are treated medically at first, with intravenous antibiotics covering *Streptococcus anginosus* (milleri), the commonest organism in complicated sinusitis in young people, plus nasal decongestant, and with vision, pupils, colour vision and eye movements checked at least every few hours. A small **medial subperiosteal abscess** in a child with normal vision often resolves without surgery. Features that favour early drainage (based on the criteria of Garcia and Harris) are age nine years or older, frontal sinusitis, a non-medial or large abscess, gas in the abscess, a dental source, any reduction in visual acuity or colour vision, an afferent pupillary defect, intracranial complications, or failure to improve within 24 to 48 hours. Adolescents with frontal sinusitis and orbital involvement need imaging of the brain as well, because *S. anginosus* readily forms intracranial abscesses and Pott's puffy tumour.
!!
