@stand A sore throat is one of the commonest reasons anyone sees a doctor, and almost all acute sore throats are viral and self-limiting. The ENT surgeon sees the other end of the spectrum: the patient who cannot swallow saliva, the quinsy, the supraglottitis that will obstruct the airway, the neck space abscess, and the adult with a persistent one-sided sore throat and earache that is a cancer. The approach separates acute from chronic, recognises the airway and the deep collection, uses antibiotics with judgement, and knows when tonsillectomy is justified.

# Acute or chronic? | Then: is the airway safe?
## The first questions
An **acute** sore throat lasts days to two weeks and is usually infective. A **chronic** sore throat persists beyond three weeks and has a different list: reflux, chronic tonsillitis, tonsilloliths, postnasal drip, smoking, voice misuse, and malignancy. In either case the first judgement is about the airway and the swallow.
!flag Red flags in sore throat
- **Drooling, inability to swallow saliva, muffled voice, stridor or sitting forward:** supraglottitis or a deep neck abscess — protect the airway, do not lie the patient flat, do not examine the throat with a tongue depressor in a child.
- **Trismus with unilateral swelling and uvular deviation:** quinsy.
- **Neck swelling, torticollis or reduced neck movement, especially in a child:** retropharyngeal or parapharyngeal abscess.
- **Persistent sore throat with fever, rigors and neck tenderness after tonsillitis:** Lemierre's syndrome.
- **Sore throat in a patient taking carbimazole, clozapine or chemotherapy:** neutropenia — urgent full blood count.
- **Persistent unilateral sore throat, referred otalgia, dysphagia, hoarseness, weight loss or a neck lump, especially in a smoker:** cancer until proven otherwise.
!!

# The acute sore throat | Assess, score, decide.
## History and examination
Ask about the duration, fever, ability to swallow food and fluids, voice change, breathing, earache and trismus, and about cough and coryza, which point to a viral cause. Examine the oropharynx, the tonsils, the neck nodes and the airway, and, when the history suggests supraglottitis, examine the larynx with a flexible endoscope in a safe setting rather than with a tongue depressor.

## Who needs antibiotics?
Most acute sore throats are viral, and antibiotics shorten symptoms by only about a day in bacterial tonsillitis. Scoring systems identify patients more likely to have streptococcal infection. **FeverPAIN** scores one point each for fever, purulence, attending rapidly (within three days), inflamed tonsils and no cough or coryza: a score of 0 to 1 needs no antibiotic, 2 to 3 a delayed or no antibiotic, and 4 to 5 an immediate antibiotic or a delayed one. The **Centor** criteria (tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical nodes, fever and absence of cough) are used similarly, with antibiotics considered at three or four. Phenoxymethylpenicillin for five to ten days is the first choice, with clarithromycin in penicillin allergy. **Amoxicillin is avoided** because it causes a rash in infectious mononucleosis.

Admission is needed for patients who cannot swallow fluids, are dehydrated, have a suspected collection or airway compromise, or are systemically unwell. They need intravenous fluids, analgesia, intravenous benzylpenicillin and often a single dose of dexamethasone.
## The important acute causes
**Acute tonsillitis** is viral or bacterial (group A streptococcus is the commonest bacterium). **Infectious mononucleosis** from Epstein–Barr virus gives a severe exudative tonsillitis with palatal petechiae, generalised lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly; it is confirmed by a monospot or EBV serology and atypical lymphocytes. Patients avoid contact sports for several weeks because of splenic rupture, and steroids are used for airway compromise. **Peritonsillar abscess (quinsy)** produces trismus, a hot potato voice, unilateral swelling above the tonsil and a deviated uvula; it is drained by needle aspiration or incision. **Supraglottitis** produces severe odynophagia with relatively normal oropharynx, a muffled voice and drooling. **Deep neck space infections** need contrast CT and drainage. **Lemierre's syndrome** is a septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, usually with *Fusobacterium necrophorum*, after a sore throat. Other causes include HIV seroconversion, diphtheria in the unvaccinated, gonococcal pharyngitis, and herpangina or hand, foot and mouth disease in children.

# The chronic sore throat | A different list.
## Causes

## Assessment
Every adult with a sore throat lasting more than three weeks, especially if unilateral, needs a full head and neck examination with flexible endoscopy of the nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx and larynx, palpation of the tongue base and tonsils, and examination of the neck. Asymmetrical tonsils in an adult, a firm or ulcerated tonsil, or a neck node lead to biopsy or tonsillectomy for histology, and imaging.

# Tonsillectomy | When it is justified.
## Indications
In adults and children, the **SIGN** criteria for tonsillectomy in recurrent tonsillitis are sore throats due to acute tonsillitis that are disabling and prevent normal functioning, with **seven or more** well-documented, clinically significant, adequately treated episodes in the preceding year, **five or more** in each of the preceding two years, or **three or more** in each of the preceding three years. Other indications include two episodes of quinsy, suspected malignancy, and obstructive sleep-disordered breathing in children.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Examining a child with suspected supraglottitis with a tongue depressor.
- Giving amoxicillin in glandular fever.
- Missing agranulocytosis in a patient on carbimazole.
- Treating a unilateral chronic sore throat with repeated antibiotics in a smoker.
!!
