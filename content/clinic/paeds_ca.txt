@stand Most lumps in a child's neck are reactive nodes, and cancer is rare. But when it comes, it is not the squamous carcinoma of adults: it is lymphoma, a sarcoma, a neural tumour or a thyroid cancer, and diagnostic delay is common. The ENT surgeon's job is to recognise the lump that does not behave, image it, take the right tissue in the right way after talking to the oncologist and the pathologist, and keep the airway safe while doing it.

# Summary | Different tumours, different rules.
Cancer is still the second commonest cause of death in childhood after trauma, and around 12% of childhood malignancies arise in the head and neck. Squamous cell carcinoma is extremely rare in children.
!table The malignancies of the child's head and neck
Tumour | Typical age | Typical presentation
Hodgkin lymphoma | Adolescents | Firm, painless nodes, often supraclavicular; fever, night sweats, weight loss
Non-Hodgkin lymphoma | 2 to 12 years | Rapidly enlarging nodes, Waldeyer's ring, often widespread
Rhabdomyosarcoma | Under 5 years in almost half | Painless mass in the orbit, nose, sinuses, nasopharynx, ear or neck
Neuroblastoma | Infants, under 1 year | Firm fixed neck mass, Horner syndrome
Thyroid carcinoma | Older children and adolescents | Thyroid nodule with cervical nodes
Nasopharyngeal carcinoma | Adolescents | Neck nodes, glue ear in one ear, nasal obstruction
Salivary malignancy | Older children | Solitary parotid mass, rapid growth
!!
!differ
Oxford Textbook: lymphoma 27%, neural tumours 23%, thyroid tumours 21%, soft tissue sarcomas 12% of childhood head and neck malignancies.
Oxford Specialist Handbook: lymphoma about 50%, rhabdomyosarcoma 20%, neuroblastoma 6%, nasopharyngeal carcinoma 6%, thyroid carcinoma 4%; rhabdomyosarcoma and neuroblastoma are the commonest under 2.
Both agree that lymphoma leads and squamous carcinoma is rare; the difference reflects the series and the age range counted.
In practice: think of lymphoma first in a child's malignant neck mass, and of rhabdomyosarcoma and neuroblastoma under 2.
!!
Each tumour has its own chapter, starting with [[@Hodgkin lymphoma]].

# Clinical features | The lump that does not behave.
## History
Duration and growth, fever, night sweats, weight loss, itching, pain, nasal obstruction or bleeding, one-sided hearing loss or ear discharge, change in the voice or breathing, swallowing, and contact with tuberculosis, travel or HIV.
## Examination
Ears, nose, mouth and teeth, and the whole neck, then the axillae, groins, abdomen (liver and spleen) and skin. In the eye look for proptosis, Horner syndrome and heterochromia. Re-examine once fever, pain and dehydration have been corrected, as the signs often change. The benign and congenital masses are in [[t:paeds_neck]].
!flag Features that need urgent investigation
- A node larger than about 2 cm, or one that keeps growing or has not shrunk after about 6 weeks.
- A supraclavicular node at any size.
- A hard, fixed or matted mass.
- Fever, night sweats, weight loss, pallor, bruising, or an enlarged liver or spleen.
- Stridor, dysphagia, cranial nerve palsy, Horner syndrome or proptosis.
- A one-sided nasal or nasopharyngeal mass, or an aural polyp with facial weakness.
Any large, rapidly growing or unusual lump needs urgent imaging and tissue.
!!
!update Current evidence
**Referral.** NICE NG12 recommends a very urgent referral for children with unexplained lymphadenopathy that is enlarging, hard, fixed or associated with fever, night sweats, weight loss or hepatosplenomegaly.
!!

# Diagnosis | Imaging and tissue.
## Investigations
- **Ultrasound** first: it tells cystic from solid, shows the architecture of nodes and guides biopsy.
- **Chest X-ray** before any general anaesthetic, to look for a **mediastinal mass**.
- **Full blood count and film, LDH and liver function**; urine catecholamines (HVA and VMA) if neuroblastoma is possible; EBV serology in suspected nasopharyngeal carcinoma.
- **MRI** (or CT) of the primary and neck to define the extent; staging CT, bone scan, MIBG scan or bone marrow as the oncologist directs.
!flag The mediastinal mass and the anaesthetic
A child with lymphoma may have a large anterior mediastinal mass that compresses the trachea. Induction of general anaesthesia can cause complete airway or cardiovascular collapse. Get a chest X-ray first; if there is a mass, discuss with the anaesthetist ([[t:anaes]]) and oncologist, and consider biopsy under local anaesthetic or sampling another site.
!!
## Taking tissue
Talk to the oncologist and the pathologist *before* the biopsy so that the sample answers the question.
- **Excision biopsy** of a whole node is preferred for suspected lymphoma: the architecture matters. Send it *fresh*, not in formalin, so that part can go for flow cytometry, cytogenetics and molecular tests.
- **Core biopsy** under ultrasound is an alternative if the pathologist is confident it will be diagnostic.
- **Fine-needle aspiration** is of limited use in children: it rarely gives enough for lymphoma typing.
- A deep primary (nasopharynx, sinuses, ear) is biopsied endoscopically or under anaesthetic.
!ops
## Investigations: what and when
!table Childhood head and neck cancer: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
First line | Ultrasound of the neck | Cystic or solid; abnormal node architecture; guides biopsy
Before any general anaesthetic | Chest X-ray | Anterior mediastinal mass (airway and circulatory collapse risk)
Every patient | Full blood count and film, LDH, uric acid, renal and liver function | Leukaemia, lymphoma, tumour lysis risk
Diagnosis (gold standard) | Excision biopsy of a whole node, sent fresh (lymphoma); core or open biopsy of a mass | Histology, flow cytometry, cytogenetics, molecular tests
Suspected neuroblastoma | Urine catecholamines (HVA, VMA); MIBG scan | 
Staging | MRI of the primary; CT or PET-CT; bone marrow as directed by oncology | 
!!

# Management | The multidisciplinary team.
Childhood cancer is treated in a paediatric oncology centre under national or international protocols, with paediatric oncologists, radiation oncologists, surgeons, pathologists, radiologists, nurses, psychologists, dietitians and speech therapists. The ENT surgeon diagnoses, secures the airway, provides surgical resection or access when needed, and manages the ear, nose and throat effects of treatment.
## Treatment: what and when
!table Childhood head and neck cancer: treatment
Tumour | Treatment | Notes
Hodgkin lymphoma | Response-adapted chemotherapy (EuroNet-PHL protocols), radiotherapy only for inadequate response | Excellent survival
Non-Hodgkin lymphoma | Intensive multi-agent chemotherapy by subtype; tumour lysis prophylaxis (hydration, allopurinol or rasburicase) | Burkitt grows fastest
Rhabdomyosarcoma | Multi-agent chemotherapy (for example ifosfamide, vincristine, actinomycin D in European protocols), with radiotherapy and surgery for local control | Parameningeal sites need radiotherapy
Neuroblastoma | By risk group: observation, surgery, or chemotherapy, with immunotherapy for high risk | 
Differentiated thyroid carcinoma | Total thyroidectomy with neck dissection as needed; radioiodine by risk | Survival over 95% despite spread
Nasopharyngeal carcinoma | Induction chemotherapy then chemoradiotherapy | Lower radiotherapy doses studied in good responders
!!
Treatment follows national children's cancer protocols (Children's Cancer and Leukaemia Group, European cooperative groups); doses are protocol-specific and are not given here.

# Complications | Late effects.
Survivors live for decades with the consequences of treatment, so follow-up is long term.
- **Radiotherapy to the head and neck:** impaired growth of bone and teeth, facial asymmetry, Eustachian tube dysfunction and glue ear, sensorineural hearing loss, cataract, hypothyroidism, pituitary dysfunction, osteoradionecrosis and second cancers.
- **Cisplatin:** permanent high-frequency hearing loss, worse in young children; monitor hearing during and after treatment ([[t:hnrt]]).
- **Surgery:** cosmetic deformity, cranial nerve deficits, swallowing and speech problems.
- **Psychological and educational** effects for the child and family.
!update Current evidence
**Late effects.** Children treated with head and neck radiotherapy need lifelong follow-up for growth, dental, endocrine, hearing (especially after cisplatin) and second cancers.
!!

# Hodgkin lymphoma {dz} | Adolescents.
## Clinical features
Commonest in adolescents and young adults. Enlarged, rubbery, non-tender nodes, especially supraclavicular; up to a third have fever, night sweats, weight loss or itching.
## Diagnosis
Staged by the Ann Arbor system after CT of the neck, chest, abdomen and pelvis.
### Staging
!table Lymphoma: Lugano staging (Cheson et al., *J Clin Oncol* 2014; modified Ann Arbor)
Stage | Extent
I | One node or a group of adjacent nodes; IE a single extranodal lesion without nodal disease
II | Two or more nodal groups on the same side of the diaphragm; IIE with limited contiguous extranodal involvement
II bulky | Stage II with bulky disease (single mass 10 cm or more, or over a third of the thoracic diameter); treated as limited or advanced according to histology and risk factors
III | Nodes on both sides of the diaphragm, or nodes above the diaphragm with the spleen
IV | Additional non-contiguous extranodal involvement
A / B | B symptoms (fever over 38 °C, drenching night sweats, loss of more than 10% of body weight in 6 months); recorded only for Hodgkin lymphoma
!!
PET-CT stages FDG-avid lymphomas and replaces routine bone marrow biopsy in Hodgkin lymphoma. Treatment is grouped as limited (I–II) or advanced (III–IV). Children are staged with the same system in Hodgkin lymphoma; childhood non-Hodgkin lymphoma uses the International Paediatric NHL Staging System (2015), which replaced the St Jude (Murphy) system.
## Management
Treatment is chemotherapy, sometimes with radiotherapy, and the ENT role is the diagnostic biopsy.
## Prognosis
Early disease is cured in about 90%.

# Non-Hodgkin lymphoma {dz} | 2 to 12 years.
## Clinical features
The commonest lymphoma between 2 and 12 years. More often widespread at presentation, it may involve Waldeyer's ring (a unilateral enlarged tonsil) and grow very fast, as in Burkitt lymphoma. Immunophenotyping separates B-cell and T-cell types.
!pearl The asymmetrical tonsil
A child with one tonsil much larger than the other, especially if it grows quickly, looks abnormal, or comes with nodes or systemic symptoms, needs the tonsil removed ([[t:tonsil]]) for histology to exclude lymphoma. Send it fresh.
!!
## Management and prognosis
Combination chemotherapy is the mainstay; prognosis is poorer than Hodgkin lymphoma but good for early disease.

# Rhabdomyosarcoma {dz} | The commonest soft tissue sarcoma.
## Epidemiology
The commonest soft tissue sarcoma of childhood; about 43% occur under 5 years and the head and neck is the commonest site.
## Subtypes and variants
Histological types are *embryonal* (about 60%, better prognosis, including the botryoid type), *alveolar* (about 20%, worse) and others. Sites are grouped as:
- **Orbital:** proptosis; the best outlook.
- **Parameningeal:** nasopharynx, sinuses, middle ear and mastoid, infratemporal fossa; spread to the skull base and meninges is common, so these are unfavourable.
- **Other head and neck sites:** face, scalp, mouth, neck, larynx.
## Clinical features
In the ear it mimics chronic otitis media with an aural polyp, discharge, bleeding and facial palsy.
!flag The aural polyp in a young child
An aural polyp with bleeding, facial weakness or rapid regrowth in a young child is a rhabdomyosarcoma until proved otherwise. Send every aural polyp for histology.
!!
### Staging
!table Rhabdomyosarcoma: pretreatment TNM stage (IRS/COG)
Stage | Site | T, size | N | M
1 | Favourable: orbit, head and neck excluding parameningeal, genitourinary (not bladder or prostate), biliary | T1 or T2, any size | N0, N1 or Nx | M0
2 | Unfavourable: parameningeal, bladder or prostate, extremity, trunk and others | T1 or T2, 5 cm or less (a) | N0 or Nx | M0
3 | Unfavourable | T1 or T2, 5 cm or less with N1; or over 5 cm (b) with any N | N0, N1 or Nx | M0
4 | Any | Any | Any | M1
!!
!table Rhabdomyosarcoma: IRS clinical group (after surgery)
Group | Definition
I | Localised, completely resected
II | Grossly resected with microscopic residual (IIA), resected nodes (IIB), or both (IIC)
III | Incomplete resection or biopsy only, with gross residual disease
IV | Distant metastases at diagnosis
!!
T1 is confined to the site of origin and T2 extends beyond it. Parameningeal sites (nasopharynx, nasal cavity, sinuses, middle ear and mastoid, pterygopalatine and infratemporal fossae) are unfavourable. Stage, group, histology (fusion status, PAX3 or PAX7–FOXO1) and age together give the risk group (low, intermediate, high) that decides treatment; European protocols use the EpSSG risk grouping.
## Management
Treatment is multimodal under a paediatric oncology protocol: biopsy, chemotherapy and radiotherapy, with surgery when the tumour can be removed without disabling loss of function.
## Prognosis
Five-year survival ranges from about 90% for completely resected disease to about 40% for metastatic disease.

# Neuroblastoma {dz} | Infants, Horner syndrome.
## Clinical features
The commonest malignancy of infants under 1 year, from neural crest cells of the sympathetic chain. Only about 5% arise in the neck, where it presents as a firm, fixed, non-tender mass with ipsilateral *Horner syndrome* and *heterochromia of the iris*, and sometimes stridor, dysphagia or cranial nerve palsies.
## Diagnosis
Urinary HVA and VMA are raised in about 90%. It is staged with MRI or CT, MIBG and bone scans and bone marrow biopsy.
### Staging
!table Neuroblastoma: International Neuroblastoma Risk Group Staging System (INRGSS, pretreatment)
Stage | Definition
L1 | Localised tumour confined to one compartment, without image-defined risk factors
L2 | Locoregional tumour with one or more image-defined risk factors (in the neck: encasing the carotid, vertebral artery or jugular vein; extending to the skull base; compressing the trachea)
M | Distant metastasis (except MS)
MS | Under 18 months, with metastases confined to skin, liver and/or bone marrow (marrow under 10%)
!!
The INRGSS is decided from imaging before any surgery, unlike the older surgical INSS (in which the equivalent of MS, stage 4S, applied under 12 months). Stage combines with age, histology, MYCN and other genetic features in the INRG risk classification.
## Management
Localised disease is excised; unresectable or metastatic disease is treated with chemotherapy.
## Prognosis
Cervical neuroblastoma does better than other sites because it is noticed early, and infants under 1 often do well whatever the stage.

# Thyroid carcinoma {dz} | Excellent survival despite spread.
## Clinical features
Mostly papillary carcinoma ([[t:thyroid]]). A thyroid nodule in a child is more likely to be malignant than one in an adult, and children more often present with cervical node and lung metastases, yet survival is excellent. Previous radiation to the neck is a risk factor. *Medullary carcinoma* occurs in multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2, where children with a RET mutation are offered prophylactic thyroidectomy.
## Management
Management is by a specialist thyroid team: total thyroidectomy with the appropriate neck dissection, and radioiodine for selected patients.
!update Current evidence
**Thyroid cancer in children.** The ATA paediatric guideline recommends total thyroidectomy for most differentiated cancers because they are more often multifocal and node-positive than in adults, with radioactive iodine selected by risk, and genetic testing for medullary carcinoma (*RET*) in at-risk families, with prophylactic thyroidectomy timed by the mutation (Francis et al., *Thyroid* 2015).
!!

# Nasopharyngeal carcinoma {dz} | Adolescents.
## Clinical features
Rare in children ([[t:npc]]), mainly in adolescents, and usually the undifferentiated type associated with Epstein-Barr virus.
It presents as in adults with neck nodes, a unilateral middle ear effusion, nasal obstruction or epistaxis and cranial nerve palsies.
## Differential diagnosis
In a child with a nasopharyngeal mass the differential is rhabdomyosarcoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma and, in an adolescent boy, [[t:juv|angiofibroma]].
## Management
Treatment is chemoradiotherapy.

# Salivary gland tumours {dz} | The solitary solid mass.
## Clinical features
At least two thirds of salivary swellings in children are not neoplastic, most commonly mucoceles and sialadenitis, and benign tumours are more often haemangiomas or vascular malformations than adenomas. But a *solitary solid salivary mass* in a child is malignant in more than half (against about a quarter in adults); series that count all solid salivary tumours give up to half ([[t:saltum]]). *Mucoepidermoid carcinoma* is the commonest malignancy (about half), followed by acinic cell carcinoma. The parotid is affected more than the submandibular gland, and rapid growth without infection suggests malignancy.
## Management
Management follows adult principles: imaging, fine-needle aspiration or core biopsy, and parotidectomy with facial nerve preservation.

# Other tumours {dz} | Rare.
Langerhans cell histiocytosis (lytic skull and temporal bone lesions, otorrhoea, nodes; [[t:jaw]]), teratomas and other germ cell tumours, Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and olfactory neuroblastoma are rare.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Repeated courses of antibiotics for a persistent hard node.
- Putting a lymphoma node into formalin.
- Anaesthetising a child with stridor or a mediastinal mass without a chest X-ray and planning.
- Calling a bleeding aural polyp in a toddler chronic otitis media.
- Fine-needle aspiration as the only test for suspected lymphoma.
!!
