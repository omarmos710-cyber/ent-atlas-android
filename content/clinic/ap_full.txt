@stand "My ear feels blocked" is one of the commonest things patients say, and the cause can lie anywhere from the canal to the cochlea. Most blocked ears are wax, a middle ear effusion or a sluggish Eustachian tube. The ones not to miss are the sudden sensorineural hearing loss, which feels like a blocked ear and needs steroids within days, the unilateral effusion in an adult that hides a nasopharyngeal tumour, and the early Ménière's disease that announces itself as fullness with low-frequency loss.

# Where is the block? | Canal, middle ear, tube or cochlea.
## Four places
The sensation of fullness has four main sources. **The canal** contributes wax, debris, a foreign body, otitis externa or exostoses. **The middle ear** contributes an effusion, a retracted or perforated drum, haemotympanum or, rarely, a mass. **The Eustachian tube** fails to open (obstructive dysfunction) or stays open (patulous tube). **The inner ear** produces fullness in Ménière's disease, sudden sensorineural loss, superior canal dehiscence and other third-window lesions, and with raised intracranial pressure. Temporomandibular joint disorders and anxiety can also make an ear feel full.
!flag Do not miss
- **Sudden hearing loss with fullness:** test the hearing that day. A sensorineural loss of 30 dB or more over three frequencies within 72 hours needs steroids now and MRI later.
- **A unilateral middle ear effusion in an adult:** examine and, if in doubt, biopsy the nasopharynx.
- **Fullness with low-frequency loss and episodic vertigo:** Ménière's disease.
- **Fullness with pulsatile tinnitus, headache and visual blurring:** idiopathic intracranial hypertension.
!!

# History | Small differences matter.
## The questions
Ask whether the fullness came on suddenly or gradually, and whether it is constant or comes and goes. A *sudden* onset, especially on waking, with muffled hearing and tinnitus, is a sudden sensorineural loss until tested. Fullness after a cold, a flight or a dive suggests Eustachian tube dysfunction or barotrauma.

Ask whether the patient can "pop" the ear and whether that relieves it. Obstructive tube dysfunction improves when the ear pops; a patulous tube makes the patient hear their own voice and breathing loudly (*autophony*), improves when lying down or with a cold, and is common after weight loss and in pregnancy. Autophony with hearing one's own eye movements or footsteps, or vertigo with loud noise or straining, points to superior canal dehiscence.

Ask about episodic vertigo, fluctuating hearing and roaring tinnitus (Ménière's disease), nasal blockage, rhinitis and reflux (tube dysfunction), jaw pain and clicking (temporomandibular disorder), and in adults about epistaxis, nasal blockage, neck lumps and ethnicity (nasopharyngeal carcinoma).

# Examination and tests | Look, then measure.
## Examination
Otoscopy shows wax, otitis externa, a perforation, a dull retracted drum with an air–fluid level or bubbles, or a blue drum of haemotympanum. Watch the drum during deep breathing: in a patulous tube it moves in and out with respiration. Tuning forks are essential, because they separate a conductive from a sensorineural loss at the bedside within minutes. With Weber to the blocked ear and Rinne negative, the loss is conductive; with Weber away from the blocked ear and Rinne positive, it is sensorineural.

Examine the nose and nasopharynx with an endoscope, and palpate the temporomandibular joints and the neck.
## Tests
**Pure tone audiometry** is the key test. A conductive loss points to the canal or middle ear, a low-frequency sensorineural loss to hydrops, and a sudden flat or high-frequency sensorineural loss to sudden sensorineural hearing loss. **Tympanometry** shows a flat type B trace with normal canal volume in an effusion, a negative-pressure type C trace in tube dysfunction, and a normal type A trace in inner ear causes, with a large volume if the drum is perforated. Respiratory variation on tympanometry supports a patulous tube.

The **seven-item Eustachian Tube Dysfunction Questionnaire (ETDQ-7)** scores symptoms such as pressure, pain, a clogged feeling, symptoms with colds, popping, tinnitus and muffled hearing, and is used to select patients for treatment. **Tubomanometry** measures how the tube opens under nasopharyngeal pressure in specialist centres. **MRI** is used for an asymmetric sensorineural loss and to examine the nasopharynx, and **CT** for suspected dehiscence.

# Causes in turn | What to do for each.
## Wax and the canal
Wax is the commonest cause. It is removed by microsuction, softening drops or irrigation, provided there is no perforation, previous surgery or infection. Otitis externa is treated as such.
## Eustachian tube dysfunction
Obstructive dysfunction is usually *mucosal inflammation* in the cartilaginous tube, from allergic rhinitis, chronic rhinosinusitis, laryngopharyngeal reflux and smoke; in children it is adenoidal hypertrophy and infection, with cleft palate as an important cause. It affects about 1% of adults, and around 40% of children have a temporary episode before age ten.

Management starts with autoinflation (Valsalva or a nasal balloon) and treatment of the nose with saline and a steroid spray. Ventilation tubes treat persistent effusion and retraction. *Balloon dilation Eustachian tuboplasty* is an option for chronic obstructive and baro-challenged dysfunction: a balloon passed through the nose under endoscopic vision is inflated in the cartilaginous tube to about 10 bar for two minutes. Patients are selected by symptoms (ETDQ-7) and testing.

## Patulous Eustachian tube
A patulous tube causes autophony, hearing one's own breathing, and fullness. It is linked to weight loss, pregnancy, hormones, radiotherapy and neuromuscular disease, and some patients "sniff" to close the tube, which can lead to retraction and cholesteatoma. Treatment is explanation, hydration, saline nasal drops and lying down, with augmentation or reconstruction of the tube for disabling cases.
## Middle ear effusion in adults
An adult with a persistent unilateral middle ear effusion needs the nasopharynx examined with an endoscope, and an MRI or biopsy if anything looks abnormal, to exclude *nasopharyngeal carcinoma*, especially in patients from endemic areas. Other causes are tube dysfunction after a cold, barotrauma, radiotherapy and sinonasal disease. Ventilation tubes relieve persistent effusions once the nasopharynx is clear.

## The inner ear
**Sudden sensorineural hearing loss** often presents as a blocked ear with tinnitus; an audiogram confirms it and treatment with oral or intratympanic steroids begins at once, followed by MRI. **Ménière's disease** produces fluctuating fullness, roaring tinnitus and low-frequency loss before or during vertigo attacks. **Superior canal dehiscence** produces fullness, autophony, sound- or pressure-induced vertigo and an apparent conductive loss with normal tympanometry and preserved reflexes. **Raised intracranial pressure**, typically idiopathic intracranial hypertension, produces fullness with pulsatile tinnitus and headache.

Otoscopy | wax or debris: remove and re-test ; effusion, retraction or haemotympanum: go to step 3 ; normal drum: go to step 2

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **The four sources of fullness;** tuning forks and tympanometry to separate them.
- **Sudden sensorineural loss** presenting as a blocked ear.
- **The adult unilateral effusion** and the nasopharynx.
- **Obstructive versus patulous tube dysfunction:** symptoms, tests, treatment, balloon dilation.
- **Third-window disorders** that mimic conductive loss.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Telling a patient with sudden sensorineural loss that "it's just wax" or "Eustachian tube".
- Inserting a grommet in an adult unilateral effusion without examining the nasopharynx.
- Treating a patulous tube with decongestants, which make it worse.
!!
