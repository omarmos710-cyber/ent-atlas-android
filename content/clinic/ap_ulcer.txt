@stand Most mouth ulcers are traumatic or aphthous and heal within two weeks. The approach is built around one rule: **a single ulcer that has not healed within three weeks must be biopsied** to exclude squamous cell carcinoma. Beyond that, the pattern of the ulcers, single or multiple, recurrent or persistent, with or without blisters, skin, eye, gut or genital disease, points to the cause, and simple blood tests find the treatable deficiencies behind many recurrent ulcers.

# Patterns | Single or multiple, acute or recurrent.
## Sorting ulcers

!flag Do not miss
- **A single ulcer over three weeks,** especially indurated, with a raised edge, on the lateral tongue or floor of mouth, or with a neck node: urgent biopsy for squamous cell carcinoma.
- **Leukoplakia or erythroplakia** next to an ulcer.
- **Widespread erosions with skin blisters:** pemphigus vulgaris — urgent dermatology.
- **Ulcers with eye involvement:** pemphigoid or Behçet's disease — ophthalmology.
- **Ulcers with sore throat and fever in a patient on carbimazole or clozapine:** neutropenia.
!!

# History and examination | The whole patient.
## History
Ask how long each ulcer lasts and how often they come, the age of onset, the number and size, any trigger (trauma, stress, menstruation, foods, stopping smoking), and whether they scar. Ask about genital ulcers, eye symptoms, rashes, joint pain, bowel symptoms, weight loss, fatigue and fevers, and take a full drug history. Ask about smoking, alcohol, betel quid and HIV risk.
## Examination
Note the site, size, number, base and edge of each ulcer, and feel it: induration and fixity suggest malignancy. Look for a sharp tooth or ill-fitting denture, white or red patches, blisters, gingival involvement and lichenoid striae. Examine the neck, skin, eyes and, when relevant, genitalia.

# Key causes | Recognising each.
## Recurrent aphthous stomatitis
This is the commonest cause of recurrent ulcers, beginning in childhood or adolescence. **Minor** aphthae are under 1 cm, on non-keratinised mucosa, and heal in 10 to 14 days without scarring. **Major** aphthae are larger, deeper and last weeks, healing with scarring. **Herpetiform** ulcers are crops of many pinhead ulcers that coalesce. Triggers include trauma, stress, hormonal changes, sodium lauryl sulphate toothpastes, and deficiencies of iron, vitamin B12 and folate. Patients with recurrent ulcers should have a full blood count, ferritin, B12, folate and coeliac serology.
## Malignancy
Oral squamous cell carcinoma presents as a non-healing ulcer, often painless at first, with induration and a raised everted edge. Risk factors are smoking, alcohol, betel quid and chewing tobacco. Biopsy is taken from the edge, including normal tissue. The tongue and the floor of mouth are the highest-risk sites.
## Mucocutaneous disease
**Lichen planus** produces white lacy striae with erosions, often bilateral on the buccal mucosa; erosive lichen planus carries a small risk of malignant change and needs follow-up. **Pemphigus vulgaris** produces fragile blisters that rupture into painful erosions, often starting in the mouth; **mucous membrane pemphigoid** produces tougher blisters and desquamative gingivitis and can scar the eyes and larynx. Both need biopsy of perilesional tissue for **direct immunofluorescence**, as well as routine histology.
## Systemic disease
**Behçet's disease** combines recurrent oral ulcers with genital ulcers, uveitis and skin lesions. Crohn's disease, coeliac disease, SLE, reactive arthritis, cyclic neutropenia and the PFAPA syndrome in children (periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis and adenitis) are other causes.

# Investigation and management | Test, biopsy, treat.
## Investigation
Blood tests for recurrent ulcers include a full blood count, ferritin, B12, folate, and coeliac serology; inflammatory markers and an HIV test when indicated. Any persistent single ulcer is biopsied, and perilesional tissue is sent fresh for immunofluorescence when a blistering disease is suspected. Swabs for herpes simplex PCR help in acute vesicular ulcers.
## Treatment
The cause is treated: a sharp tooth smoothed, a denture adjusted, a deficiency replaced, a causative drug stopped with the prescriber. Aphthous ulcers are managed with chlorhexidine or benzydamine mouthwashes, topical corticosteroids (such as betamethasone dissolved as a mouthwash, or beclometasone spray), avoiding triggers including sodium lauryl sulphate, and colchicine or other systemic agents in severe cases under specialist care. Blistering diseases, Behçet's disease and lichen planus are managed with oral medicine, dermatology and rheumatology colleagues.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **The three-week rule** and biopsy from the edge.
- **Recurrent aphthous stomatitis:** types and blood tests.
- **Pemphigus versus pemphigoid:** level of the split, immunofluorescence.
- **Behçet's disease** and other systemic causes.
- **Drug-induced ulceration:** nicorandil.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Giving repeated topical steroids for a single persistent ulcer that is a cancer.
- Biopsying only the necrotic centre of an ulcer.
- Missing iron, B12, folate or coeliac disease behind recurrent ulcers.
!!
