@stand A vestibular schwannoma is a slow, benign tumour of the eighth nerve sheath that announces itself through the ear: one-sided hearing loss and tinnitus, with speech understanding worse than the audiogram predicts. An MRI makes the diagnosis; the choice to watch, irradiate or remove depends on size, hearing, age and growth.

# Overview | The ear is the first to know.
## Definition
A benign tumour of Schwann cells, usually arising from the vestibular division of the eighth nerve in the internal auditory canal. *Acoustic neuroma* is the traditional, inaccurate name: it is rarely acoustic and never a neuroma.
## Epidemiology
It accounts for 8 to 10% of intracranial tumours and about 80% of cerebellopontine angle tumours, usually presenting between 40 and 60. Most are sporadic and one-sided. Bilateral tumours mean *neurofibromatosis type 2*.
## Why it matters
It is the commonest serious cause of asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss, and every asymmetric loss is screened for it. Untreated large tumours compress the brainstem and cause hydrocephalus.

# Pathology | Slow growth from inside the canal.
## Origin and growth
The tumour arises near the glial-Schwann cell junction in the internal auditory canal, widening the canal before it grows into the cerebellopontine angle. Growth is slow, around 1 to 2 mm a year, and some tumours never grow at all.

## Histology
Two patterns: *Antoni A*, compact spindle cells with palisading nuclei (Verocay bodies), and *Antoni B*, loose, cystic tissue.
## Neurofibromatosis type 2
Autosomal dominant, from loss of the NF2 gene on chromosome 22. Bilateral vestibular schwannomas, often with meningiomas and other schwannomas, present in young adults.

# Clinical features | A stepwise spread.
## Eighth nerve
### Hearing loss
Progressive, one-sided sensorineural loss with poor speech discrimination, often far worse than the pure-tone thresholds suggest. About one in ten presents as sudden hearing loss.
### Tinnitus
One-sided and persistent.
### Balance
Unsteadiness rather than true vertigo, because the brain compensates as slowly as the tumour grows.
## Fifth nerve
At around 2.5 cm: reduced corneal reflex, often the earliest sign, then facial numbness.
## Seventh nerve
The sensory fibres fail first: numbness of the posterior meatal wall (*Hitselberger's sign*), loss of taste and reduced lacrimation. Motor weakness of the face comes late.
## Lower cranial nerves
Ninth and tenth: dysphagia, hoarseness and a reduced gag.
## Cerebellum and brainstem
Ataxia, nystagmus, and finally raised intracranial pressure with headache, vomiting and papilloedema from obstructed CSF flow.
!flag Urgent
Headache, vomiting, ataxia or papilloedema with a large tumour means hydrocephalus or brainstem compression: urgent neurosurgical review.
!!

# Investigations | MRI first.
## Audiometry
Asymmetric sensorineural loss, poor discrimination, *roll-over* (discrimination falls as loudness rises), absent or decaying stapedial reflex, and abnormal tone decay: the signs of a retrocochlear lesion.
## MRI with gadolinium
The gold standard: finds tumours of a few millimetres. A brightly enhancing mass centred on the internal auditory canal, with a cone into the angle (*ice-cream cone*).
!imaging | Intracanalicular and angle schwannomas, cystic change, radiotherapy effects, NF2, and the mimics: meningioma, epidermoid and arachnoid cyst.
## Auditory brainstem response
Used when MRI is not possible. A prolonged inter-aural wave V latency or I–V interval suggests a lesion, but small tumours are missed.
!differ Abnormal wave V difference
Dhingra: More than 0.2 ms.
Lee: 0.4 to 0.5 ms.
!!
## Caloric test
Reduced on the affected side in most, normal in very small tumours.
## CT
If MRI cannot be done, and to show the bony canal and approach anatomy.
## Avoid lumbar puncture
CSF protein is raised, but a puncture with a large tumour risks herniation.
## Differential diagnosis
!table Other cerebellopontine angle lesions
Lesion | Distinguishing features
Meningioma | Broad dural base, dural tail, eccentric to the canal, calcification, hyperostosis
Epidermoid | Does not enhance; bright on diffusion-weighted MRI
Facial schwannoma | Facial palsy, enlarges the geniculate or labyrinthine segment
Lipoma | Bright on T1 before contrast; suppresses with fat saturation
Haemangioma | Ground-glass erosion; palsy out of proportion to size
Arachnoid cyst | Follows CSF on all sequences
!!

# Management | Watch, radiate, or remove.
## Choosing
The decision rests on tumour size and growth, hearing in both ears, age and health, and the patient's wishes.
!differ First choice
Dhingra: Favours surgical removal.
Lee: Presents observation, radiosurgery and microsurgery as equal options, chosen by patient and tumour.
Modern practice follows Lee: many small tumours are simply watched.
!!
## Observation
For small tumours, older or unfit patients, and those with good hearing: serial MRI at 6 to 12 months, then less often. Treat if it grows.
## Stereotactic radiosurgery
A single high dose (around 12 to 13 Gy at the margin), usually for tumours under 2.5 to 3 cm. Growth is controlled in 90 to 95%. Hearing is not reliably preserved; the facial nerve risk is about 1%; malignant change is very rare, about 1 in 1000. Conventional external beam radiotherapy has no role.
## Microsurgery
!table Surgical approaches
Approach | Best for | Hearing | Main risk
Translabyrinthine | Any size | Lost | CSF leak
Retrosigmoid (suboccipital) | Medial, small or large tumours | May be preserved | Headache, cerebellar retraction
Middle fossa | Canal tumours under 1.5 cm | Preserved in 60–70% | Temporal lobe retraction, facial nerve
!!
### Translabyrinthine
Through the mastoid and labyrinth: no brain retraction and the best view of the facial nerve, but hearing is sacrificed.
### Retrosigmoid
Through the posterior fossa behind the sigmoid sinus: hearing can be preserved for tumours that spare the lateral canal; postoperative headache is commoner.
### Middle fossa
Above the temporal bone: for small intracanalicular tumours with good hearing. The facial nerve lies over the tumour and is at greater risk.
!ops
## Subtotal removal
Leaving a little tumour on the facial nerve preserves facial function; the remnant often stays stable and can be watched or irradiated.
## NF2
Bevacizumab may shrink tumours and improve hearing. An auditory brainstem implant or cochlear implant can restore some hearing.

# Complications | Of the tumour and of treatment.
- Facial palsy.
- Hearing loss.
- CSF leak and meningitis.
- Chronic headache, especially after the retrosigmoid route.
- Hydrocephalus and brainstem compression with large tumours.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Origin:** the vestibular nerve in the internal auditory canal.
- **Presenting symptoms,** and why vertigo is uncommon.
- **Order of cranial nerve involvement:** VIII, V (corneal reflex), VII (Hitselberger's sign), IX and X.
- **Retrocochlear audiology:** discrimination, roll-over, reflex decay.
- **MRI with gadolinium** as the gold standard.
- **The three surgical approaches,** with hearing outcome for each.
- **NF2:** bilateral tumours, chromosome 22.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Not requesting MRI for an asymmetric loss.
- Attributing sudden hearing loss to a virus without imaging.
- Doing a lumbar puncture with a large tumour.
- Promising hearing preservation after radiosurgery.
!!
