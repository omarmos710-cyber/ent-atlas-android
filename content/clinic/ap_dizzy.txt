@stand "Dizzy" means different things to different patients: spinning, swaying, light-headedness, or a vague sense that the world is not right. The diagnosis rarely comes from the word they use. It comes from the timing and the triggers. Seconds of vertigo on rolling over in bed is BPPV. Hours of vertigo with a full, roaring ear is Ménière's disease, and similar attacks with headache or light sensitivity are vestibular migraine. Days of continuous vertigo is vestibular neuritis, or a stroke. Chronic daily unsteadiness after an earlier vestibular upset is persistent postural-perceptual dizziness. Most dizzy patients can be diagnosed from the history and the eyes, and the one you must not miss is the cerebellar stroke.

# The history | Timing and triggers.
## What kind of dizziness?
Ask the patient to describe the sensation without using the word "dizzy". **Vertigo** is an illusion of movement, usually spinning, and points to the vestibular system. **Disequilibrium** is unsteadiness on the feet without a feeling in the head, typical of bilateral vestibular loss, neuropathy and cerebellar disease. **Presyncope** is light-headedness and a sense of impending faint, typical of orthostatic and cardiac causes. **Visual or motion sensitivity**, feeling worse in supermarkets, crowds or scrolling screens, suggests migraine, persistent postural-perceptual dizziness or an uncompensated vestibular loss. Vestibular migraine can produce any of these.

## Triggers and associations
Ask what brings the dizziness on. Rolling over in bed, looking up or bending down suggests BPPV. Standing up suggests orthostatic hypotension. Straining, lifting, coughing or loud noise suggests a third window. Sleep loss, stress, caffeine, menstruation and bright light suggest migraine.

Then ask about the ear and the brain. Hearing loss, tinnitus and fullness put the labyrinth at the centre, and fluctuating hearing suggests Ménière's disease. But *acute vertigo with sudden hearing loss* can also be an anterior inferior cerebellar artery stroke, because that artery supplies both the labyrinth and the lateral pons. Headache, double vision, dysarthria, dysphagia, numbness, weakness and falls point to a central cause. Ask about migraine past and present, motion sickness as a child, anxiety, ototoxic drugs (gentamicin), head injury, recent ear surgery and barotrauma.
!flag Central red flags in the dizzy patient
- Cannot stand or walk unaided during an attack.
- New headache, especially occipital, or neck pain (vertebral dissection).
- Diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, facial numbness, limb weakness or ataxia.
- Direction-changing, vertical or purely torsional nystagmus, or nystagmus not suppressed by fixation.
- A *normal* head impulse test in a patient with acute continuous vertigo and nystagmus.
- Vascular risk factors with acute vertigo and sudden hearing loss.
!!

# Examination | The eyes tell you where.
## Eyes and head
Look for spontaneous nystagmus with the eyes in the primary position and on gaze to each side, with and without fixation (Frenzel lenses remove fixation). **Peripheral** nystagmus is horizontal-torsional, *direction-fixed* with the fast phase away from the affected ear, increases on gaze towards the fast phase (Alexander's law) and is suppressed by fixation. **Central** nystagmus may change direction with gaze, be purely vertical or torsional, and is not suppressed by fixation.

The **head impulse test** looks at the vestibulo-ocular reflex. The patient fixes on your nose while you turn the head briskly through about 15° to each side. A corrective saccade back to the target after a turn towards one side means that canal is weak. The **test of skew**, alternate covering of each eye, reveals a vertical refixation in skew deviation, a central sign. Check smooth pursuit and saccades, which are normal in peripheral disease.
!draw vhit | The head impulse at the bedside and on video: a weak canal shows low gain and catch-up saccades.
## Positional testing
The **Dix–Hallpike test** looks for posterior canal BPPV. Turn the head 45° towards the test ear, lie the patient back quickly with the head hanging about 20° below horizontal, and watch the eyes for 30 seconds. A positive test gives upbeating, torsional nystagmus with the upper pole of the eye beating towards the lower ear. It starts after a latency of a few seconds, lasts under a minute, reverses on sitting up and fatigues with repetition. The **supine roll test**, turning the head to each side while lying flat, looks for horizontal canal BPPV, which gives horizontal geotropic or apogeotropic nystagmus.

Positional nystagmus that has no latency, does not fatigue, is purely vertical (especially downbeating), or comes without vertigo suggests a central lesion.
!fig d6-1 | The Dix–Hallpike manoeuvre.
## The rest of the examination
Otoscopy, tuning forks and a fistula test (pressure on the tragus or with a pneumatic speculum) cover the ear. A cranial nerve and cerebellar examination covers the brain: finger–nose, heel–shin, rapid alternating movements, Romberg and tandem gait. Lying and standing blood pressure looks for orthostatic hypotension, defined as a fall of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within three minutes of standing (some definitions allow up to ten); a heart rate rising by 30 or more without hypotension suggests postural tachycardia syndrome. Finally, ask the patient to walk.

# Acute continuous vertigo | Ear or brain?
## HINTS
In a patient who has been continuously vertiginous for hours or days, with nausea and spontaneous nystagmus, the question is whether this is vestibular neuritis or a stroke in the posterior circulation, which accounts for roughly one in ten such patients. **HINTS** (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew), done by a trained examiner, is more sensitive for stroke in the first 48 hours than early MRI, which misses small infarcts.

A *peripheral* pattern is reassuring only when all three agree: an *abnormal* head impulse test (a corrective saccade towards the affected side), direction-fixed horizontal nystagmus, and no skew. *Any* central feature (a normal head impulse, direction-changing nystagmus or skew) means stroke until proved otherwise. **HINTS-plus** adds new hearing loss as a central warning, because of anterior inferior cerebellar artery infarcts. Inability to stand unaided is an additional central sign.
!table HINTS in acute continuous vertigo
Test | Peripheral (neuritis) | Central (stroke)
Head impulse | Abnormal: catch-up saccade | Normal
Nystagmus | Direction-fixed, horizontal-torsional, suppressed by fixation | Direction-changing on gaze, vertical or purely torsional
Test of skew | No vertical refixation | Vertical refixation (skew)
Plus: hearing | Usually normal (labyrinthitis if lost) | New loss suggests AICA infarct
Gait | Leans but can stand | Cannot stand unaided
!!
## Management
A central pattern needs urgent MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging and the stroke team. A peripheral pattern is managed with short-term vestibular sedatives and antiemetics (prochlorperazine, cyclizine) for no more than a few days, fluids, and early mobilisation with vestibular rehabilitation exercises. A short course of oral steroids may be given early for vestibular neuritis. Sedatives slow compensation if they are continued.

# Recurrent episodic vertigo | The big three.
## BPPV
BPPV is the commonest cause of vertigo: brief spinning on head movement, often first noticed on getting out of bed, typically in older adults and after head injury or a period in bed. The posterior canal is affected in about 85 to 90%. The diagnosis is made by the Dix–Hallpike test, and the cure is a repositioning manoeuvre: the *Epley* (or Semont) manoeuvre for the posterior canal, and the *Lempert (barbecue) roll* or Gufoni manoeuvre for the horizontal canal. Recurrence is common, so patients can be taught self-treatment. Medication has no role, and atypical or refractory cases need MRI.
!fig d7-1 | The Epley manoeuvre for posterior canal BPPV, with the position of the debris in the canal at each step.
## Ménière's disease
*Definite* Ménière's disease (Bárány Society) requires two or more spontaneous episodes of vertigo, each lasting 20 minutes to 12 hours, an audiometrically documented low- to mid-frequency sensorineural loss in the affected ear on at least one occasion, and fluctuating aural symptoms (hearing, tinnitus or fullness) in that ear, not better explained by another diagnosis. An audiogram is essential, and asymmetric sensorineural loss means MRI.

Treatment aims to reduce the number and severity of attacks. It runs from acute vestibular sedatives through lifestyle measures (moderation of salt, caffeine and alcohol, though the evidence is weak), betahistine (in widespread use despite a large trial showing no benefit over placebo) and diuretics, to intratympanic steroid, intratympanic gentamicin and, rarely, surgery.

## Vestibular migraine
Vestibular migraine is the commonest cause of spontaneous recurrent vertigo, and it is underdiagnosed because many attacks come without headache. *Definite* vestibular migraine (Bárány Society and International Headache Society) requires at least five episodes of moderate or severe vestibular symptoms lasting 5 minutes to 72 hours. The patient must have current or previous migraine, and at least half the episodes must carry at least one migrainous feature: a migraine-type headache, photophobia with phonophobia, or a visual aura.

There is no confirmatory test, so the diagnosis is clinical. Treatment begins with sleep, regular meals, hydration and the identification of triggers such as caffeine and alcohol. Acute attacks are treated with prochlorperazine and, for headache, a triptan with an NSAID and an antiemetic. Frequent attacks need prophylaxis: propranolol, amitriptyline or nortriptyline, or candesartan. The trial evidence comes from migraine headache rather than vestibular migraine itself, and CGRP antibodies are emerging.
!pearl Ménière's or vestibular migraine?
The two overlap more than the textbooks admit, and some patients have both. Documented, progressive low-frequency hearing loss favours Ménière's disease. Headache, photophobia, visual aura, motion sensitivity, a long migraine history and attacks lasting more than 12 hours or only minutes favour migraine. When in doubt, a trial of migraine prophylaxis is safe and often diagnostic.
!!
## Less common episodic causes
**Superior canal dehiscence** causes vertigo with straining or loud sound, autophony and pulsatile tinnitus, with low bone conduction thresholds; it is confirmed by VEMPs and CT. **Perilymph fistula** follows stapes surgery, head injury or barotrauma. **Vestibular paroxysmia**, from a vascular loop compressing the eighth nerve, causes many brief spells a day and responds to carbamazepine. **Episodic ataxia type 2** (CACNA1A) is rare and responds to acetazolamide. **Vertebrobasilar TIAs** cause brief vertigo with other brainstem symptoms in patients with vascular risk.

# Chronic dizziness | Months of unsteadiness.
## The causes
Chronic dizziness usually has one of five causes, alone or in combination. An *uncompensated unilateral vestibular loss* after neuritis or surgery leaves symptoms on quick head turns. *Bilateral vestibular loss*, from gentamicin, meningitis, bilateral Ménière's disease or idiopathic causes, gives oscillopsia and unsteadiness in the dark. *Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness* is non-spinning dizziness and unsteadiness present on most days for more than three months, worse when upright, moving or in busy visual surroundings; it usually follows a vestibular event, migraine or anxiety, examination and tests are normal, and it is treated with vestibular rehabilitation, cognitive behavioural therapy and SSRIs. The *multisensory imbalance of the elderly* combines vestibular, visual, proprioceptive and musculoskeletal decline with drugs. *Central causes*, cerebellar degeneration, multiple sclerosis and posterior fossa tumours, are rare but must be sought.

Vestibular rehabilitation is the treatment for most chronic vestibular dizziness, and vestibular sedatives should be stopped.
## Medical causes
Many dizzy patients have no ear disease at all. Orthostatic hypotension, arrhythmias and aortic stenosis cause presyncope. Hypoglycaemia, anaemia and thyroid disease, drugs (antihypertensives, anticonvulsants, sedatives), anxiety and panic, and hyperventilation complete the list. Hyperventilation causes dizziness without nystagmus, unless the eighth nerve is demyelinated or compressed.

# Investigations | When and which.
## Tests that help
A **pure tone audiogram** is needed for every patient with ear symptoms and is required to diagnose Ménière's disease. **MRI of the internal auditory meati and brain** is needed for asymmetric hearing loss or tinnitus, central signs, atypical positional nystagmus and suspected stroke. **Vestibular tests** (videonystagmography, calorics, video head impulse test, VEMPs, rotational chair) quantify and localise vestibular loss; they are useful when the history is unclear, before ablative treatment, and in suspected bilateral loss or dehiscence. **CT temporal bones** is for suspected dehiscence or fistula. An **ECG**, lying and standing blood pressure, glucose and blood count are for presyncope.

## Children
Dizziness in children is uncommon and is usually benign. Vestibular migraine and *benign paroxysmal vertigo of childhood* (brief attacks in toddlers, a migraine precursor) are the commonest causes. Otitis media with effusion, vestibular neuritis, head injury and BPPV after trauma follow, along with the third-window effect of an enlarged vestibular aqueduct. Posterior fossa tumours, seizures and cardiac causes are the rare serious ones to exclude.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Labelling acute vertigo "labyrinthitis" without examining the eyes.
- Prescribing long-term vestibular sedatives, which delay compensation.
- Missing vestibular migraine because the patient has no headache during attacks.
- Giving betahistine for BPPV instead of doing an Epley manoeuvre.
