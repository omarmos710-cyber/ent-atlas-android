@stand A slow-growing, painless lump high in the neck that moves from side to side but not up and down, sometimes with a bruit, is a carotid body tumour until the scan says otherwise. Never needle it. Paragangliomas are often hereditary: every patient is tested for SDH mutations and screened for catecholamine secretion. In children, the vascular anomalies are sorted by one question. A haemangioma is a tumour that grows after birth and then involutes. A malformation is present at birth and grows with the child.

# Paragangliomas | Tumours of the paraganglia.
## Biology
- **Origin:** paragangliomas arise from neural-crest *paraganglia*, the chemoreceptor tissue of the carotid body, the vagal ganglia, the jugular bulb and the middle ear.
- **Histology:**
  - Nests of *chief (type I) cells* surrounded by *sustentacular (type II) cells*, in a pattern called *Zellballen* ("balls of cells").
  - The stroma is highly vascular.
- **Sites in the head and neck:**
  - Carotid body: the commonest, about half to 60%.
  - Jugular foramen (glomus jugulare).
  - Middle ear (glomus tympanicum).
  - Vagal (glomus vagale): about 5 to 10%.
- **Genetics:**
  - Up to 40% are hereditary, mostly from mutations in the *succinate dehydrogenase* genes (SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, and others).
  - Hereditary tumours appear younger, are more often multiple and bilateral (about a third), and come with phaeochromocytomas.
  - SDHB carriers carry the highest risk of malignancy.
  - *Genetic testing is offered to all patients.*
  - High-altitude hypoxia causes carotid body hyperplasia.
- **Malignancy:** about 5 to 10% of carotid tumours, more of vagal tumours. It is defined only by metastasis to nodes or distant sites, not by histology or local invasion.
- **Secretion:** catecholamine secretion occurs in only 1 to 3%. Screen everyone with *plasma free metanephrines* or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines, both to find a functioning tumour and to find a phaeochromocytoma before anaesthesia.

## Carotid body tumour
- **Presentation:** a painless, slowly enlarging mass at the *carotid bifurcation*, at the anterior border of sternomastoid at the level of the hyoid.
  - It grows about 1 mm a year (doubling in four to five years).
  - It is mobile from side to side but *not vertically* (*Fontaine sign*).
  - It transmits or has its own pulsation, and may have a bruit.
  - It may bulge into the oropharynx.
  - Cranial nerve palsies (X, XII, the sympathetic chain) are uncommon before surgery.
  - Pain and rapid growth suggest malignancy.
- **Imaging:**
  - Contrast CT or MRI shows an intensely enhancing mass *splaying the internal and external carotids* apart.
  - MRI shows a "*salt-and-pepper*" pattern: haemorrhage is the salt, flow voids are the pepper.
  - Angiography shows the *lyre sign* (the splayed carotids round a vascular blush).
  - Functional imaging (Ga-68 DOTATATE PET) looks for multiple tumours.
  - If carotid sacrifice is possible, assess the circle of Willis and collateral flow with balloon occlusion testing.
!flag No needle, no biopsy
FNA of a suspected carotid body tumour is avoided, and open or core biopsy condemned. They bleed, they rarely give a diagnosis, and imaging is characteristic.

!table Shamblin classification
Group | Relation to the carotids | Surgical meaning
I | Small, localised, easily separated from the vessels | Low risk
II | Adherent to or partly surrounding the vessels | Careful subadventitial dissection; nerve injury more likely
III | Completely encasing the carotids | May need carotid resection and grafting; highest risk of stroke and nerve injury
!!
- **Treatment options:**
  - *Surgery*: the only cure. Preferred for younger, fit patients and for growing, secreting or symptomatic tumours.
  - *Observation with serial MRI*: for older or frail patients with small, stable tumours.
  - *Radiotherapy* (conventional or stereotactic): for unresectable tumours, the unfit, and some bilateral cases. It halts growth in about 90%, but the tumour does not disappear.
- **Operation:**
  1. Transcervical approach, beginning with a level II to III dissection.
  2. Control of the common, internal and external carotids.
  3. Identification of the vagus, hypoglossal and superior laryngeal nerves and the sympathetic chain.
  4. Dissection in the periadventitial plane (the "white line"), working from below upwards.
  5. The external carotid may be ligated.
  6. A vascular surgeon should be available for reconstruction.
- **Preoperative embolisation:** some use it for large tumours. It carries a risk of stroke and nerve palsy.
- **Complications:**
  - Cranial nerve deficits (vagus, hypoglossal, sympathetic), common in large tumours. Many recover.
  - Stroke.
  - *First bite syndrome*: sharp parotid pain at the first bites of a meal, from loss of sympathetic supply to the parotid.
  - *Baroreflex failure*: labile blood pressure and tachycardia after bilateral resection. Bilateral tumours are staged, or one is observed.

## Vagal paraganglioma
- **Origin:** usually the inferior (nodose) ganglion of the vagus, high in the parapharyngeal space.
- **Imaging:** it displaces both carotids *anteromedially* without splaying them, and separates the internal jugular vein from the carotid. It may extend to the jugular foramen and intracranially.
- **Behaviour:** vagal weakness (hoarseness) is present in about a third. Malignancy is commoner than with carotid tumours.
- **Treatment:** surgery almost always sacrifices the vagus nerve, so observation or radiotherapy is preferred for small tumours with normal nerve function. Surgery is used for growing tumours and for those that have already paralysed the nerve.
## Jugular and tympanic paragangliomas
Glomus jugulare and tympanicum tumours present with *pulsatile tinnitus*, conductive hearing loss, a red mass behind the drum (the *rising sun* sign; *Brown sign*, blanching on pneumatic pressure), and lower cranial nerve palsies (*jugular foramen syndrome*). Classification: Fisch and Glasscock–Jackson. Treatment: surgery through transcanal, mastoid or infratemporal approaches; radiotherapy; or observation.

# Neural tumours | Schwannomas and neurofibromas.
## Schwannoma (neurilemmoma)
- **Pathology:**
  - A benign, encapsulated tumour of Schwann cells that grows *eccentrically* on the nerve, so the fascicles can often be separated.
  - Histology shows *Antoni A* (cellular, with palisading *Verocay bodies*) and *Antoni B* (loose) areas.
- **Sites:**
  - Most head and neck schwannomas arise in the *parapharyngeal space*. They are the second commonest tumour there after salivary tumours.
  - Usually from the vagus or the cervical sympathetic chain. Also IX, XI, XII, the cervical plexus and the brachial plexus.
- **Presentation:** a slow, painless lateral neck or parapharyngeal mass. It is mobile from side to side but not vertically, and pain on pressure may radiate along the nerve. Function of the nerve of origin is usually normal.
- **Imaging:**
  - Enhancement is less intense than in a paraganglioma, with *no flow voids*. A target sign is seen on T2.
  - *Vagal* schwannomas push the carotids forward and separate the carotid from the jugular vein.
  - *Sympathetic* schwannomas push both carotids forward without separating the vessels.
- **Treatment:**
  - Intracapsular enucleation preserving the fascicles.
  - Postoperative deficits are still common: hoarseness for vagal tumours, *Horner syndrome* and first bite syndrome for sympathetic tumours.
  - Observation is reasonable for small tumours in older patients.
## Neurofibroma
- **Pathology:** unencapsulated, and intertwined with the nerve, so the nerve has to be sacrificed to remove it.
- **Neurofibromatosis type 1:** multiple and plexiform neurofibromas.
- **Risk:** malignant change to a *malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour*. Suspect it with rapid growth or pain.

# Vascular anomalies | Tumours and malformations.
## Classification (ISSVA)
!table Haemangioma versus vascular malformation
Feature | Infantile haemangioma | Vascular malformation
Nature | True tumour (endothelial proliferation) | Error of vessel development
At birth | Absent or a faint precursor mark | Present (may not be visible)
Course | Proliferates for about a year, then involutes over years | Grows in proportion with the child; never involutes; growth spurts at puberty, pregnancy, infection, trauma
Marker | GLUT-1 positive | GLUT-1 negative
Treatment | Observation; propranolol | Laser, sclerotherapy, embolisation, surgery according to type
!!
## Infantile haemangioma
The commonest tumour of infancy, about 4 to 5% of infants. Commoner in girls, premature and low-birth-weight babies.
- **Course:**
  - Appears in the first weeks.
  - *Proliferates* rapidly to about 9 to 12 months.
  - Then *involutes* slowly, largely by five to ten years, often leaving fibrofatty tissue and telangiectasia.
- **Forms:** superficial (bright red "strawberry"), deep (bluish), or mixed. Focal, multifocal or segmental.
- **Congenital haemangiomas:** fully grown at birth. They involute rapidly (RICH), partially (PICH) or not at all (NICH).
- **Associations:**
  - *Beard distribution*: subglottic haemangioma in about two-thirds. Biphasic stridor appears at about two to three months.
  - *Segmental facial lesions*: PHACES syndrome (posterior fossa anomalies, haemangioma, arterial anomalies, cardiac and aortic anomalies, eye anomalies, sternal clefts). Assess with MRI and echocardiography.
  - *Five or more skin lesions*: look for hepatic haemangiomas.
  - The *Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon* (platelet trapping and coagulopathy) belongs to kaposiform haemangioendothelioma and tufted angioma, not to common haemangiomas.
- **When to treat:**
  - The airway.
  - Periocular lesions that threaten vision (amblyopia).
  - Ulceration and bleeding.
  - Lip, nose and ear lesions that will deform.
  - Large facial lesions.
  - High-output heart failure.
- **Treatment:**
  - *Propranolol* (about 2 mg/kg/day, monitoring for hypoglycaemia, bradycardia and bronchospasm) is first line.
  - Topical timolol for small superficial lesions.
  - Steroids are now second line.
  - Pulsed-dye laser for ulceration and residual telangiectasia.
  - Surgery for residual deformity, or for selected subglottic and localised lesions.
## Vascular malformations
- **Capillary malformation (port-wine stain):**
  - A flat pink to purple patch present at birth, often in a trigeminal distribution. It darkens and thickens ("cobblestones") with age.
  - V1 involvement raises the question of *Sturge–Weber syndrome* (leptomeningeal angiomatosis, seizures, glaucoma).
  - Treatment: pulsed-dye laser.
- **Venous malformation:**
  - A soft, bluish, compressible mass that swells when dependent or with Valsalva.
  - *Phleboliths* on imaging. Pain from thrombosis. D-dimer raised in large lesions.
  - Treatment: sclerotherapy (ethanol, sodium tetradecyl sulfate, bleomycin), Nd:YAG laser, and excision of localised lesions. Compression and head elevation help symptoms.
- **Lymphatic malformation:** macrocystic or microcystic, as above under congenital masses.
- **Arteriovenous malformation:**
  - A high-flow lesion with a *nidus*: warm, pulsatile, with a thrill and bruit.
  - Quiescent in childhood, then expands at puberty, in pregnancy or after trauma. Can cause ulceration, bleeding, bone destruction and heart failure.
  - *Schobinger staging*: quiescence, expansion, destruction, decompensation.
  - Treatment:
    - Superselective embolisation followed within days by complete surgical excision of the nidus, with reconstruction.
    - *Never ligate the feeding vessels proximally*: it does not cure, it recruits collaterals and it blocks future embolisation.
- **Arteriovenous fistula:** usually traumatic, with one or two connections. Endovascular closure or surgery.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Carotid body tumour:**
  - Signs: Fontaine sign and a bruit.
  - Imaging: splaying and the lyre sign; salt-and-pepper MRI.
  - No biopsy.
  - Metanephrines and SDH genetics.
  - Shamblin groups.
  - Complications, including first bite syndrome and baroreflex failure.
- **Vagal paraganglioma versus schwannoma:** vessel displacement and flow voids.
- **Haemangioma versus malformation:** natural history and GLUT-1; propranolol; subglottic haemangioma with beard-distribution lesions.
- **AVM:** embolise and excise; never ligate proximally.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Needling a pulsatile mass at the bifurcation.
- Operating on a functioning paraganglioma, or on a patient with an undiagnosed phaeochromocytoma, without alpha blockade.
- Resecting both carotid body tumours at one sitting.
- Calling a venous malformation a "haemangioma" and waiting for it to involute.
!!
