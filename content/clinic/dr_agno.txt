@stand Silver nitrate applied on a stick cauterises tissue chemically. It is the standard way to treat a visible bleeding point or prominent vessels on the anterior septum in epistaxis, and it shrinks granulation tissue in ear cavities, at tracheostomy stomas and in the nose. It needs a dry field to work, and cauterising both sides of the septum at the same level risks perforation.

# At a glance | The agent on one page.
## Summary
!table Silver nitrate
 | Summary
Preparations | Applicator sticks with a tip of silver nitrate (commonly 75% with potassium nitrate)
How it works | Silver ions denature proteins and form a coagulum (eschar) on contact with moisture
ENT uses | Anterior epistaxis from a visible vessel; prominent vessels in recurrent childhood epistaxis; granulation tissue in mastoid cavities, ear canals, tracheostomy stomas and wounds; small bleeding points
Technique | Anaesthetise and decongest; dry the area; touch the stick to the vessel and roll it around the bleeding point, starting at the periphery and working inward; a few seconds of contact only
Avoid | Cauterising opposing areas of both sides of the septum at one sitting; contact with skin and eyes
!!

# Using it in ENT | Practice.
## Epistaxis
After topical co-phenylcaine or lidocaine with a decongestant, identify the vessel with a headlight or endoscope and suction. Cauterise the bleeding point; if both sides need treatment, wait 4 to 6 weeks between sides. Afterwards, an antiseptic cream (such as Naseptin, if no nut allergy) or petroleum jelly for 1 to 2 weeks helps the eschar heal.
## Granulation tissue
Apply to granulations in a dry cavity or stoma, then treat the cause (infection, foreign body, a tube rubbing).

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
Pain and stinging (adequate anaesthesia); black staining of skin; chemical burns of the alar skin from run-off; septal perforation from bilateral or excessive cautery; failure if the field is wet.
