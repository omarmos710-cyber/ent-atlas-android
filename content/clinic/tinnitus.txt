@stand Tinnitus is a sound with no outside source, and it is a symptom rather than a disease. Two kinds need imaging: tinnitus in one ear, and tinnitus that pulses with the heartbeat, because a tumour or a vascular lesion may lie behind them. Most other tinnitus needs good hearing care, explanation and sound therapy, not drugs.

# Overview | A sound no one else can hear.
## Definition
The perception of sound, ringing, hissing, buzzing or roaring, in the absence of an external source.
## Epidemiology
Between 10 and 15% of adults have tinnitus, but only about one in thirty find it at least moderately annoying and about one in two hundred are severely affected. Most have some hearing loss, often age-related or noise-induced. About half of those with troublesome tinnitus also have reduced tolerance of sound.
## Why it matters
It can be the first symptom of a vestibular schwannoma, a glomus tumour or a dural arteriovenous fistula. And severe tinnitus disrupts sleep, concentration and mood.

# Classification | Who can hear it?
## Subjective tinnitus
Heard only by the patient: by far the commonest.
## Objective tinnitus
Can be heard by the examiner with a stethoscope or a Toynbee tube.
- **Vascular:** pulsatile, from arterial or venous flow.
- **Muscular:** clicking from palatal myoclonus, or from spasm of the stapedius or tensor tympani.
- **Patulous Eustachian tube:** a blowing sound with breathing.
- **Temporomandibular joint:** clicks with jaw movement.
## A normal phenomenon
*Sudden brief unilateral tapering tinnitus*, a few seconds of ringing in one ear with fullness, happens to most people and needs no investigation.

# Causes | Ear, body and blood vessels.
## Otological
Wax, middle ear fluid, otitis media, otosclerosis, Ménière's disease, presbycusis, noise-induced hearing loss, sudden hearing loss, ototoxicity and vestibular schwannoma.
## General
Anaemia, hypertension, thyroid disease, hyperlipidaemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, head injury and multiple sclerosis.
## Drugs
Aspirin and other salicylates, quinine, aminoglycosides, loop diuretics and some chemotherapy. Most drug-induced tinnitus reverses when the drug is stopped.
## Pulsatile tinnitus

Tumour | Glomus tympanicum or jugulare | other vascular tumours
Shunt | Dural arteriovenous fistula | arteriovenous malformation
High output | Anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy | conductive hearing loss makes body sounds louder

## How it arises
Reduced input from a damaged cochlea leads to reorganisation and increased spontaneous activity in the central auditory pathways. Everyone has some random activity in these pathways: in a silent room most people soon hear a faint hum or ringing. Normally, subconscious filters ignore it. When the brain labels the sound as a threat, the limbic and autonomic systems respond, attention is drawn to it, and a vicious circle of awareness, anxiety and amplification follows. This model explains why the loudness of tinnitus predicts distress so poorly, and why treatment aims at the reaction rather than the sound.
## Disorders of sound tolerance
**Hyperacusis** is intolerance of everyday sounds that others find comfortable, often with tinnitus. **Misophonia** is a strong emotional reaction to specific sounds, often mouth sounds such as chewing, regardless of loudness. **Phonophobia** is fear of particular sounds, and **recruitment** is the abnormally rapid growth of loudness in a cochlear hearing loss. Facial palsy, by removing the stapedial reflex, can cause hyperacusis. Sound tolerance is treated with explanation, graded sound exposure and cognitive behavioural therapy; ear plugs worn constantly make it worse.

# Assessment | Character, side, and mood.
## History
Is it in one ear or both? Is it pulsatile? Is there hearing loss, vertigo or fullness? Noise exposure and drugs? How much does it disturb sleep, work and mood?
!flag Ask about mood
Severe tinnitus is linked with anxiety and depression. Ask directly how the patient is coping, including any thoughts of self-harm, and arrange support where needed.
!!
## Examination
Otoscopy under the microscope (a red mass behind the drum, a blue drum, wax), tuning forks, cranial nerves, and auscultation over the ear, orbit and neck. Pressing on the carotid stops arterial tinnitus; pressing on the jugular vein or turning the head stops a venous hum. A rhythm with breathing points to a patulous tube; clicks with palatal movement mean myoclonus.
## Investigations
### Audiometry
Pure-tone and speech audiometry, tympanometry, and tinnitus matching for pitch and loudness. Loudness and pitch do not predict how distressing it is.
### Imaging
- **One-sided tinnitus or asymmetric hearing:** MRI of the internal auditory canals.
- **Pulsatile tinnitus:** CT angiography, MR angiography and venography; formal angiography if a dural fistula is suspected.
- **Palatal myoclonus:** MRI of the posterior fossa.
!imaging | A persistent stapedial artery, glomus tympanicum and vestibular schwannoma.
### Blood tests
As suggested by the history: full blood count, thyroid function, lipids, glucose, B12.
### Long-duration tympanometry
Shows rhythmic compliance changes in myoclonus or a patulous tube.

# Management | Treat the cause, then the reaction.
## Treat the cause
Remove wax, treat otitis media, operate on otosclerosis, stop ototoxic drugs where possible, and treat anaemia or thyroid disease.
## Hearing aids
The first step whenever there is hearing loss: restoring external sound reduces the contrast with the tinnitus.
!ops
## Explanation and counselling
Explaining that tinnitus is common, real and not a sign of a brain tumour (once excluded) relieves much of the distress.
## Sound therapy
### Masking
Background sound, a bedside sound generator or a wearable masker, alone or combined with a hearing aid.
### Tinnitus retraining therapy
Directive counselling plus low-level broadband sound, aiming at habituation: the tinnitus is still there but no longer noticed or disturbing.
!differ Retraining sound exposure
Dhingra: At least 8 hours a day for 18 to 24 months.
Lee: Up to 16 hours a day for about a year.
!!
## Psychological therapy
Cognitive behavioural therapy has the strongest evidence for reducing distress; relaxation and biofeedback also help. Treat anxiety, depression and insomnia.
## Specific objective causes
- **Dural arteriovenous fistula:** embolisation, surgery or radiosurgery; fistulas with cortical venous drainage can bleed.
- **Palatal myoclonus:** muscle relaxants or botulinum toxin.
- **Stapedial or tensor spasm:** muscle relaxants, or division of the tendon.
- **Patulous tube:** see the Eustachian tube topic.
## Treatments without proven benefit
No drug reliably suppresses tinnitus, and betahistine is not recommended for it. Transcranial magnetic stimulation and implanted stimulators remain investigational. A cochlear implant often reduces tinnitus in patients who qualify for it because of their hearing loss.
## Stepped care
Severity is measured with a validated questionnaire such as the **Tinnitus Handicap Inventory** or the Tinnitus Functional Index. Most patients need only explanation, hearing care and self-help; those with greater distress move to audiologist-led tinnitus support and sound therapy, and then to cognitive behavioural therapy delivered by a psychologist, with access to mental health services for those at risk.

# Prognosis | Most get better with time.
About a quarter of patients find their tinnitus nearly resolves, half improve, and a quarter stay the same. Habituation is the realistic goal.
!pearl Pearl
Most people adapt to tinnitus. What a clinician says in the first consultation strongly shapes how well they do.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Telling a patient that nothing can be done.
- Missing pulsatile tinnitus because nobody asked.
- Forgetting to image one-sided tinnitus.
- Ignoring the patient's mood.
