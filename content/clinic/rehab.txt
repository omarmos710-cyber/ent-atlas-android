@stand Hearing rehabilitation means matching the device to the loss. A hearing aid helps whatever amplification can help; a bone-conduction device helps when the canal or middle ear is in the way; a cochlear implant takes over when the cochlea can no longer do the job; and a brainstem implant is kept for the patient who has no cochlear nerve.

# Overview | The right device for the loss.
!table Which device?
Situation | Consider
Sensorineural loss with useful speech understanding | Air-conduction hearing aid
Chronic discharge, canal atresia | Bone-anchored device
Single-sided deafness | CROS aid, bone-anchored device, cochlear implant
Moderate to severe loss, cannot tolerate an aid | Middle ear implant
Severe to profound loss with poor speech understanding | Cochlear implant
No cochlear nerve, NF2 | Auditory brainstem implant
!!
## Beyond devices
Assistive listening devices (loop systems, FM and remote microphones), alerting devices, speech reading, auditory training and speech conservation.
## Two ears are better than one
Bilateral fitting gives the *head shadow* effect, *binaural summation* (sound seems about 3 dB louder) and *squelch* (better hearing in noise), and restores sound localisation.

# Hearing aids | Amplification.
## Components
A microphone, amplifier and receiver (speaker), powered by a battery, with digital processing that shapes gain by frequency and compresses loud sounds.
## Styles
!fig d20-1 | Types of hearing aid: (A) body-worn, (B) behind-the-ear, (C) spectacle and (D) in-the-ear.
- **Body-worn:** high power; now rare.
- **Behind-the-ear:** the most versatile, from mild to profound loss, and the choice for children because the mould can be changed as the ear grows.
- **Receiver-in-canal and open fit:** for high-frequency loss with good low tones; avoid the occlusion effect.
- **In-the-ear, in-the-canal, completely-in-canal:** cosmetic, for mild to moderate loss in adults with good dexterity.
## Who does well
Speech discrimination of at least about 50%, and a wide *dynamic range* between the threshold and the level of discomfort.
## Problems
### Recruitment
Abnormal growth of loudness in a cochlear loss narrows the dynamic range: soft sounds are missed but loud sounds are intolerable. Compression circuits help.
### Occlusion effect
A blocked canal makes the patient's own voice boom. Venting reduces it, but venting also loses low-frequency gain.
### Feedback
Whistling from sound leaking back to the microphone; better moulds and feedback cancellation help.

# Bone-conduction devices | Around the canal and middle ear.
## How they work
Sound vibrates the skull directly and reaches both cochleas, bypassing the canal and middle ear.
## Types
Conventional bone-conduction aids on a headband or spectacles; *percutaneous* bone-anchored devices fixed to an osseointegrated titanium implant through the skin; and *transcutaneous* (magnetic) devices under intact skin.
!fig d20-2 d20-3 | First, a bone-anchored device: titanium fixture, abutment and sound processor. Second, the device worn.
## Indications
- Chronic discharging ears that cannot tolerate an aid.
- Canal atresia and microtia.
- Conductive or mixed loss where surgery is unsuitable.
- Single-sided deafness with a good other ear (about 20 dB or better), sending sound across the skull.
## Practical points
Osseointegration takes about three months, longer in children. Complications include failed integration and skin reactions around the abutment. A bone-anchored device does not restore localisation.

## CROS and BiCROS aids
A microphone on the deaf side sends sound wirelessly to the better ear; BiCROS adds amplification for a better ear that also has some loss.

# Middle ear implants | Driving the ossicles directly.
A transducer fixed to an ossicle or the round window, driven electromagnetically or piezoelectrically. For adults with moderate to severe sensorineural or mixed loss who are unhappy with conventional aids, and some conductive losses. The canal is left open.

# Cochlear implants | Bypassing the cochlea.
## Principle
An external microphone and processor convert sound into electrical patterns, sent across the skin to an internal receiver, which stimulates the spiral ganglion cells through an electrode array in the scala tympani. The cochlea's place coding is reproduced: basal electrodes for high pitches, apical for low.
!fig d20-9 | Principle of the cochlear implant.
!anat s70_5 | Cross-section of the cochlea: the electrode lies in the scala tympani, next to the spiral ganglion.
## Candidates
### Adults
Severe to profound bilateral sensorineural loss with limited benefit from well-fitted aids. Lee gives moderate to profound loss with an aided sentence score of 50% or less in the ear to be implanted and 60% or less best-aided.
### Children
From about twelve months, with severe to profound loss and little benefit after a three to six month trial of aids. The trial is waived after meningitis, when ossification threatens.
!differ Age of implantation
Dhingra: From twelve months.
Lee: Earlier implantation is safe and may help language.
!!
### Outcomes
Duration of deafness and pre-operative speech scores explain most of the variation. Postlingually deafened adults often achieve high sentence scores; children implanted early commonly join mainstream schools; prelingually deaf adults gain mostly sound awareness, because the window for language (birth to about two years) has passed.
## Assessment
Medical review; audiology with an aid trial; MRI to show the cochlear nerve and a fluid-filled cochlea; HRCT for bony anatomy, malformations, a narrow internal auditory canal (under 3 mm) and surgical hazards; psychological and speech assessment.
!imaging | A cochlear implant in place, labyrinthitis ossificans, a gusher ear and iatrogenic fenestration.
## Contraindications
Complete cochlear or labyrinthine aplasia (Michel), complete ossification, and an absent cochlear nerve. Retrocochlear disease is a relative contraindication.
## The operation
Cortical mastoidectomy, posterior tympanotomy through the facial recess, entry into the scala tympani through the round window or a cochleostomy, slow insertion of the array, sealing, and testing of the device. *Soft surgery* (a flexible array, the round window route, slow insertion, no suction of perilymph, and steroids) preserves residual hearing. In an ossified cochlea, the basal turn is drilled out or a split array used.
!differ Route into the cochlea
Dhingra: Describes a cochleostomy.
Lee: Prefers the round window.
!!
!ops
## After surgery
Activation at about two to four weeks, then repeated *mapping* of each electrode and intensive auditory therapy; sound quality improves over three to six months.
## Vaccination
Implant recipients are vaccinated against pneumococcus (PCV13, then PPSV23 at least eight weeks later), because meningitis is commoner in implanted children, especially with malformations or a CSF leak.
## Complications
- Flap breakdown and device extrusion.
- Device failure, about 4% over a lifetime; revision through the same track.
- Facial nerve injury (under 1%) and facial stimulation, managed by switching off electrodes.
- Taste change, vertigo, tinnitus.
- CSF gusher, especially with malformations.
- Meningitis.
!pearl Pearls
- Bilateral implants restore head shadow, summation and squelch, and localisation.
- Inner ear malformations raise the risk of an anomalous facial nerve, gusher and meningitis.
- Auditory neuropathy can do well with an implant.
- Acute otitis media in an implanted child is treated promptly, intravenously if severe.
!!

# Auditory brainstem implant | When there is no nerve.
An electrode placed on the cochlear nucleus, through the lateral recess of the fourth ventricle, for patients without a usable cochlear nerve: neurofibromatosis type 2, nerve aplasia, or complete cochlear ossification. Results are more modest than with cochlear implants.
!update The implant family today (Oxford Textbook)
Implantable devices act at different levels of the pathway. **Bone-conduction hearing devices** bypass the outer and middle ear. *Skin-drive* (passive transcutaneous) devices, such as Baha Attract and the adhesive Adhear, lose energy through the skin and are being superseded. *Direct-drive* devices are either *percutaneous*, with an abutment through the skin (Baha Connect, Ponto), or *active transcutaneous*, with the vibrator implanted under intact skin (Bonebridge, Osia). UK criteria require a conductive or mixed loss, or single-sided deafness, with bone conduction in the fitting range and stable (no more than 15 dB change at two or more frequencies over two years), failure or unsuitability of a conventional or CROS aid after at least four weeks, and benefit from a trial on a headband for at least 14 days. **Active middle ear implants**, most often the Vibrant Soundbridge, drive the incus, stapes or round window with a floating mass transducer, and suit patients who cannot wear an aid but have a cochlea worth amplifying.

**Cochlear implantation** now aims to preserve the cochlea and any residual hearing: a round window or cochleostomy approach into the scala tympani, slow insertion over minutes to avoid basilar membrane rupture, and steroids. Children with profound bilateral loss are implanted on both sides, simultaneously, ideally between 9 and 12 months. In aural atresia, a headband bone-conduction aid is started by about six months, with a definitive implant at about four to five years. **Auditory brainstem implants** stimulate the cochlear nucleus when a cochlear implant cannot work: neurofibromatosis type 2, cochlear or nerve aplasia, and severe cochlear ossification after meningitis or fracture. In adults, treating hearing loss may slow cognitive decline.

!pit
- Fitting an aid to an asymmetric loss without excluding a retrocochlear cause.
- Delaying implantation after meningitis.
