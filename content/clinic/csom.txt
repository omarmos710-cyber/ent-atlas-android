@stand Chronic suppurative otitis media of the mucosal, or safe, type is a wet ear discharging through a hole that will not heal. It rarely threatens life, but it wears down hearing and patience. The aim is a dry, safe ear: clean it properly, treat it topically for long enough, prove there is no cholesteatoma, and then decide whether repair is worth it.

# Overview | A persistent hole and a discharging ear.
## Definition
Chronic suppurative otitis media is long-standing infection of part or all of the middle ear cleft, with ear discharge and a permanent perforation. It is classically divided into two types: the *tubotympanic* or *safe* type, which involves the mucosa and the anteroinferior cleft, and the *atticoantral* or *unsafe* type, which involves bone and usually cholesteatoma. This chapter covers the safe, mucosal type.
!fig d11-7 | Classification of chronic otitis media into tubotympanic (mucosal) and atticoantral (squamous) types.
## Clinical stages
### Active mucosal disease
Discharge through a pars tensa perforation.
### Inactive mucosal disease
A dry, permanent perforation that may become active with a cold or water.
### Healed chronic otitis media
An atrophic, healed drum, often with tympanosclerosis and some conductive loss.
## Why it matters
It is a common cause of preventable hearing loss worldwide, especially where crowding, poverty and limited access to care allow acute infections to go untreated.

# Pathogenesis | Why the hole does not heal and the ear keeps running.
## Origins
Most cases follow repeated acute otitis media in childhood, or a perforation from acute necrotising otitis or trauma. Chronic effusion and Eustachian tube dysfunction keep the mucosa inflamed.
## The permanent perforation
Once the squamous epithelium of the drum grows round the edge of the hole and meets the mucosa on the inside, the perforation behaves like a lined fistula and will not close on its own.
!fig d11-5 | A permanent perforation of the pars tensa: the epidermal and mucosal layers meet across its edge, so it behaves like a non-healing fistula.
## Why the discharge recurs
### Reinfection through the tube
Infection ascends from the nose, sinuses, adenoids and tonsils, especially in children, and nose blowing pushes secretions into the middle ear.
### Reinfection through the canal
Water entering through the perforation during bathing or swimming.
### Biofilms
Bacteria, particularly *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, form biofilms on the mucosa that shield them from antibiotics and immune cells, explaining the relapses after apparently successful treatment.
## Organisms
*Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Proteus*, *Escherichia coli* and *Staphylococcus aureus* are the commonest aerobes, often with anaerobes such as *Bacteroides*. Fungi may follow repeated antibiotic drops.

# Clinical features | The safe ear.
## Symptoms
### Discharge
Profuse, mucoid or mucopurulent, without a foul smell, and intermittent: it flares with colds and with water in the ear and dries between episodes.
### Hearing loss
Conductive, of variable degree, and rarely over 50 dB. Some patients hear better when the ear is wet, because the discharge shields the round window and restores a phase difference between the windows.
## Signs
### The perforation
Central, with a rim of drum all round it. It may lie anterior, posterior or inferior to the handle of malleus, or be subtotal. A large perforation may expose the ossicles and the promontory.
!fig d11-10 d11-11 | First, the types of perforation seen in the pars tensa. Second, a large central perforation.
### The middle ear mucosa
Normal and pale when inactive, red and oedematous when active. A pale polyp may fill the perforation.
!fig d11-9 | An aural polyp in the canal, and how a polyp from the promontory can present through the perforation.
!pearl Never avulse a polyp
A polyp may arise from the stapes, the facial nerve or the lateral semicircular canal. Pulling it out risks facial palsy or a dead ear; remove it under the microscope or at surgery, and send it for histology.
!!
## Safe or unsafe?
!table Mucosal versus squamous chronic otitis media
 | Mucosal (safe) | Squamous (unsafe)
Discharge | Profuse, mucoid, not foul | Scanty, foul-smelling
Perforation | Central | Attic or posterosuperior marginal
Polyp | Pale | Red, fleshy
Cholesteatoma | Absent | Present
Complications | Rare | More common
Hearing | Mild to moderate conductive | Conductive or mixed

# Investigations | Look, test, and image when needed.
## Examination under the microscope
Every chronic ear should be cleaned and examined with the microscope. An apparently dry ear can hide discharge, and rarely cholesteatoma coexists with a central perforation.
!fig d11-12 | Examination of the ear under the operating microscope.
## Swab for culture
Guides the choice of drops when the ear does not respond.
## Tuning forks and audiogram
Measure the conductive loss and bone conduction. An air-bone gap over about 40 dB suggests ossicular discontinuity, often erosion of the long process of the incus.
## Imaging
CT is not routine for mucosal disease, where it shows no bone erosion. It is used when cholesteatoma or a complication is suspected, and before revision surgery. MRI is reserved for intracranial concerns.
## Biopsy
For granulation that persists despite treatment, to exclude tuberculosis and malignancy.
## Differential diagnosis
- **Cholesteatoma:** scanty foul discharge, attic or marginal defect, white flakes.
- **Tuberculous otitis media:** painless thin discharge, pale granulation, multiple perforations, early facial palsy.
- **Granular myringitis:** granulation on the drum itself, without a perforation.
- **Carcinoma of the temporal bone:** pain, bleeding, and granulation that does not settle.

# Management | Dry the ear, then decide on repair.
## Aural toilet
Drops cannot work through pus. Clean the ear under the microscope by suction, dry mopping or gentle saline irrigation; never use forceful syringing.
## Topical treatment
### Drops
Quinolone drops are preferred when the drum is perforated. Continue for four to six weeks after debridement when the ear is persistently active.
### Technique
Tragal pumping after instilling the drops pushes them through the perforation into the middle ear.
### Acidifying drops
Acetic acid 1.5% helps against *Pseudomonas*.
!differ Ototoxic drops
Dhingra: Advises avoiding aminoglycoside and polymyxin drops with a perforation.
Lee: Lists neomycin and polymyxin preparations among the options.
Quinolone drops are the safer choice with an open middle ear.
!!
## Systemic antibiotics
Mainly for acute flares, chosen by culture.
## Treating the source
Treat infected tonsils, adenoids and sinuses, and allergy; avoid hard nose blowing.
## Keep water out
Cotton wool with petroleum jelly, or a moulded plug, during bathing; no swimming while the ear is active.
## Surgery
!fig d11-14 | A management pathway for chronic suppurative otitis media, from medical treatment to tympanoplasty and mastoid surgery.
### Tympanoplasty
Once the ear has been dry for some weeks, repair of the perforation (myringoplasty), with ossicular reconstruction if needed, restores a closed, protected middle ear. Success is 60 to 90%, higher when air passes through the perforation on Valsalva, a sign of a working tube.
### Mastoidectomy
For discharge that persists despite proper medical treatment. Cortical mastoidectomy may be combined with tympanoplasty; on its own it does not improve the rate of drum closure.
### Hearing aids
For patients unfit for or unwilling to have surgery, ideally once the ear is dry.
!pearl Pearls
- The other ear predicts how well the Eustachian tube works.
- Think of tuberculosis when an ear fails to respond, with pale granulation, multiple holes and facial weakness.
- No treatment of the Eustachian tube has proven benefit.
!!

# Complications | Uncommon in the safe ear, but real.
## Ossicular erosion
The long process of the incus is a vascular watershed and erodes first, giving a larger conductive loss.
## Sensorineural hearing loss
Toxins crossing the round window can damage the basal turn of the cochlea, producing a high-frequency loss.
## Tympanosclerosis
Chalky hyaline plaques in the drum and middle ear, which may fix the ossicles.
## Others
Rarely, mastoiditis, facial palsy, labyrinthitis or intracranial infection in an acute flare.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Prescribing drops into an ear full of pus.
- Using aminoglycoside drops through a perforation without need.
- Calling a chronic ear safe without examining it under the microscope.
- Missing tuberculosis or malignancy in granulation that will not settle.
!!
