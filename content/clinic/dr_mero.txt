@stand Meropenem is a carbapenem: the broadest beta-lactam in routine use, active against almost all Gram-negative bacilli including ESBL producers and Pseudomonas, most Gram-positives apart from MRSA, and anaerobes. In ENT it is reserved for resistant organisms and the critically ill: severe deep neck and mediastinal sepsis, hospital-acquired infection after major head and neck surgery, intracranial infection with resistant organisms, and necrotising otitis externa that has failed first-line treatment.

# At a glance | The drug on one page.
## Summary
!table Meropenem
 | Summary
Class | Carbapenem (beta-lactam)
How it works | Cell wall synthesis inhibitor, stable to almost all beta-lactamases including ESBL and AmpC
Spectrum | Enterobacterales including ESBL producers, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter (some), streptococci, methicillin-sensitive S. aureus, anaerobes; not MRSA, not Enterococcus faecium, not Stenotrophomonas, not carbapenemase producers
ENT uses | Severe or hospital-acquired head and neck sepsis; descending mediastinitis; ESBL infection; meningitis and brain abscess with resistant Gram-negatives; failed treatment of skull base osteomyelitis
Adult dose | 1 g every 8 hours intravenously; 2 g every 8 hours for meningitis and severe infection
Child dose | 10 to 20 mg/kg every 8 hours; 40 mg/kg every 8 hours for meningitis (maximum 2 g per dose)
Avoid | Beta-lactam anaphylaxis (cross-reactivity with penicillin is low, about 1%)
Pregnancy | Use if needed
!!

# How it works | Mechanism and handling.
## Mechanism
Carbapenems bind multiple penicillin-binding proteins and resist hydrolysis by nearly all serine beta-lactamases. Resistance comes from **carbapenemases** (KPC, OXA-48, NDM) and porin loss, and it spreads; every unnecessary carbapenem prescription adds to the problem, which is why most hospitals restrict them to microbiology approval.
## Handling
Intravenous; renally excreted, so reduce the dose when the creatinine clearance is below 50 ml/min. It crosses the inflamed meninges and has a lower seizure risk than imipenem.

# Using it in ENT | Indications and doses.
## Severe neck and mediastinal sepsis
**Descending necrotising mediastinitis** from a dental, pharyngeal or deep neck source carries a high mortality: secure the airway, drain the neck and mediastinum (with thoracic surgeons), and give broad cover such as meropenem with vancomycin or linezolid, narrowed by cultures.
## Resistant and hospital organisms
After major resection and free-flap reconstruction, fistula and wound infection may involve ESBL Enterobacterales or resistant Pseudomonas: meropenem is used when cultures demand it, not as a reflex.

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
- Diarrhoea, **C. difficile** and candidiasis; selection of resistant organisms.
- Seizures (less than imipenem), particularly in renal failure or CNS disease.
- Hypersensitivity, raised liver enzymes, thrombocytopenia.
## Interactions
**Sodium valproate**: carbapenems lower valproate levels profoundly within a day, and seizures follow; the combination should be avoided.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- What carbapenems cover and what they do not (**MRSA**, E. faecium, Stenotrophomonas).
- The **valproate** interaction.
- Antimicrobial stewardship: microbiology advice, cultures first, review at 48 to 72 hours.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Using meropenem first-line for community-acquired head and neck infection.
- Forgetting MRSA cover in the septic post-operative patient.
- Giving it to a patient taking valproate.
!!
