@stand Gentamicin is an aminoglycoside: rapidly bactericidal against Gram-negative bacilli, cheap, and synergistic with beta-lactams against streptococci and enterococci. It is also the most important cause of drug-induced deafness and vestibular failure worldwide. ENT surgeons meet it as a cause of ototoxicity, as a drug they must monitor in their own patients, as an ear drop, and as an intratympanic ablative treatment for Ménière's disease.

# At a glance | The drug on one page.
## Summary
!table Gentamicin (systemic)
 | Summary
Class | Aminoglycoside (others: amikacin, tobramycin, streptomycin, neomycin)
How it works | Binds the 30S ribosomal subunit, causing misreading of mRNA; concentration-dependent, bactericidal, with a post-antibiotic effect
Spectrum | Enterobacterales, Pseudomonas, S. aureus (synergy); synergy with penicillins against streptococci and enterococci; no anaerobic activity; poor in acidic, anaerobic pus
ENT uses | Gram-negative sepsis; surgical prophylaxis in penicillin allergy (single dose with clindamycin); topical ear drops; intratympanic chemical labyrinthectomy
Adult dose | Once-daily dosing 5 to 7 mg/kg (based on ideal or adjusted body weight) with levels by a nomogram (for example the Hartford nomogram); divided dosing for endocarditis synergy (1 mg/kg every 12 hours)
Child dose | 7 mg/kg once daily (neonates on specific regimens), guided by levels
Avoid | Myasthenia gravis; take care with renal impairment, pre-existing hearing loss, family history of aminoglycoside deafness, and other ototoxic or nephrotoxic drugs
Pregnancy | Avoid (fetal ototoxicity)

# How it works | Mechanism and handling.
## Mechanism
Uptake into bacteria needs oxygen-dependent transport, so aminoglycosides do not work against anaerobes or in abscesses. Once-daily dosing exploits **concentration-dependent killing** and the post-antibiotic effect while giving the kidney and ear a drug-free interval.
## Handling
Not absorbed orally. Distributes in extracellular fluid, excreted unchanged by glomerular filtration; it accumulates in renal tubular cells and in the **perilymph and endolymph**, where it remains long after plasma levels fall.

# Ototoxicity | What the ENT surgeon must know.
## How it damages the ear
Aminoglycosides enter hair cells through the mechanotransduction channels, generate reactive oxygen species and trigger apoptosis. **Gentamicin and streptomycin are mainly vestibulotoxic**; amikacin, kanamycin, neomycin and tobramycin are mainly cochleotoxic. Cochlear damage begins in the **outer hair cells of the basal turn**, so high frequencies go first. Damage is usually **permanent** and can progress for weeks after the drug stops.
## Who is at risk
- Duration over 5 to 7 days, high cumulative dose, high troughs, renal impairment and older age.
- Concurrent **loop diuretics**, vancomycin, cisplatin.
- **Mitochondrial 12S rRNA mutations** (m.1555A>G, m.1494C>T) cause profound deafness after even a single dose; in the UK, neonates are now tested rapidly for m.1555A>G before gentamicin where possible, and patients with a maternal family history of aminoglycoside deafness should not receive it.
## Recognising vestibular toxicity
Bilateral vestibular loss presents with **oscillopsia** (the world jumps when walking) and imbalance in the dark, often noticed only when the patient is mobilised after an ICU stay; a head impulse test shows bilateral catch-up saccades. There is no vertigo, because both sides are lost together.
## Monitoring
Measure levels by the protocol (a trough before the next dose with once-daily dosing), check renal function, question patients about hearing, tinnitus and imbalance, and consider high-frequency audiometry on long courses.

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
- **Ototoxicity** (cochlear and vestibular), as above.
- **Nephrotoxicity**: usually reversible acute tubular injury.
- Neuromuscular blockade, especially in myasthenia and with anaesthetic agents.
## Interactions
Loop diuretics, vancomycin, ciclosporin, cisplatin, amphotericin and neuromuscular blockers.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Which aminoglycosides are **vestibulotoxic** versus **cochleotoxic**.
- Mechanism of hair cell damage and why it starts in the basal turn.
- The **m.1555A>G** mitochondrial mutation.
- How bilateral vestibular loss presents (oscillopsia) and how you test for it.
- Monitoring and risk factors.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Continuing gentamicin beyond a few days without levels and review.
- Giving it with furosemide without considering ototoxicity.
- Missing bilateral vestibular failure in a patient who was on ICU.
!!
