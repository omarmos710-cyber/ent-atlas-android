@stand A discharging ear with headache, fever, fits or drowsiness is an intracranial complication until proven otherwise. Meningitis is the commonest, brain abscess the next; often there is more than one. The order of work matters: resuscitate, image before any lumbar puncture, start antibiotics, involve neurosurgery, and only then deal with the ear.

# Overview | When otitis media crosses the dura.
## Definition and scope
Infection spreading from the middle ear and mastoid into the cranial cavity: extradural abscess, subdural empyema, meningitis, brain abscess, lateral (sigmoid) sinus thrombophlebitis and otitic hydrocephalus. Perforation, ossicular erosion, tympanosclerosis and atelectasis are *sequelae* of otitis media, not complications.
!fig d12-1 | The complications of suppurative otitis media and the routes of spread.
## Epidemiology
Antibiotics have made these complications rare where care is good, but they remain important where chronic ears go untreated. Half of brain abscesses in adults and a quarter in children are otogenic, and a temporal lobe abscess is about twice as common as a cerebellar one.
## Acute or chronic disease
Infants with acute otitis media tend to develop meningitis by blood-borne spread. Adults with chronic otitis media, especially cholesteatoma, develop abscesses and sinus thrombosis by bone erosion.
!differ How often there is more than one
Dhingra: About one-third of patients with otogenic meningitis have another intracranial lesion.
Lee: About half.
Either way, look for a second complication in every patient.
!!

# Pathogenesis | Three routes into the skull.
## Routes of spread
### Direct bone erosion
Cholesteatoma, osteitis and granulation destroy the tegmen or the sinus plate, bringing infection to the dura.
### Venous thrombophlebitis
Infected thrombus in small veins crosses intact bone, which is why an abscess can arise without visible erosion.
### Preformed pathways
Congenital dehiscences, a patent petrosquamous suture or Hyrtl's fissure in children, inner ear malformations, fractures, previous surgery, and the round and oval windows via the labyrinth.
## Risk factors
Extremes of age, poverty and poor access to care, virulent or resistant organisms, inadequate antibiotic courses, immunosuppression, preformed defects and cholesteatoma.
## Organisms
Mixed: *Staphylococcus aureus*, streptococci, *Proteus*, *Pseudomonas* and *E. coli*, with anaerobes such as *Bacteroides*, particularly in abscesses.

# The complications | Each with its own picture.
## Extradural abscess
### Picture
Pus between the bone and the dura of the middle or posterior fossa. Often silent and found at mastoidectomy; sometimes a deep, one-sided headache that eases when pus flows from the ear.
### Treatment
At mastoidectomy, remove bone until healthy dura is exposed. If pus is suspected behind an intact plate, take the plate down.
## Subdural empyema
### Picture
Pus in the subdural space spreads quickly over the hemisphere: severe headache, fever, meningism, seizures, aphasia and rapidly progressive hemiplegia, with raised pressure.
### Treatment
A neurosurgical emergency: urgent drainage through burr holes or craniotomy, with high-dose antibiotics.
## Meningitis
### Picture
The commonest complication. Headache, fever, photophobia, vomiting, neck stiffness, and positive Kernig and Brudzinski signs; later, drowsiness, fits and coma.
### CSF
Turbid, under raised pressure, with many polymorphs, high protein and low sugar.
### Treatment
Intravenous antibiotics that penetrate the CSF, and steroids. Treat the ear: myringotomy for acute otitis media, mastoidectomy for chronic disease once the patient is fit.
!pearl After meningitis
Check hearing early. Labyrinthitis ossificans can close the cochlea within weeks, and a cochlear implant must go in before it does.
!!
## Brain abscess
### Stages
!differ Stages of an otogenic brain abscess
Dhingra: Four stages: invasion (initial encephalitis), localisation (the latent, quiet stage), enlargement, and termination by rupture into the ventricle or subarachnoid space.
Lee: Three stages: encephalitis, a quiet period, and rupture.
The quiet stage is the trap: the patient seems better while the abscess is forming.
!!
### Raised intracranial pressure
Morning headache, projectile vomiting, papilloedema, a slow pulse and a subnormal temperature, with drowsiness.
### Temporal lobe abscess
Nominal aphasia (with a dominant-side lesion), a contralateral upper quadrant field defect, contralateral weakness, and uncinate fits with hallucinations of taste and smell. An ipsilateral third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil warns of tentorial herniation.
### Cerebellar abscess
Suboccipital headache, nystagmus, ipsilateral ataxia with past-pointing, intention tremor and dysdiadochokinesia, and early papilloedema.
!fig d12-6 d12-7 | First, CT of a left cerebellar abscess. Second, CT of a right otogenic cerebral (temporal lobe) abscess.
### CSF
Moderately raised cells and protein with normal sugar; but a lumbar puncture is dangerous and is avoided while there is mass effect.
### Treatment
Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics with anaerobic cover, steroids or mannitol for raised pressure, and neurosurgical drainage by aspiration through a burr hole or excision. The ear is then made safe through a separate operative field.
!differ Timing of ear surgery with an abscess
Dhingra: Operate on the ear once the abscess is controlled.
Lee: Timing is debated; some treat both at one sitting when the patient is stable.
!!
## Lateral sinus thrombophlebitis
### Picture
Inflammation of the sigmoid sinus wall, mural thrombus, then an infected clot. The fever is the clue: high, swinging, *picket-fence* fever with rigors and well periods in between, like malaria but irregular.
### Signs
- **Griesinger's sign:** oedema over the mastoid emissary vein.
- **Tobey-Ayer test:** compressing the internal jugular vein on the healthy side raises CSF pressure, but compressing the affected side does not.
- **Crowe-Beck test:** compressing the healthy jugular vein engorges the retinal veins.
- Papilloedema, more often with a right-sided thrombus as the right sinus is usually dominant.
- Tenderness along the jugular vein; septic emboli to the lungs, bones and joints.
### Investigations
Blood culture during a rigor, a smear to exclude malaria, and contrast CT (the *empty delta sign*) or MR venography.
### Treatment
Intravenous antibiotics and mastoidectomy with exposure of the sinus. If it is needle-aspirated and yields pus or no blood, it is opened between packs placed above and below, and infected clot is removed; healthy clot is left alone. Anticoagulation is debated. Ligation of the internal jugular vein is kept for septic emboli or clot spreading down the neck.
## Otitic hydrocephalus
### Picture
Raised intracranial pressure with normal CSF and normal-sized ventricles, usually after lateral sinus thrombosis that blocks venous outflow. Headache, vomiting, blurred vision, papilloedema and a sixth nerve palsy.
### CSF
Pressure over 300 mm of water, with normal contents.
### Treatment
Treat the ear and the sinus; lower the pressure with acetazolamide, steroids and mannitol, with repeated lumbar punctures or a shunt if needed. Serial eye examinations are essential, because the danger is blindness from optic atrophy.

# Investigations | Image before you puncture.
## Imaging
### Contrast CT of the brain and temporal bone
The first study: abscess, empyema, bone erosion and the delta sign.
### MRI with contrast and MR venography
More sensitive for early cerebritis, subdural collections and sinus thrombosis.
## Lumbar puncture
Only when imaging has excluded mass effect and raised pressure.

## Other tests
Blood cultures, full blood count, ear swab, audiogram when possible.
!table Which complication?
Complication | Clue | Next step
Meningitis | Neck stiffness | CT, then lumbar puncture
Temporal abscess | Aphasia, field defect | MRI, drain
Cerebellar abscess | Ataxia, nystagmus | MRI, drain
Sinus thrombosis | Picket-fence fever | MR venography
Otitic hydrocephalus | Papilloedema, clear CSF | MRV, lower pressure
!!

# Management | Patient first, ear last.
## The sequence
!ward On call: discharging ear with fever and headache
1. Airway, breathing, circulation; treat sepsis.
2. Full neurological examination, including fundi and cranial nerves.
3. Contrast CT of brain and temporal bones, then MRI and MRV.
4. Start intravenous antibiotics with CSF penetration and anaerobic cover.
5. Call neurosurgery early.
6. No lumbar puncture until mass effect and raised pressure are excluded.
!!
## Treating the ear
Acute otitis media: myringotomy and grommet. Chronic disease or cholesteatoma: mastoidectomy to remove the source, once the intracranial problem is controlled and the patient is fit.
## Outcomes
Deafness, epilepsy, focal deficits, loss of vision and death. Rupture of an abscess into the ventricle carries a mortality of about 80%.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Routes of spread:** erosion, thrombophlebitis, preformed pathways.
- **Stages of brain abscess,** and the danger of the quiet stage.
- **Temporal versus cerebellar abscess signs.**
- **Lateral sinus thrombosis:** picket-fence fever, Griesinger's sign, Tobey-Ayer and Crowe-Beck tests, delta sign.
- **CSF in each complication.**
- **Otitic hydrocephalus:** raised pressure, normal CSF, risk of blindness.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Doing a lumbar puncture before imaging.
- Being reassured by the quiet stage of an abscess.
- Treating swinging fever as malaria without examining the ear.
- Stopping at one complication and missing a second.
- Forgetting a hearing test after meningitis.
!!
