@stand Petrous apicitis is infection that has tracked from the middle ear deep into the tip of the petrous bone. The classic picture is Gradenigo's triad: a discharging ear, deep pain behind the eye and a sixth nerve palsy. It is rare now, but it can appear just as the ear seems to be improving.

# Summary | Infection at the tip of the temporal bone.
Petrous apicitis is inflammation, and sometimes abscess, of the air cells of the petrous apex, spreading from acute or chronic otitis media and [[t:mastoiditis|mastoiditis]]. The apex lies against the sixth nerve, the trigeminal ganglion, the internal carotid artery and the meninges, which explains Gradenigo's triad and the dangerous complications. CT and MRI are both needed. Most acute cases now settle on intravenous antibiotics with treatment of the middle ear; surgery is kept for an abscess or failure.

# Etiology | Why only some people get it.
- **Source:** acute or chronic otitis media and mastoiditis.
- **Pneumatisation:** only about 30% of petrous apices are pneumatised; the rest are diploic (marrow-filled) or sclerotic. Infection can only track into an aerated apex.

# Pathophysiology | Tracts into the apex, and its neighbours.
## Air cell tracts
The middle ear and mastoid connect with the apex by perilabyrinthine cell tracts:
- **Posterosuperior:** from the mastoid and antrum, above and behind the labyrinth, through *Trautmann's triangle* and the subarcuate region.
- **Anteroinferior:** from the hypotympanum and the region around the Eustachian tube, below the cochlea.
!fig d1-13 | The air cell tracts of the temporal bone.
!anat s22_14 s24_17 | The anterior and posterior surfaces of the petrous bone.
## Neighbours
### Dorello's canal
The sixth nerve passes under the petroclinoid (Gruber's) ligament, between the petrous tip and the sphenoid; inflammation here causes a lateral rectus palsy and double vision.
### Meckel's cave
The trigeminal ganglion sits in a depression on the apex; its irritation causes deep pain behind the eye.

# Clinical features | Gradenigo's triad.
## The triad
- **Persistent ear discharge.**
- **Deep retro-orbital or facial pain,** from the trigeminal ganglion.
- **Diplopia from a sixth nerve palsy.**
The full triad is uncommon today; often only one or two features are present.
## Other features
Fever, headache, neck stiffness and vomiting suggest meningeal spread; the seventh and eighth nerves are occasionally involved.
!pearl When to suspect it
Persistent discharge or deep pain despite an adequate mastoidectomy, or new pain after the ear has apparently settled.
!!

# Diagnosis | You need both CT and MRI.
## High-resolution CT
Shows the pneumatisation of the apex, breakdown of the cell septa and erosion of the bone.
## MRI with contrast
Separates marrow from fluid or pus (diffusion-weighted imaging separates apicitis from cholesterol granuloma, cholesteatoma and effusion), shows dural enhancement and intracranial spread, and assesses the carotid and cavernous sinus. MR venography looks for venous sinus thrombosis.
!imaging | Petrositis, Gradenigo syndrome and apical pseudotumours.

# Differential diagnosis | Other lesions of the apex.
## The list
- **Apical effusion:** fluid in an aerated apex, usually asymptomatic.
- **Asymmetric marrow:** a non-pneumatised apex full of fat that looks abnormal on MRI; a common false alarm.
- **Cholesterol granuloma:** bright on both T1 and T2, expanding slowly.
- **Cholesteatoma** of the apex.
- **Nasopharyngeal carcinoma** and other skull base tumours. Lower cranial nerve palsies with an apex lesion raise concern for malignancy.
## On MRI and CT

## Cholesterol granuloma
The commonest primary apex lesion, about ten times commoner than apical cholesteatoma, and forms when bleeding fills an obstructed air cell. Asymptomatic lesions are watched with serial imaging; symptomatic ones are drained, preserving hearing where possible, through an infracochlear or infralabyrinthine route, or endoscopically through the sphenoid when the lesion abuts it.

# Management | Antibiotics first.
## Medical treatment
Admission, prolonged intravenous antibiotics that cover the organisms of otitis media and penetrate bone, and treatment of the middle ear source with myringotomy or mastoidectomy. Most acute cases now settle on antibiotics alone.
## Surgery
For an abscess, bone necrosis, or failure of medical treatment. The route follows the cell tracts and the state of the hearing:
- **Hearing preserved:** through the mastoid via Trautmann's triangle or the subarcuate cells, infralabyrinthine or infracochlear routes, or through the middle cranial fossa.
- **Dead ear:** translabyrinthine or transcochlear routes, which give the widest access.
- **Medially placed lesions:** endoscopic transsphenoidal drainage is increasingly used.
!ops
!update Current evidence
**Treatment.** Modern case series show that most petrous apicitis responds to intravenous antibiotics with myringotomy or grommet, or cortical mastoidectomy; apical drainage is kept for failure of medical treatment. Gradenigo's full triad is present in only a minority.
!!
## Drugs and doses
!table Petrous apicitis: drugs and doses
Situation | Drug and dose | Course and notes
After acute otitis media | Ceftriaxone 50 to 75 mg/kg IV once daily (maximum 2 g; adults 2 g); add metronidazole if anaerobes are suspected | With myringotomy and grommet
After chronic otitis media or failure | Piperacillin–tazobactam (children 90 mg/kg, maximum 4.5 g, every 6 to 8 hours; adults 4.5 g every 8 hours), or ceftazidime with metronidazole | Mastoidectomy and drainage
Penicillin anaphylaxis | Specialist advice (for example a quinolone with metronidazole in adults) | 
Duration | Intravenous until clinically and biochemically settled, then oral; often 3 to 6 weeks in total for bone infection | Guided by CRP and imaging
Intracranial spread | Treat as intracranial infection | See intracranial complications
!!
Doses from paediatric mastoiditis pathways (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia; Johns Hopkins All Children's) and the BNF; duration from published case series. Local microbiology advice takes precedence.

# Complications | Neighbours at risk.
- Meningitis and other intracranial infection ([[t:icc]]).
- Injury to or thrombosis of the internal carotid artery.
- Skull base osteomyelitis.
- Cavernous sinus thrombosis.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Reading asymmetric apical marrow as infection.
- Missing the diagnosis because the ear seemed to be getting better.
- Waiting for the full triad.
