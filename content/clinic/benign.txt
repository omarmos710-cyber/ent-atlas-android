@stand Most hoarse voices come from the vocal folds themselves, and most of what grows on them is benign: nodules in the teacher, a polyp after a shout, Reinke's oedema in the smoker, a granuloma on the vocal process, papillomas in a child. Benign does not mean harmless. These lesions take voices from those who earn their living by them and airways from small children, and a white patch on a smoker's fold may already be carcinoma in situ. Look carefully, ideally with a stroboscope; give voice therapy its due; and when surgery is needed, preserve the delicate cover of the fold.

## Assessment of the hoarse voice
Any hoarseness lasting more than three weeks, or sooner in a smoker or with pain, dysphagia, otalgia, stridor or a neck mass, needs laryngoscopy. *Videostroboscopy* shows the mucosal wave, which distinguishes superficial lesions (nodules, polyps) from those that stiffen the fold (cysts, scar, sulcus, carcinoma). Perceptual rating, acoustic analysis and voice handicap indices measure the voice before and after treatment.
## Surgery
*Phonomicrosurgery* under suspension microlaryngoscopy with cold instruments or laser: a *microflap* is raised through a small incision on the upper surface, the lesion dissected from beneath, and the epithelium laid back, preserving the superficial lamina propria and the vocal ligament. The anterior commissure is never stripped on both sides at once, to avoid a web. Voice therapy before and after prevents recurrence.

# Phonotraumatic lesions | Nodules, polyps and Reinke's oedema.
## Vocal nodules
Singers', screamers' or teachers' nodes. *Bilateral, symmetrical* swellings on the free edge at the junction of the anterior third and posterior two-thirds, the midpoint of the membranous fold, where vibration and collision are greatest. They arise from chronic misuse of the voice (shouting, singing too loudly or in the wrong register, speaking at an unnaturally low pitch): submucosal oedema and haemorrhage organise and hyalinise, and the epithelium thickens. Early nodules are soft, red and oedematous; mature ones grey-white and firm. Teachers, singers, actors, vendors and talkative children, especially boys, are typical; in adults, women more often.
!fig d61-1 d61-2 | First, the site of vocal nodules at the junction of the anterior third and posterior two-thirds of the folds. Second, vocal nodules on laryngoscopy.
- **Symptoms:** hoarseness, a breathy voice that tires, loss of the upper range in singers, and neck pain after prolonged speaking.
- **Stroboscopy:** an hourglass closure with a preserved mucosal wave.
- **Treatment:** *voice therapy* is the cornerstone; most children's nodules disappear, and many adults'. Lee goes further: lesions that do not shrink after good, compliant therapy are by definition not nodules. Microsurgical excision for large, fibrotic nodules persisting after therapy, with care not to injure the vocal ligament, and continued therapy afterwards; intralesional steroid may help.
## Vocal polyp
Usually a *unilateral*, soft, smooth, often pedunculated swelling at the same site, after sudden shouting causing haemorrhage into the fold, with smoking and allergy contributing; mostly men of thirty to fifty. It may be haemorrhagic, gelatinous or fibrous. Hoarseness, *diplophonia* (two pitches from two differently vibrating folds), and if large and pedunculated, choking or stridor as it flops through the glottis. Treatment is microsurgical excision followed by voice therapy.
!fig d61-4 | A pedunculated polyp on the right vocal fold.
## Reinke's oedema (polypoid degeneration)
Bilateral, diffuse, symmetrical, pale, translucent swelling of the whole membranous folds from gelatinous fluid in Reinke's space; smoking is almost always responsible, with voice abuse, reflux and hypothyroidism. The voice becomes deep and rough, more noticeable in women, who may be mistaken for men on the telephone; large swellings obstruct breathing. Stop smoking; treat reflux and thyroid; voice therapy; *decortication* by a lateral incision, suction of the gel and trimming of excess epithelium, traditionally one side at a time.
!fig d61-5 | Bilateral Reinke's oedema in a 50-year-old woman who smoked.

# Granulomas and keratosis | The back of the fold and the white patch.
## Contact ulcer and granuloma
On the *vocal processes* of the arytenoids, where they hammer together with hard glottal attacks, forceful low-pitched speech and habitual throat clearing; laryngopharyngeal reflux is a major factor, and intubation another. Hoarseness, pain on speaking radiating to the ear, a foreign-body feeling and constant throat clearing. Ulcers or granulomas sit on one or both vocal processes with red arytenoid mucosa.
- Anti-reflux treatment (twice-daily proton pump inhibitor), even without heartburn.
- Voice therapy to stop throat clearing and correct pitch.
- Inhaled or injected steroid.
- Botulinum toxin to the adductors (lateral cricoarytenoid and thyroarytenoid) for recurrent granulomas.
- Surgery only for airway obstruction, failure of treatment or suspicion of tumour; recurrence after excision is common. Send tissue for histology to exclude carcinoma and granular cell tumour.
## Intubation granuloma
Injury to the vocal processes from rough intubation, a large tube or prolonged intubation; usually bilateral on the posterior folds, with hoarseness and, if large, dyspnoea. Many mature into a pedunculated mass and drop off; treatment is as for contact granuloma.
## Leukoplakia and keratosis
White plaques or warty thickening on the upper surface of one or both folds with normal mobility, in smokers. Histology ranges from simple keratosis through dysplasia to carcinoma in situ and invasive carcinoma, so it is a *precancerous condition*. Biopsy or *excisional microflap stripping* for histology, stop smoking, treat reflux, and follow up for recurrence and new lesions; avoid repeated deep stripping that scars the voice.
!flag The white patch on a smoker's fold
A keratotic or red lesion with a reduced mucosal wave on stroboscopy, irregular surface, or impaired mobility is suspicious for invasive carcinoma: biopsy rather than repeated observation.
!!
## Amyloid
Men of fifty to seventy: a yellow or orange submucosal mass in the false fold, ventricle, true fold or subglottis, causing hoarseness or dyspnoea; apple-green birefringence with Congo red. Exclude myeloma and systemic amyloidosis; remove localised deposits endoscopically; the outlook is good.

# Cysts and sulcus | Hidden in the fold.
## Ductal (retention) cysts
From blocked seromucinous glands in the vallecula, aryepiglottic fold, false fold, ventricle and piriform fossa: silent when small; hoarseness, cough, throat pain and dyspnoea when large.
!fig d61-6 | (A) An aryepiglottic cyst causing intermittent laryngeal obstruction. (B) The cyst after removal.
## Vocal fold cysts
- **Epidermoid cyst:** an inclusion cyst of keratin, congenital or from phonotrauma, white and deep in the fold, often against the ligament.
- **Mucous retention cyst:** smooth, glistening and fluid-filled.
Usually unilateral, sometimes with a reactive nodule on the opposite fold, and easily confused with nodules; stroboscopy shows a stiff segment with reduced mucosal wave, and they do not resolve with voice therapy. Microflap excision, ideally intact; residual wall recurs.
!fig d61-7 | An epidermoid cyst of the left vocal fold, full of cheesy material at excision.
## Saccular cysts and laryngocele
Obstruction of the saccule's mouth fills it with mucus (a *saccular cyst*: anterior, bulging from the ventricle; or lateral, into the false fold and aryepiglottic fold and even the neck). Distension with air forms a *laryngocele*: internal, external (through the thyrohyoid membrane, a reducible neck swelling that grows with coughing or Valsalva) or combined, linked to raised pressure in wind players, glass blowers and weight lifters. Diagnosed by laryngoscopy, radiographs with Valsalva and CT. Endoscopic marsupialisation or excision for internal lesions; external excision for external and combined lesions. In an adult, a ventricular carcinoma obstructing the saccule must be looked for and biopsied.
!fig d61-8 d61-9 | First, a laryngocele of the mixed type with internal and external components. Second, a left laryngocele as seen on Valsalva (arrow).
!imaging | Laryngocele on imaging.
## Sulcus vocalis
A furrow along the free edge where the lamina propria is deficient and the epithelium dips to the ligament or muscle: congenital, or from phonotrauma, perhaps an opened epidermoid cyst. A breathy, weak voice with incomplete closure, a spindle-shaped gap and a reduced mucosal wave; confirmed by palpation at microlaryngoscopy. Prolonged voice therapy first; treat reflux and allergy. Surgery has limited success: steroid or filler injection, fat or fascia implantation, excision of the sulcus, slicing or multiple relaxing incisions, undermining, and medialisation thyroplasty for closure.

# Neoplasms | Papillomas first.
## Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis
The commonest benign tumour of the larynx (about 80% of benign neoplasms) and of the child's larynx, caused by *HPV 6 and 11*.
- **Juvenile onset:** acquired from mothers with genital warts at birth; first-born children of young mothers by vaginal delivery are at greatest risk (caesarean section does not reliably prevent it). Presents at two to five years with hoarseness, a weak cry or aphonia and then stridor; often misdiagnosed as asthma or croup. Multiple, aggressive and recurrent, at junctions of squamous and ciliated epithelium (supraglottis, glottis, subglottis), spreading to the trachea and bronchi, especially after tracheostomy.
- **Adult onset:** men of thirty to fifty, often single, at the anterior fold or commissure, less aggressive; malignant change is rare (more with HPV 16 and 18, smoking and irradiation).
!fig d61-11 | Supraglottic papillomatosis.
### Treatment
The aim is a safe airway and a useful voice with as few scars as possible; surgery debulks, it does not cure.
- **Surgery:** microdebrider, CO2 laser or pulsed KTP laser under suspension laryngoscopy, avoiding injury to the vocal ligament and the anterior commissure; repeated as often as needed. Office KTP laser in adults.
- **Adjuvants** for frequent recurrence or distal spread: intralesional *cidofovir*, systemic or intralesional *bevacizumab*, indole-3-carbinol, and (older) interferon alpha and retinoids; the HPV vaccine as therapy is under study, and as prevention will reduce maternal transmission.
- **Tracheostomy** is avoided if possible, because it encourages tracheal spread.

## Chondroma
Usually from the posterior lamina of the cricoid, also the thyroid, arytenoid or epiglottis; men of forty to sixty. A slow, hard, submucosal mass causing dyspnoea when subglottic, or a lump feeling and dysphagia when growing back into the hypopharynx. CT shows a calcified cartilaginous mass; biopsy (with the laser through hard tissue) to exclude chondrosarcoma. Excision through a laryngofissure or lateral pharyngotomy, preserving as much cricoid as possible; laryngectomy for large or recurrent tumours.
## Haemangioma
- **Infantile:** subglottic, capillary, presenting with stridor in the first six months, half with skin haemangiomas; involutes, and is treated with propranolol (see congenital lesions).
- **Adult:** cavernous, on the fold or supraglottis; left alone if silent, treated with steroids, laser or excision if symptomatic.
## Granular cell tumour
From Schwann cells, submucosal, usually on the posterior fold; the overlying *pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia* can be mistaken for well-differentiated carcinoma. Complete excision prevents recurrence.
## Others
Pleomorphic adenoma, oncocytoma, neurilemmoma and neurofibroma, rhabdomyoma, lipoma and fibroma are rare and excised endoscopically or through an external approach.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Site of nodules** and why there.
- **Nodule versus polyp versus cyst:** bilaterality, stroboscopy and response to therapy.
- **Reinke's oedema** and smoking.
- **Contact granuloma:** reflux and voice misuse.
- **Leukoplakia** as a premalignant lesion.
- **Laryngocele:** types and the hidden carcinoma.
- **Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis:** HPV types, behaviour, treatment and adjuvants, and why tracheostomy is avoided.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Operating on nodules before a proper course of voice therapy.
- Stripping both sides of the anterior commissure at once.
- Repeatedly observing a white patch in a smoker without a biopsy.
- Diagnosing a child with papillomas as asthma.
!!
