@stand The pinna is all cartilage and skin, and the cartilage depends entirely on its perichondrium. A haematoma after a blow must be drained early, because blood trapped under the perichondrium becomes a cauliflower ear. Congenital anomalies are many, and most are cosmetic; the one to examine fully is microtia, which may hide a deaf ear.

# Summary | Shape, development and injury.
This topic covers the conditions of the pinna, each in its own chapter: [[@Congenital anomalies]], [[@Haematoma]], [[@Wounds, bites and cold]], and [[@Keloid]]. Infections and inflammation of the pinna are in [[t:perich]].

# Applied anatomy and development | Cartilage and skin.
## Applied anatomy
A single plate of elastic cartilage covered by skin that is tightly adherent on the lateral surface. The lobule has no cartilage. The *incisura terminalis*, between the tragus and the crus of the helix, also has no cartilage, which is why an endaural incision there avoids cutting it.
!fig d1-1 | (A) The divisions of the ear. (B) Elevations and depressions of the lateral surface of the pinna. (C) The auricular cartilage.
!anat s30_28 s31_30 | Surface landmarks of the auricle, and its nerve supply.
## Development
The pinna develops from six *hillocks of His* around the first branchial cleft. The tragus comes from the first arch and most of the rest from the second. It reaches its adult shape by about 20 weeks of gestation and most of its adult size by about nine years.
!fig d1-24 | Development of the pinna from six hillocks around the first branchial cleft.
!differ Hillocks
Dhingra: The tragus from the first arch; the rest from the second arch.
Lee: The first three hillocks from the first arch, the last three from the second.
In practice: Both put the tragus on the first arch and most of the pinna on the second.
!!

# Congenital anomalies {dz} | From a tag to an absent ear.
!fig d8-1n | A normal pinna.
## Absent or small
### Anotia
Complete absence of the pinna, often with an absent canal.
### Microtia
A small, malformed pinna, graded from mild deformity to a peanut-shaped vestige. It is often associated with canal atresia and middle ear anomalies, so hearing must be tested ([[t:atresia]]).
!fig d8-2n d8-3 | First, anotia: no pinna or canal on the left. Second, microtia of the right ear (a peanut ear).
## Abnormal shape
- **Bat ear (prominent ear):** the commonest anomaly, from an underdeveloped antihelical fold or deep concha. The normal angle between the pinna and the head is about 15 to 30 degrees.
- **Cup and lop ear:** folding or deficiency of the upper pinna.
- **Cryptotia:** the upper pinna is buried under the scalp skin.
- **Stahl's ear:** an extra crus giving a pointed helix.
- **Satyr ear:** a pointed upper pole.
- **Darwin's tubercle:** a small, harmless nodule on the helix.
- **Coloboma:** a cleft in the pinna.
!fig d8-4 | Stahl's ear, with an extra crus.
## Accessory tissue
- **Preauricular tags:** may contain cartilage.
- **Preauricular pit or sinus:** a small opening in front of the crus of the helix, from faulty fusion of the hillocks. It may become repeatedly infected.
!fig d8-5 d8-6 | First, preauricular appendages. Second, an infected preauricular sinus discharging pus.
## Management
- **Bat ear:** otoplasty, usually after age six, before teasing at school.
- **Stahl's and other shape anomalies:** a mould in the first six weeks of life, while the cartilage is soft, can correct them without surgery.
- **Microtia:** autologous rib cartilage reconstruction, usually from about age eight to ten, remains the standard; porous polyethylene frameworks allow earlier, single-stage reconstruction but carry a higher risk of exposure.
- **Preauricular sinus:** antibiotics for infection; excision of the whole tract when it has been repeatedly infected ([[t:earminor]]).
!ops
!update Current evidence
**Microtia.** Autologous rib cartilage reconstruction, usually from about age eight to ten, remains the standard; porous polyethylene frameworks allow earlier, single-stage reconstruction but carry a higher risk of exposure. **Prominent ears.** Ear moulding in the first weeks of life corrects many deformities without surgery.
!!

# Haematoma {dz} | The boxer's ear.
## Etiology
Blunt trauma from boxing, wrestling or rugby shears the perichondrium from the cartilage, and blood collects between them.
## Clinical features
A tense, fluctuant, smooth swelling on the lateral surface that obliterates the landmarks.
## Management
!ward On call: a swollen pinna after a blow
1. Aspirate or incise the haematoma.
2. Pressure bolster with the concavities packed.
3. Antibiotics; check for reaccumulation within a day or two, and review the bolster at 4 to 7 days.
4. Lacerations: repair early, covering the cartilage.
!!
Drain early: aspiration for small, fresh collections, or incision for larger or recurrent ones. Then apply a pressure bolster with the concavities packed, through-and-through sutures or a moulded dressing, give antibiotics, and recheck for reaccumulation. The choice of method, the bolster and aftercare are covered in detail in [[t:earminor]].
!update Current evidence
**Haematoma.** A Cochrane review found no randomised trials comparing needle aspiration with incision and drainage, or different pressure dressings (Jones and Mahendran, 2004); case series favour incision and drainage with a bolster or through-and-through sutures, which reduce reaccumulation.
!!
### Drugs and doses
!table Pinna haematoma: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Every patient | Drainage and pressure bolster; analgesia | Recheck at 24 to 48 hours
After drainage (commonly given; evidence limited) | Co-amoxiclav 500/125 mg three times a day; or ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice a day for anti-pseudomonal cover in adults | About 5 to 7 days
Infected haematoma | Treat as perichondritis (ciprofloxacin 750 mg twice a day) and drain | 
!!
Doses from the BNF; practice from case series (no randomised trials).
## Complications
Untreated or infected, the cartilage necroses and new cartilage forms irregularly, giving a *cauliflower ear*.
!fig d8-7 | A cauliflower (boxer's) ear.

# Wounds, bites and cold {dz} | Other injuries.
## Lacerations
Repair early, covering the cartilage with skin and keeping the perichondrium attached.
!fig d8-8 | A laceration of the left pinna.
## Avulsion
A partly avulsed segment on a pedicle is reattached. A completely avulsed part may be reattached as a composite graft if small, banked under postauricular skin, or replanted microvascularly.
## Bites
The lobe is the commonest site; human bites are the most likely to become infected. Clean thoroughly, give antibiotics and repair.
### Bites: drugs and doses
!table Bites of the ear: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Every bite | Irrigate, debride, repair; tetanus status; assess rabies and blood-borne virus risk | 
First line, adults | Co-amoxiclav 250/125 mg or 500/125 mg three times a day | 3 days for prophylaxis, 5 days for treatment (up to 7 with review)
Penicillin allergy, adults | Doxycycline 200 mg on day 1, then 100 to 200 mg daily, with metronidazole 400 mg three times a day | Same durations; not in pregnancy
Children | Co-amoxiclav by weight or age (BNF for Children); under 12 with penicillin allergy, co-trimoxazole | Same durations
Severe infection | Intravenous co-amoxiclav 1.2 g three times a day | Review at 48 hours
!!
Doses from NICE NG184 Human and animal bites.
## Frostbite
The pinna is exposed and thin. Rewarm with moist pledgets at 38 to 42°C, give analgesia and antibiotics, avoid pressure dressings, and delay debridement for weeks to months until the dead tissue demarcates.
!differ Rewarming
Dhingra: Moist pledgets at 38 to 42°C, noting that rapid rewarming is painful.
Lee: Favours slow rewarming.
In practice: Rewarm with moist pledgets at 38 to 42°C and give good analgesia, because rewarming hurts.
!!

# Keloid {dz} | After piercing.
## Epidemiology
After piercing, often in darker skin.
## Management
Excision alone recurs; combine with steroid injection, pressure or radiotherapy ([[t:extca]]).
### Treatment steps
!table Keloid: treatment
Step | Treatment and dose | Course and notes
Small or early | Intralesional triamcinolone acetonide 10 to 40 mg/ml | Every 4 to 6 weeks; skin atrophy and pigment change
Established | Excision (intramarginal) with steroid injection at surgery and after | Excision alone recurs in most
Adjuncts | Pressure earring or clip; silicone gel | Months
Recurrent | Excision with postoperative radiotherapy | Within days of surgery
!!
Based on international scar management recommendations (Gold et al., *Dermatol Surg* 2014) and the BNF.

!pit

- Draining without a pressure dressing.
