@stand A teenage boy with a blocked nose and nosebleeds that keep coming back has a juvenile angiofibroma until proven otherwise. It is benign, but it bleeds torrentially, grows through the natural doorways of the skull base, and is diagnosed by imaging, never by a biopsy in clinic.

# Summary | The bleeding tumour of adolescent boys.
Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (also called nasopharyngeal fibroma) is a benign, locally aggressive, highly vascular tumour arising at the back of the nasal cavity, near the sphenopalatine foramen. It presents with profuse, recurrent epistaxis and nasal obstruction in a teenage boy. Profuse epistaxis, anaemia, and spread into the orbit and skull base. Biopsy in clinic can cause catastrophic bleeding. It does not regress on its own, so waiting is not an option. Contrast CT (the antral sign) makes the diagnosis; angiography maps the feeders and allows embolisation; complete surgical removal, now mostly endoscopic, is the treatment.

# Epidemiology | Who gets it.
The commonest benign tumour of the nasopharynx, yet under 1% of head and neck tumours. Almost exclusively in boys and young men of 10 to 20 years.

# Etiology | Hormones and malformed vessels.
## Why adolescent boys
It is thought to be testosterone-dependent: a hamartomatous nidus of vascular tissue, perhaps a remnant of the first branchial arch artery, is activated at puberty. About three-quarters of tumours carry androgen receptors.
## Molecular clues
Vascular endothelial growth factor and insulin-like growth factor II drive growth; stromal cells show beta-catenin activity, and C-MYC is found in large tumours. Angiofibroma is about 24 times commoner in familial adenomatous polyposis of the colon.

# Pathophysiology | Origin, pathology and spread.
## Site of origin
The posterior nasal cavity, at the superior margin of the sphenopalatine foramen, near the junction of the sphenoidal process of the palatine bone with the root of the pterygoid plate.
## Gross and microscopic pathology
A reddish-purple, lobulated, sessile mass covered by mucosa, without a capsule. Two components: endothelium-lined vascular spaces lacking muscle and elastic tissue (so they cannot contract and bleed profusely), and a stroma of stellate and spindle cells laying down collagen, whose amount decides how firm the tumour is.
!fig d49-4 | Angiofibroma: dilated vascular spaces in a fibrous stroma.
## Three modes
- **Along paths of least resistance:** into the nose and nasopharynx, and through fissures and foramina: the foramen rotundum, vidian (pterygoid) canal, palatovaginal canal and inferior orbital fissure.
- **By expansion:** pushing the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus forwards from the pterygopalatine fossa (the antral sign).
- **By bone destruction:** at the root of the pterygoid and the greater wing of the sphenoid.
!fig d49-1 d49-2 | First, the pterygopalatine fossa from the side, with its routes to the orbit, infratemporal fossa and cranial cavity. Second, the openings in its posterior wall: foramen rotundum, vidian canal and palatovaginal canal.
!anat s162_74 | Communications of the pterygopalatine fossa, the crossroads through which the tumour spreads.
## Extensions
1. Nasal cavity and nasopharynx, blocking the choanae and displacing the septum.
2. Paranasal sinuses: maxillary, sphenoid, ethmoid.
3. Pterygopalatine fossa, then through the pterygomaxillary fissure into the infratemporal and temporal fossae and the cheek.
4. Orbit, through the inferior orbital fissure: proptosis, diplopia, optic nerve compression.
5. Cranial cavity in 20 to 25%, mainly the middle fossa (lateral to the internal carotid if it erodes the floor, medial if it comes through the sphenoid and sella), less often the anterior fossa; usually extradural, sometimes into the cavernous sinus.
!fig d49-3 | Axial view of the spread: into the nose, the nasopharynx, behind the antrum into the pterygopalatine fossa, and on into the cheek.
!pearl Recurrence hides in canals
Tumour left in the vidian canal or the basisphenoid is the commonest source of recurrence: drill the canal at surgery.
!!

# Clinical features | Epistaxis and obstruction in a boy.
## Nasal and nasopharyngeal
- **Profuse, recurrent, spontaneous epistaxis:** the commonest presentation, often with anaemia ([[t:epistaxis]]).
- **Progressive nasal obstruction** with a hyponasal voice.
- **Nasal discharge** from obstruction and sinusitis.
## Ear
Conductive hearing loss and otitis media with effusion from tubal obstruction.
## Eye and nerves
Proptosis and diplopia; involvement of the second, third, fourth and sixth nerves with orbital and intracranial spread.
## Face
Broadening of the nasal bridge, swelling of the cheek, and a *frog face* with bilateral proptosis.
## Examination
Endoscopy shows a smooth or lobulated, pink to purplish mass with prominent surface vessels in the back of the nose and nasopharynx, obstructing one or both choanae, sometimes pushing the soft palate down or hanging into the oropharynx. Never palpate it with a finger outside the operating theatre.
!fig d49-5 | Endoscopic view of an angiofibroma (arrow) in the left nasal cavity, with prominent surface vessels; septum (S), inferior turbinate (IT), middle turbinate (MT).
!flag Never biopsy in clinic
Diagnosis is made by imaging. If tissue is truly needed to exclude another tumour, biopsy is done under general anaesthesia with blood cross-matched and everything ready to control bleeding.
!!

# Diagnosis | Imaging makes the diagnosis.
## Contrast CT
The investigation of choice. It shows the enhancing mass, bone erosion and the extent of spread. *Anterior bowing of the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus* (the antral or Holman-Miller sign) is pathognomonic. Widening of the pterygopalatine fossa and erosion of the vidian canal are typical.
## MRI
Complements CT for soft tissue extension: intracranial, cavernous sinus, orbit, infratemporal fossa. Flow voids give a *salt and pepper* appearance.
## Angiography and embolisation
Digital subtraction angiography shows the feeders, mainly from the external carotid (the internal maxillary artery's sphenopalatine and posterior nasal branches, the ascending pharyngeal and ascending palatine arteries), and from the internal carotid in large or intracranial tumours. External carotid feeders can be embolised; surgery should follow within 24 to 48 hours (up to 72 in some series), before collaterals develop. Embolisation carries risks of stroke, blindness and cranial nerve palsy, so some surgeons omit it for small tumours.
!fig d49-6 d49-7 | First, the specimen of an extensive angiofibroma and its CT, showing bone destruction and spread into the pterygopalatine fossa. Second, angiography before and after embolisation.
!imaging | Juvenile angiofibroma on CT, MRI and angiography.
## Preparation
Group and cross-match two to three units of blood.
## Investigations: what and when
!table Juvenile angiofibroma: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
First (clinic) | Nasal endoscopy | Smooth red-purple mass at the sphenopalatine foramen in an adolescent boy
Not done | Biopsy in clinic | Risk of severe bleeding; imaging makes the diagnosis
Imaging of choice | Contrast CT of the sinuses and skull base | Enhancing mass, widened sphenopalatine foramen, Holman–Miller sign, bone erosion
Soft tissue extent | Contrast MRI | Orbit, infratemporal fossa, cavernous sinus, intracranial spread; flow voids

Before surgery | Full blood count; group and cross-match 2 to 3 units |

# Differential diagnosis | Other masses at the back of the nose.
Antrochoanal polyp ([[t:crs]]), enlarged adenoids, olfactory neuroblastoma ([[t:sinus_ca]]), rhabdomyosarcoma, lobular capillary haemangioma and haemangiopericytoma.

# Subtypes and variants | Staging: extent decides the approach.
Several systems exist (Sessions, Fisch, Chandler, Radkowski, Andrews, and newer endoscopic systems). The older ones (Fisch, Radkowski, Andrews) stage by extent only: extension into the sinuses, pterygopalatine and infratemporal fossae, orbit and cranial cavity. The **University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC)** system adds residual vascularity from the internal carotid artery after embolisation, and predicts blood loss and recurrence better.
!table UPMC staging of angiofibroma
Stage | Extent
I | Nasal cavity, medial pterygopalatine fossa
II | One or more sinuses, lateral pterygopalatine fossa; no residual vascularity
III | Skull base erosion, orbit or infratemporal fossa; no residual vascularity
IV | Skull base erosion, orbit or infratemporal fossa; residual vascularity
V | Intracranial extension with residual vascularity (medial or lateral)
!!

# Management | Surgery is the treatment.
## Principles
Complete surgical removal is the treatment of choice; the tumour does not regress with age. Embolisation reduces blood loss: early tumours are removed endoscopically, often without embolisation; advanced tumours with internal carotid supply have the external carotid branches embolised first. Hormonal treatment is no longer used.
### Treatment steps
!table Juvenile angiofibroma: treatment
Extent | Treatment | Notes
Nose, nasopharynx, sinuses, pterygopalatine fossa | Endoscopic resection, with or without embolisation | Lower blood loss and recurrence than open surgery in series
Infratemporal fossa or limited intracranial spread | Extended endoscopic or combined open approach after embolisation | Specialist skull base centre
Unresectable intracranial disease, residual next to critical structures | Radiotherapy (fractionated or stereotactic) | Growth, cataract and second tumour risks
Small asymptomatic residual | Observation with serial MRI | Some regress
Not recommended | Hormonal treatment (anti-androgens) as primary therapy | 
!!
## Approaches by extent
!table Extent and approach
Extent | Approach
Nose and nasopharynx | Endoscopic, or transpalatal
Plus sinuses and pterygopalatine fossa | Endoscopic, lateral rhinotomy with medial maxillectomy, midfacial degloving, or Le Fort I
Plus infratemporal fossa | Extended endoscopic, infratemporal fossa approach, or maxillary swing
Plus cheek | Maxillary swing (facial translocation)
Plus intracranial extension | Combined endoscopic or open skull base surgery with neurosurgery; radiotherapy if inaccessible
Residual or recurrent | Observation, revision surgery, or radiotherapy
!!
!fig d49-8 | Angiofibroma exposed through a transpalatal approach.
### Endoscopic surgery
Now used for most tumours, including many with infratemporal and limited intracranial extension, with less bleeding and no facial scars; recurrence rates are similar to open surgery once tumour stage is accounted for.
### Open approaches
Transpalatal (with Sardana's sublabial extension), lateral rhinotomy and midfacial degloving with medial maxillectomy, the Le Fort I approach, the maxillary swing (Wei's facial translocation), the infratemporal fossa approach, and combined intracranial-extracranial resection.
!ops
## Radiotherapy
For unresectable intracranial disease, residual tumour next to critical structures, and recurrence; stereotactic radiosurgery for small residuals. Long-term risks include growth disturbance, cataract and radiation-induced malignancy in young patients.
!update Current evidence
**Endoscopic or open.** A systematic review of 1047 cases found that endoscopic resection had less blood loss (about 540 ml against 1580 ml) and fewer recurrences than open surgery, although the recurrence difference disappeared once tumour stage was accounted for (Boghani et al., *Laryngoscope* 2013). **Staging.** The UPMC system also predicts the need for staged surgery (Snyderman et al., *Arch Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2010). **Embolisation** 24 to 72 hours before surgery reduces blood loss in larger tumours; its value in small tumours removed endoscopically is debated. **Hormones** such as flutamide shrink some tumours but are not standard treatment.
!!

# Complications | Recurrence and treatment effects.
## Recurrence
Up to about a third, usually within two years, from residual tumour in the basisphenoid and vidian canal. Follow with contrast MRI for at least three years. Small residual tumour may be watched for growth; accessible recurrence is removed endoscopically, and disease in high-risk sites such as the cavernous sinus may be observed or treated with stereotactic radiosurgery.
## Of treatment
- **Embolisation:** stroke, blindness and cranial nerve palsy.
- **Radiotherapy:** growth disturbance, cataract and radiation-induced malignancy in young patients.
