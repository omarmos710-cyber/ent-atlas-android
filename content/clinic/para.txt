@stand Primary hyperparathyroidism used to be the disease of "bones, stones, abdominal groans and psychic moans". Today most patients are found by a raised calcium on a routine blood test. Usually one gland is at fault, a single adenoma, and a localised, focused operation cures it. The art is to confirm the biochemistry, localise the gland, and remember the exceptions: multigland disease, the ectopic gland and familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia.

# Applied anatomy | Four small glands, two journeys.
## Embryology
- **Superior parathyroids:**
  - Arise from the *fourth pharyngeal pouch*.
  - Travel a short distance with the lateral thyroid anlage, so their position is fairly constant.
- **Inferior parathyroids:**
  - Arise from the *third pouch* together with the thymus.
  - Descend further, so they are more variable: from the angle of the jaw to the mediastinum, often in the *thyrothymic ligament* or the thymus.
## The normal glands
- **Appearance:**
  - Each gland is about 5 × 3 × 1 mm and weighs about 40 mg.
  - Flat, bean- or leaf-shaped, and caramel, tan or mahogany in colour. Fat is paler and brighter yellow.
  - A gland "glides" within the surrounding fat when it is gently pushed (the *gliding sign*).
- **Number:** most people have four. About 5% have supernumerary glands.
- **Symmetry:** glands are usually mirror-images side to side, which helps in finding the opposite gland.
- **Superior gland:**
  - At the level of the cricothyroid joint, on the back of the upper thyroid lobe.
  - About 1 cm above the crossing of the RLN and inferior thyroid artery.
  - *Deep (dorsal) to the RLN*.
- **Inferior gland:**
  - Near the lower pole, within 1 to 2 cm of it.
  - *Superficial (ventral) to the RLN*.
- **Blood supply:** mostly from the inferior thyroid artery. This is why its branches are divided on the thyroid capsule at thyroidectomy.
- **Ectopic sites of adenomas:**
  - Retro-oesophageal and retrotracheal.
  - Anterior mediastinum (the thymus).
  - Within the thyroid.
  - In the carotid sheath.
  - Undescended, high in the neck.

# Calcium physiology | PTH and vitamin D.
## Parathyroid hormone
Parathyroid hormone (PTH) raises serum calcium in four ways:
- It mobilises calcium from bone (osteoclast activity).
- It increases renal tubular reabsorption of calcium and excretion of phosphate.
- It stimulates renal 1α-hydroxylation of vitamin D.
- Through vitamin D, it increases gut absorption of calcium.

Calcitonin from the thyroid C cells has only a minor opposing role in humans. The half-life of PTH is only a few minutes, which is what makes *intraoperative PTH monitoring* possible.
## Measuring calcium
- **Total calcium:** varies with albumin. Correct it by adding about 0.8 mg/dL (0.2 mmol/L) for each 1 g/dL fall in albumin below normal.
- **Ionised calcium:** used when albumin is abnormal.

# Hyperparathyroidism | Primary, secondary, tertiary.
## Primary hyperparathyroidism
- **Frequency:** about 1 in 500 women and 1 in 2000 men, mostly after the menopause.
- **Causes:**
  - *A single adenoma*: about 85%.
  - *Four-gland hyperplasia*: 10 to 15%. It may be sporadic, familial, or part of MEN 1 or MEN 2A.
  - *Double adenomas*: 2 to 5%, mostly in the elderly.
  - *Carcinoma*: under 1%.
- **Risk factors:** neck irradiation and lithium.
- **Biochemistry:**
  - High calcium with a raised or *inappropriately normal* PTH.
  - Low phosphate.
  - High or high-normal urinary calcium.
  - Vitamin D is measured and replaced if it is low.
## Clinical features
!table Manifestations of hypercalcaemia
System | Features
Bones | Pain, osteoporosis, fractures; osteitis fibrosa cystica, brown tumours (rare now)
Kidneys | Stones, nephrocalcinosis, polyuria and polydipsia, renal impairment
Gut | Constipation, nausea, peptic ulcer, pancreatitis
Mind and muscle | Fatigue, weakness, depression, poor memory, confusion
Other | Hypertension, short QT, band keratopathy, gout and pseudogout
!!
- **Hypercalcaemic crisis:** dehydration, confusion and arrhythmia when calcium is very high. Treat with:
  - Intravenous saline.
  - Bisphosphonates or denosumab.
  - Calcitonin.
  - Then urgent surgery.
## Differential diagnosis of hypercalcaemia
!table Causes of hypercalcaemia
PTH raised or normal | PTH suppressed
Primary hyperparathyroidism | Malignancy (PTH-related peptide from lung, breast, kidney cancers; bone metastases; myeloma)
Tertiary hyperparathyroidism | Granulomas: sarcoidosis, tuberculosis (excess vitamin D activation)
Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (low urinary calcium) | Vitamin D or A excess, milk-alkali syndrome
Lithium | Thyrotoxicosis, adrenal insufficiency, immobilisation, Paget disease; thiazides
!!
!pearl Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (FHH)
- **Cause:** an autosomal dominant mutation of the calcium-sensing receptor.
- **Biochemistry:** lifelong mild hypercalcaemia with normal or slightly raised PTH, and a *low urinary calcium* (calcium/creatinine clearance ratio under 0.01).
- **Course:** benign.
- **Surgery:** does not help. Always check the 24-hour urinary calcium before operating.
!!
## Secondary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism
- **Secondary:** a hyperplastic response of all glands to low calcium, mostly from *chronic kidney disease* (phosphate retention and low active vitamin D), or from vitamin D deficiency and malabsorption.
  - PTH is high and calcium is low or normal.
  - Medical treatment: phosphate binders, active vitamin D, and *calcimimetics* (cinacalcet).
  - Surgery for refractory disease (bone pain, pruritus, calciphylaxis): *subtotal (three-and-a-half gland) parathyroidectomy*, or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation into the forearm, plus thymectomy.
- **Tertiary:** autonomous hypersecretion that persists after the cause is corrected, typically after renal transplantation, with hypercalcaemia.
## Parathyroid carcinoma
- **Features:**
  - Very high calcium (often over 14 mg/dL) and PTH.
  - A palpable neck mass.
  - RLN palsy.
  - Both bone and renal disease at once.
- **At surgery:** a hard, grey-white, adherent gland invading the thyroid or muscle.
- **Treatment:** *en bloc* resection with the thyroid lobe and adjacent tissue, without rupturing the capsule.
- **Recurrence:** common. Death usually comes from uncontrollable hypercalcaemia.
## Indications for surgery
Parathyroidectomy is the only cure. It is recommended for all symptomatic patients. In asymptomatic patients it is recommended for any of the following:
- Serum calcium more than 1 mg/dL (0.25 mmol/L) above the upper limit of normal.
- Age under 50.
- Osteoporosis (T-score −2.5 or less) or a vertebral fracture.
- Creatinine clearance under 60 mL/min, kidney stones or nephrocalcinosis, or high urinary calcium.
- Patients who cannot be followed up.

Many surgeons also offer surgery to asymptomatic patients who are otherwise fit, because of neurocognitive and bone benefits.

# Localisation | Before a focused operation.
Localisation does not make the diagnosis; the biochemistry does. It is used to plan a focused operation and is essential before reoperation.
- **Ultrasound:**
  - Cheap and operator-dependent.
  - Shows a hypoechoic, oval nodule behind the thyroid, with a polar feeding vessel.
  - Poor for retrotracheal and mediastinal glands.
- **Sestamibi (technetium-99m MIBI) scintigraphy:**
  - Taken up by both thyroid and parathyroid, but *retained* by the mitochondria-rich adenoma after the thyroid has washed out.
  - Detects about 85 to 90% of single adenomas.
  - Less sensitive for small adenomas, double adenomas and hyperplasia.
  - SPECT and SPECT-CT give a three-dimensional position.
- **Four-dimensional CT:** shows the typical enhancement of an adenoma (arterial enhancement, early washout). Useful when the scans disagree and for reoperation.
- **Others:**
  - Choline PET-CT, which is increasingly used.
  - MRI.
  - Selective venous sampling for PTH, in difficult reoperations.
!flag Concordance matters
A *concordant* ultrasound and sestamibi scan localise a single adenoma in most patients and permit a focused approach. *Discordant* or negative scans suggest multigland disease or an ectopic gland. These patients need bilateral exploration by an experienced surgeon.
!!

# Surgery | Focused or bilateral.
## Approaches
- **Focused (minimally invasive) parathyroidectomy:**
  - A small incision over the localised gland.
  - The adenoma removed by controlling its vascular pedicle, keeping clear of the RLN.
  - *Intraoperative PTH* measured before and 10 minutes after excision. A fall of more than 50% from the highest value, into the normal range (the *Miami criterion*), confirms cure.
- **Bilateral neck exploration:**
  - All four glands are identified.
  - Used for non-localising scans, multigland disease, MEN and familial disease, and when PTH fails to fall.
- **Searching for a missing gland:**
  - *Superior gland*: in the tracheo-oesophageal groove and retro-oesophageal space, along the inferior thyroid artery.
  - *Inferior gland*: in the thymus and thyrothymic ligament (cervical thymectomy), and in the carotid sheath.
  - Then intrathyroidal ultrasound or lobectomy on that side.
  - Do not perform blind sternotomy at the first operation.
- **Hyperplasia:**
  - Subtotal parathyroidectomy, leaving a well-vascularised remnant of about 50 mg marked with a clip.
  - Or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation.
  - Thymectomy is added in MEN 1.

## Complications
- **Hypocalcaemia:**
  - Temporary hypocalcaemia is common.
  - *Hungry bone syndrome*: severe, prolonged hypocalcaemia with low phosphate and magnesium after removal of a long-standing adenoma with bone disease. It needs large doses of calcium and calcitriol.
  - Permanent hypoparathyroidism: about 1 to 5% after adenoma surgery, more after surgery for hyperplasia or secondary disease.
- **RLN palsy:** 0.4 to 4%.
- **Haematoma.**
- **Persistent or recurrent hyperparathyroidism.** Causes:
  - A missed adenoma or a second adenoma.
  - Unrecognised hyperplasia.
  - A supernumerary or ectopic gland.
  - Parathyromatosis from rupture of the capsule.
  - Carcinoma.
  - A wrong diagnosis, such as FHH.

# Hypoparathyroidism and hypocalcaemia | After thyroid and parathyroid surgery.
## Causes and features
- **Causes:**
  - Usually removal or devascularisation of the parathyroids during thyroidectomy or central neck dissection.
  - Rarely autoimmune, DiGeorge syndrome (absent third and fourth pouch derivatives), magnesium deficiency, or infiltration.
- **Features:** usually at 24 to 72 hours after surgery.
  - Perioral and fingertip tingling and cramps.
  - Carpopedal spasm and tetany.
  - Laryngospasm with stridor.
  - Seizures.
  - Confusion.
  - Prolonged QT and arrhythmia.
- **Signs:**
  - *Chvostek sign*: tapping the facial nerve in front of the ear makes the face twitch. About 5 to 10% of normal people have it, so record it before surgery.
  - *Trousseau sign*: carpal spasm with a blood-pressure cuff inflated above systolic for three minutes.
## Management
- **Monitoring:** measure calcium after total thyroidectomy. A PTH level a few hours after surgery predicts who will become hypocalcaemic and who can go home safely.
- **Mild symptoms:** oral calcium carbonate and calcitriol (alfacalcidol).
- **Severe symptoms or tetany:**
  - Intravenous 10% calcium gluconate, 10 to 20 mL over ten minutes, then an infusion with cardiac monitoring.
  - Correct magnesium.
- **Permanent hypoparathyroidism:** defined as lasting beyond six months. Lifelong calcium and active vitamin D, aiming for low-normal calcium to avoid hypercalciuria; recombinant PTH in selected cases.
- **Prevention:**
  - Capsular dissection.
  - Identifying and preserving the glands with their blood supply.
  - Autotransplanting any gland that is devascularised or removed by mistake.
  - Near-infrared autofluorescence to identify glands.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Embryology and anatomy:**
  - Fourth pouch superior glands, deep to the RLN.
  - Third pouch inferior glands, superficial to it.
  - Ectopic sites.
- **Biochemistry:** high calcium with non-suppressed PTH. Exclude FHH with urinary calcium.
- **Adenoma versus hyperplasia;** the MEN syndromes.
- **Localisation:** ultrasound, sestamibi, 4D CT; why localisation is not diagnosis.
- **Focused parathyroidectomy** and the intraoperative PTH fall.
- **Post-thyroidectomy hypocalcaemia:** signs and treatment.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Operating on familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia.
- Treating negative localisation scans as a reason not to operate on proven disease.
- Stopping after one adenoma in MEN 1, where all glands are abnormal.
- Rupturing the capsule of a suspected carcinoma or adenoma, which seeds parathyromatosis.
!!
