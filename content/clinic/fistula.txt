@stand A dizzy patient with an infected ear raises one question: has the labyrinth been breached, or inflamed? A fistula can be sealed, and serous labyrinthitis recovers; suppurative labyrinthitis destroys the hearing for good. After meningitis, the cochlea can turn to bone within weeks, and the window for a cochlear implant can close.

# Overview | When infection reaches the inner ear.
## Definitions
### Labyrinthine fistula
Erosion of the bony labyrinth, usually the lateral semicircular canal, exposing the membranous labyrinth without infecting it. Dhingra calls it *circumscribed labyrinthitis*.
### Serous labyrinthitis
Diffuse irritation of the labyrinth by bacterial toxins through the windows or a fistula, without bacteria in the inner ear. Reversible.
### Suppurative labyrinthitis
Bacteria and pus in the labyrinth: total, permanent loss of hearing and vestibular function.
### Perilymph fistula
A leak of perilymph through the oval or round window, after stapes surgery, barotrauma, diving, straining or trauma.
## Other forms
Viral labyrinthitis (mumps, measles, rubella, cytomegalovirus) and syphilitic labyrinthitis.

# Pathways | How infection gets in.
## From the middle ear
Cholesteatoma eroding the lateral canal (the commonest), through the oval or round window in acute or chronic otitis media, and after surgery or trauma.
## From the meninges
Bacteria spread from the subarachnoid space through the cochlear aqueduct or the internal auditory canal. *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae* and *Neisseria meningitidis* are the organisms; pneumococcus carries the highest risk of deafness, around 20%.
## Blood-borne
Viral and syphilitic infections.

# Labyrinthine fistula | The eroded lateral canal.
## Causes
Cholesteatoma in most cases, then tumours, trauma and surgery. About 7 to 10% of cholesteatomas have a fistula, 90% of them in the lateral canal.
!anat s65_1 | The bony labyrinth: the lateral semicircular canal bulges into the medial wall of the attic and antrum, where cholesteatoma erodes it.
## Clinical picture
Brief vertigo provoked by pressure on the ear: pressing the tragus, cleaning the ear, or nose blowing. Hearing is often preserved.
## Fistula test
Pressure applied to the canal (tragal pressure or a Siegle speculum) moves the endolymph through the fistula and causes vertigo and nystagmus. With positive pressure the fast phase usually beats towards the tested ear; with negative pressure, away from it.
!pearl The fistula test
It is specific but insensitive: positive in only 30 to 50% of fistulae. A negative test does not exclude one; it may also be negative in a dead labyrinth or if cholesteatoma seals the fistula. Assume a fistula in every cholesteatoma.
!!
## Other positive fistula tests
Ménière's disease (Hennebert's sign), superior canal dehiscence, perilymph fistula and congenital syphilis.
## Investigations
HRCT shows only about 60% of fistulae before surgery.
## Management
Surgery for the cholesteatoma; the matrix over a small fistula may be removed carefully at the end of the operation and the defect sealed with fascia or bone pâté; over a large fistula, or in an only hearing ear, the matrix is left and the ear exteriorised.

# Serous and suppurative labyrinthitis | Irritation or destruction.
## Serous labyrinthitis
### Clinical picture
Vertigo with nausea, nystagmus towards the affected ear (an irritative lesion), and some sensorineural hearing loss that recovers if treated.
### Treatment
Bed rest with the affected ear uppermost, full-dose antibiotics, labyrinthine sedatives, and myringotomy for a bulging drum. Mastoidectomy treats a chronic source once medical treatment has begun.
## Suppurative labyrinthitis
### Clinical picture
Severe vertigo with nausea and vomiting, nystagmus beating towards the healthy ear (the labyrinth has failed), total deafness in the ear, and a toxic patient. The vertigo settles over three to six weeks as the brain compensates.
### Danger
Infection can spread from the labyrinth to the meninges along the internal auditory canal.
### Treatment
Intravenous antibiotics, removal of the middle ear source, and rarely drainage of the labyrinth if it is feeding an intracranial infection.
!table Serous or suppurative?
 | Serous | Suppurative
Cause | Toxins | Bacteria
Nystagmus | Towards the affected ear | Towards the healthy ear
Hearing | Partial loss, recovers | Total, permanent loss
Patient | Unwell | Toxic, may have meningitis
Outcome | Recovery | Dead ear, ossification
!!
## Labyrinthitis ossificans
After suppurative labyrinthitis, especially meningitic, fibrous tissue and new bone fill the cochlea, starting in the basal turn near the round window, within weeks to months.
!flag Deafness after meningitis
- Test hearing in every patient after bacterial meningitis.
- Bilateral profound loss: image urgently (MRI and CT) for ossification and refer for early cochlear implantation.
- Steroids reduce hearing loss in *H. influenzae* and pneumococcal meningitis.
!!
!imaging | Labyrinthine fistula, labyrinthitis ossificans, labyrinthitis with meningitis, and otosyphilis.

# Perilymph fistula | A leak at the windows.
## Causes
Stapes surgery, barotrauma, diving, heavy lifting, straining or coughing, and head injury. A leak in an unoperated ear usually sits around the annular ligament or the round window membrane. Spontaneous fistula is controversial.
## Clinical picture
Fluctuating or sudden hearing loss, roaring tinnitus, disequilibrium or vertigo, often after the precipitating event, and a positive fistula test (Hennebert's sign). A penetrating ear injury with vertigo and hearing loss suggests stapes subluxation into the vestibule.
## Management
Bed rest for 7 to 10 days with the head raised, no straining, and stool softeners. Explore and patch the window if hearing is worsening or symptoms persist.

# Viral and syphilitic labyrinthitis | The other causes.
## Viral
Mumps (usually one-sided profound loss), measles, rubella and cytomegalovirus. CMV is the commonest non-syndromic cause of congenital sensorineural hearing loss, and the loss may progress after a normal newborn screen.
## Syphilis
Congenital or acquired. Late congenital syphilis may first appear at 8 to 20 years as bilateral sudden or fluctuating loss with vertigo, and Hennebert's sign. Test with VDRL or RPR and FTA-ABS; treat with penicillin and steroids.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## On call
!ward Vertigo in an infected ear
1. Examine for nystagmus and its direction; do a gentle fistula test.
2. Audiogram and HRCT.
3. Intravenous antibiotics; myringotomy for acute otitis media.
4. Plan surgery for cholesteatoma.
5. Hearing loss with meningitis: treat urgently and image for ossification.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Being reassured by a negative fistula test.
- Forgetting a hearing test after meningitis.
- Missing late congenital syphilis in a young adult with fluctuating bilateral loss.
