@stand A dizzy patient with an infected ear raises one question: has the labyrinth been breached, or inflamed? A fistula can be sealed, and serous labyrinthitis recovers; suppurative labyrinthitis destroys the hearing for good. After meningitis, the cochlea can turn to bone within weeks, and the window for a cochlear implant can close.

# Summary | When infection reaches the inner ear.
This topic covers the conditions in which infection, pressure or injury reaches the inner ear, each in its own chapter:
- [[@Labyrinthine fistula]] (*circumscribed labyrinthitis*): erosion of the bony labyrinth, usually the lateral semicircular canal, exposing the membranous labyrinth without infecting it.
- [[@Serous labyrinthitis]]: diffuse irritation of the labyrinth by bacterial toxins through the windows or a fistula, without bacteria in the inner ear. Reversible.
- [[@Suppurative labyrinthitis]]: bacteria and pus in the labyrinth: total, permanent loss of hearing and vestibular function.
- [[@Perilymph fistula]]: a leak of perilymph through the oval or round window, after stapes surgery, barotrauma, diving, straining or trauma.
- [[@Viral labyrinthitis]] (mumps, measles, rubella, cytomegalovirus) and [[@Syphilitic labyrinthitis]].

# Etiology | How infection gets in.
## From the middle ear
Cholesteatoma eroding the lateral canal (the commonest), through the oval or round window in acute or chronic otitis media, and after surgery or trauma.
## From the meninges
Bacteria spread from the subarachnoid space ([[t:icc]]) through the cochlear aqueduct or the internal auditory canal. *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae* and *Neisseria meningitidis* are the organisms; pneumococcus carries the highest risk of deafness, around 20%.
## Blood-borne
Viral and syphilitic infections.

# Management | Vertigo in an infected ear.
!ward On call: vertigo in an infected ear
1. Examine for nystagmus and its direction; do a gentle fistula test.
2. Audiogram and HRCT.
3. Intravenous antibiotics; myringotomy for acute otitis media.
4. Plan surgery for cholesteatoma.
5. Hearing loss with meningitis: treat urgently and image for ossification.
!!
!imaging | Labyrinthine fistula, labyrinthitis ossificans, labyrinthitis with meningitis, and otosyphilis.
!ops
## Drugs and doses
!table Labyrinthitis from ear disease: drugs and doses (adults)
Situation | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Acute otitis media with labyrinthitis | Ceftriaxone 2 g IV once daily (children 50 to 80 mg/kg, maximum 2 g, once daily per local policy) with myringotomy | Switch to oral when settled, about 10 to 14 days in total
Chronic otitis media or cholesteatoma source | Add anti-pseudomonal and anaerobic cover (for example piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g IV every 8 hours) | Then surgery to remove the source
Topical (discharging ear) | Quinolone drops (ofloxacin 0.3% 10 drops twice a day, or ciprofloxacin) | No aminoglycoside drops in an open ear
Severe vertigo and vomiting | Prochlorperazine 12.5 mg IM, or buccal 3 to 6 mg twice a day; then 5 mg three times a day by mouth | Few days only
With meningitis | Treat as bacterial meningitis, with dexamethasone 10 mg every 6 hours for 4 days | Reduces hearing loss in pneumococcal and *H. influenzae* disease
Perilymph fistula | Bed rest, head up, stool softener; no straining | 7 to 10 days, then explore if worse
Syphilitic labyrinthitis | Procaine penicillin 1.8 to 2.4 million units IM daily with probenecid 500 mg four times a day for 14 days (or doxycycline 200 mg twice a day for 28 days if allergic); prednisolone 40 to 60 mg daily for 3 days starting 24 hours before the penicillin | Treat as neurosyphilis with genitourinary medicine; a longer steroid course may be used for hearing
!!
Doses from the BNF, NICE NG240 (meningitis) and the BASHH UK guideline on syphilis (Kingston et al., *Int J STD AIDS* 2024). Local microbiology advice takes precedence.

# Labyrinthine fistula {dz} | The eroded lateral canal.
## Etiology
[[t:chole|Cholesteatoma]] in most cases, then tumours, trauma and surgery. About 7 to 10% of cholesteatomas have a fistula (5 to 10% in some reviews), 90% of them in the lateral canal.
!anat s65_1 | The bony labyrinth: the lateral semicircular canal bulges into the medial wall of the attic and antrum, where cholesteatoma erodes it.
## Clinical features
Brief vertigo provoked by pressure on the ear: pressing the tragus, cleaning the ear, or nose blowing. Hearing is often preserved.
## Diagnosis
### Fistula test
Pressure applied to the canal (tragal pressure or a Siegle speculum) moves the endolymph through the fistula and causes vertigo and nystagmus. With positive pressure the fast phase usually beats towards the tested ear; with negative pressure, away from it.
!pearl The fistula test
It is specific but insensitive: positive in only 30 to 50% of fistulae. A negative test does not exclude one; it may also be negative in a dead labyrinth or if cholesteatoma seals the fistula. Assume a fistula in every cholesteatoma.
!!
### Imaging
HRCT shows only about 60% of fistulae before surgery.
!update Current evidence
**Labyrinthine fistula.** High-resolution CT predicts most fistulas but misses some, so the surgeon should expect one when the patient has vertigo or a positive fistula test.
!!
### Investigations: what and when
!table Labyrinthine fistula and labyrinthitis: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
First (bedside) | Fistula test; direction of nystagmus; tuning forks | Positive test means a fistula; nystagmus direction separates serous from suppurative
Every patient | Pure-tone audiogram | Sensorineural component; dead ear in suppurative disease
Gold standard for a bony fistula | High-resolution CT of the temporal bones | Erosion of the lateral canal (most common)
Labyrinthitis, meningitis, deafness after meningitis | MRI with contrast and heavy T2 | Enhancement of the labyrinth; early fibrosis or ossification before cochlear implant
Syphilis suspected | Treponemal antibody (EIA) with RPR or VDRL; lumbar puncture with genitourinary medicine | Otosyphilis
!!
## Differential diagnosis
Other causes of a positive fistula test: [[t:meniere|Ménière's disease]] (Hennebert's sign), [[t:scd|superior canal dehiscence]], perilymph fistula and congenital syphilis.
## Management
Surgery for the cholesteatoma; the matrix over a small fistula may be removed carefully at the end of the operation and the defect sealed with fascia or bone pâté; over a large fistula, or in an only hearing ear, the matrix is left and the ear exteriorised. Whether to remove the matrix at the first operation or leave it for a second stage is debated; in experienced hands, removal with immediate sealing preserves hearing in most patients, and steroids are often given.

# Serous labyrinthitis {dz} | Irritation that recovers.
## Clinical features
Vertigo with nausea, nystagmus towards the affected ear (an irritative lesion), and some sensorineural hearing loss that recovers if treated.
## Management
Bed rest with the affected ear uppermost, full-dose antibiotics, labyrinthine sedatives, and myringotomy for a bulging drum. Mastoidectomy treats a chronic source once medical treatment has begun.

# Suppurative labyrinthitis {dz} | A dead ear.
## Clinical features
Severe vertigo with nausea and vomiting, nystagmus beating towards the healthy ear (the labyrinth has failed), total deafness in the ear, and a toxic patient. The vertigo settles over three to six weeks as the brain compensates.
## Management
Intravenous antibiotics, removal of the middle ear source, and rarely drainage of the labyrinth if it is feeding an intracranial infection.
## Complications
### Meningitis
Infection can spread from the labyrinth to the meninges along the internal auditory canal.
### Labyrinthitis ossificans
After suppurative labyrinthitis, especially meningitic, fibrous tissue and new bone fill the cochlea, starting in the basal turn near the round window, within weeks to months.
!flag Deafness after meningitis
- Test hearing in every patient after bacterial meningitis.
- Bilateral profound loss: image urgently (MRI and CT) for ossification and refer for early cochlear implantation ([[t:rehab]]).
- Steroids reduce hearing loss in *H. influenzae* and pneumococcal meningitis.
!!

# Perilymph fistula {dz} | A leak at the windows.
## Etiology
Stapes surgery, barotrauma, diving, heavy lifting, straining or coughing, and head injury. A leak in an unoperated ear usually sits around the annular ligament or the round window membrane. Spontaneous fistula is controversial.
## Clinical features
Fluctuating or sudden hearing loss, roaring tinnitus, disequilibrium or vertigo, often after the precipitating event, and a positive fistula test (Hennebert's sign). A penetrating ear injury with vertigo and hearing loss suggests stapes subluxation into the vestibule.
## Management
Bed rest for 7 to 10 days with the head raised, no straining, and stool softeners. Explore and patch the window if hearing is worsening or symptoms persist.

# Viral labyrinthitis {dz} | Mumps, measles, rubella, CMV.
## Clinical features
Mumps (usually one-sided profound loss), measles, rubella and cytomegalovirus.
## Congenital CMV
CMV is the commonest non-syndromic cause of congenital sensorineural hearing loss, and the loss may progress after a normal newborn screen ([[t:deaf]]).

# Syphilitic labyrinthitis {dz} | Congenital or acquired.
## Clinical features
Late congenital syphilis may first appear at 8 to 20 years as bilateral sudden or fluctuating loss with vertigo, and Hennebert's sign.
## Diagnosis
VDRL or RPR and FTA-ABS.
## Management
Penicillin and steroids. Syphilitic otitis media is covered in [[t:tbom]].
