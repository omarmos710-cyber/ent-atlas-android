@stand In acute mastoiditis the infection has broken out of the middle ear into the bone behind it. The typical patient is a small, unwell child with a red, tender swelling behind an ear that is pushed forward; the dangerous one is the patient on antibiotics whose ear has gone quiet while the bone is still being destroyed.

# Summary | The whole topic in one screen.
*Acute mastoiditis* is clinically evident infection of the mastoid process, a complication of acute otitis media that mostly affects small children. Pain, fever or discharge that persist or return during an otitis media, with a red, tender swelling behind an ear pushed forwards and downwards, are the classic picture. Diagnosis is clinical; contrast CT is kept for a suspected complication or failure to improve within 48 hours. Treatment is admission, an intravenous third-generation cephalosporin and a wide myringotomy, with cortical mastoidectomy for coalescence, an abscess or failure to respond. What makes the disease dangerous is its complications ([[10]]): abscesses around the mastoid and in the neck, facial palsy, labyrinthitis and intracranial infection.

# Epidemiology | Who gets it.
- **Age:** half of children with acute mastoiditis are four years old or younger.
- **Ear history:** only about 30% have a history of recurrent otitis media; often it is the first severe ear infection.
- **Frequency:** mastoiditis is rare. Avoiding antibiotics for uncomplicated otitis media raises its incidence only slightly in large population studies, so preventing it is not a reason to treat every ear infection.

# Etiology | What causes it.
## The underlying ear disease
Acute mastoiditis follows acute otitis media ([[t:om]]). The chronic form complicates an ear with a perforation, cholesteatoma or grommet ([[t:csom]], [[t:chole]]).
## Organisms
*Streptococcus pneumoniae* leads, with *Streptococcus pyogenes*, *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Haemophilus influenzae*. Pneumococcal serotype 19A is both resistant and virulent. Cultures are negative in many children who have already had antibiotics.
!differ Commonest organism
Dhingra: Beta-haemolytic streptococcus.
Lee: Streptococcus pneumoniae.
In practice: Cover *Streptococcus pneumoniae* first; more recent series find it the commonest.
!!

# Pathophysiology | From inflamed mucosa to destroyed bone.
## The mastoid in every otitis media
Every acute otitis media inflames the mastoid air cells to some degree, because the antrum is continuous with the middle ear; a CT in plain otitis media often shows an opaque mastoid. This alone is not mastoiditis.
## How the bone is destroyed
Two processes run together. Pus under pressure in the cells causes ischaemic necrosis of the thin bony septa, and inflamed mucosa activates osteoclasts that resorb bone (*hyperaemic decalcification*). The cells coalesce into an abscess cavity, which then looks for a way out.
!anat s60_30 s61_31 | Types of mastoid (cellular, diploic, acellular) and the groups of air cells through which infection spreads.
## Where the pus goes
The route the pus takes decides the complication: outwards through the cortex, down through the tip into the neck, inwards to the labyrinth and facial nerve, or up and back to the dura and sigmoid sinus. Each route is described under [[10]].
!fig d12-1 | Routes by which suppurative otitis media spreads to cause its complications.

# Clinical features | The child, the ear and the bone.
## Symptoms
- **Pain** behind the ear that persists, often after an apparently improving otitis media.
- **Fever** that persists or returns despite treatment.
- **Discharge** that becomes profuse and creamy, or persists beyond three weeks. Mucoid discharge must come from the middle ear, because the canal has no mucous glands.
!pearl The change is what matters
Pain, fever or discharge that continues or comes back during an acute otitis media points to mastoiditis.
!!
## Signs
### The mastoid
Tenderness is most telling over the tip and posterior border, rather than over the suprameatal (MacEwen's) triangle, which is tender in plain otitis media too. Always compare with the healthy side. Later the area becomes red, boggy and fluctuant.
### The pinna
Pushed forwards and downwards, with loss of the postauricular sulcus. This means a subperiosteal abscess has formed ([[10.1.1]]).
### The canal and drum
- **Sagging** of the posterosuperior canal wall, from periostitis over the antrum.
- **Reservoir sign:** the canal refills with pus straight after mopping.
- A pars tensa perforation with pulsatile discharge, sometimes with a *light-house* reflex.
- Hearing loss is conductive.
!fig d12-2 | Acute mastoiditis: the pinna is pushed forward and downward by the swelling behind it.
## Look for complications in every patient
Examine the facial nerve, balance, neck and neurology. A neck swelling, a facial weakness, vertigo or neurological signs mean a complication has begun ([[10]]).

# Diagnosis | Mainly clinical, confirmed by imaging.
## Approach
- Diagnosis is mainly clinical.
- **All patients:** full blood count, inflammatory markers and blood cultures; send pus from the ear or the myringotomy for culture.
- **Contrast CT of the temporal bones and brain** if:
  - an abscess or other complication is suspected, or
  - there is no improvement within 48 hours.
- **MRI with MR venography** if an intracranial complication or sinus thrombosis is suspected.
- CT is not needed in every child.
!pearl An opaque mastoid is not mastoiditis
Plain otitis media often opacifies the mastoid on CT. Mastoiditis is a clinical diagnosis; CT looks for coalescence and complications.
!!
## Laboratory tests
- **Full blood count:** raised white cells.
- **Inflammatory markers** and **blood cultures**.
- **Pus** from the ear or myringotomy for culture; often negative after antibiotics.
## Imaging
### Contrast CT of the temporal bones and brain
Shows coalescence (loss of the septa), cortical erosion and abscesses, and looks for intracranial spread.
!imaging | Otomastoiditis, petrositis and skull base infection on CT and MRI.
### MRI and MR venography
Better than CT for sigmoid sinus thrombosis and intracranial spread, so it is the test when either is suspected.

# Differential diagnosis | Other swellings behind the ear.
These are separate diseases that can look like mastoiditis. They are not its complications.
!table Swelling behind the ear
 | Distinguishing points
Furuncle of the canal | No preceding otitis media, painful tragus and pinna movement, normal drum, no mucoid discharge
Suppurating postauricular node | No ear history, superficial abscess, mastoid not tender
Otitis externa with nodes | Canal swollen and tender, drum normal
Infected sebaceous cyst | Superficial, punctum, ear normal
!!
The neck mass of a Bezold abscess has its own differential, given with the abscess ([[10.1.3]]).

# Subtypes and variants | The four forms.
!tree Mastoiditis
Acute | Acute mastoiditis | otitis media with mastoid inflammation
Bone loss | Coalescent | septa destroyed, pus in a single cavity
After antibiotics | Masked (latent) | slow bone destruction, few symptoms
Long-standing | Chronic | with perforation, cholesteatoma or grommet
!!
## Coalescent mastoiditis
The bony septa between the air cells have been destroyed, turning the cells into a single cavity full of pus. It is shown on CT and is an indication for cortical mastoidectomy ([[9.4]]).
## Masked (latent) mastoiditis
Repeated or inadequate antibiotic courses can sterilise the middle ear while bone destruction continues. The patient has little pain and no discharge, or only a mild ache or persistent otalgia. The drum is dull and thickened with lost landmarks, and hearing is reduced. It may hide an extradural or perisinus abscess ([[10.5]]).
!differ Symptoms of masked mastoiditis
Dhingra: Painless, without discharge; mild ache at most.
Lee: Persistent, chronic otalgia after antibiotics.
In practice: A patient whose ear never quite settles after antibiotics needs a CT.
!!
## Chronic mastoiditis
Mastoid infection in a long-standing ear disease, with a perforation, cholesteatoma or grommet ([[t:csom]], [[t:chole]]). Antibiotics then need anti-pseudomonal cover ([[9.2]]).

# Management | Admit, drain, and decide on surgery.
## Approach
!ward On call: acute mastoiditis
1. Admit; take blood cultures.
2. Start an intravenous third-generation cephalosporin; add anti-pseudomonal cover in a chronic ear.
3. Examine the facial nerve, balance, neck and neurology.
4. Image as in [[6.1]]: contrast CT for a suspected complication or no improvement in 48 hours; MRI and MRV if intracranial spread is possible.
5. Perform a wide myringotomy with a grommet, and send the pus for culture.
6. Call your senior for an abscess, coalescence, or no response at 24–48 hours.
!!
## Antibiotics
- **First choice:** an intravenous third-generation cephalosporin.
- **Chronic ear:** add anti-pseudomonal cover.
- **Adjust** according to culture.
- **Duration:** intravenous, then oral step-down, to complete three to six weeks in total.
## Myringotomy
A wide myringotomy with a grommet drains the middle ear and provides pus for culture. Uncomplicated acute mastoiditis without coalescence often settles with antibiotics and myringotomy alone.
## Surgery
### Indications for cortical mastoidectomy
- Subperiosteal abscess (in a small child, aspiration or incision may be tried first: [[10.1.1]]).
- Coalescence on CT.
- Sagging of the posterosuperior canal wall or a positive reservoir sign.
- No improvement after 48 hours of adequate treatment.
- Any complication, or cholesteatoma.
### The operation
Cortical (Schwartze) mastoidectomy removes the diseased cells and opens the antrum to the middle ear, leaving the canal wall and ossicles intact.
### In infants
The infant mastoid is small and vascular, landmarks are poor, and the facial nerve exits superficially.
!ops
!update Current evidence
**Conservative or surgical?** A systematic review of 21 studies (564 children) found cure rates of 96% with medical treatment, 96% with conservative surgery (myringotomy with or without grommet, abscess drainage) and 89% with mastoidectomy, supporting conservative first-line treatment for uncomplicated mastoiditis (Loh et al., *J Laryngol Otol* 2018). Immediate needle aspiration of a subperiosteal abscess, with myringotomy and intravenous antibiotics, avoided mastoidectomy in most children in an Israeli series (Bartov et al., *Otol Neurotol* 2019).
!!

# Complications | Where the infection goes next.
Mastoiditis is the gateway to the other complications of otitis media. Up to one in ten patients develop an intracranial complication, even on intravenous antibiotics. Everything in this chapter is a sequel of mastoiditis, not a different diagnosis.
!tree Complications of mastoiditis
Outwards | Abscesses around the mastoid | subperiosteal, zygomatic, Bezold, meatal (Luc), Citelli
Downwards | Deep neck abscesses | parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal
Inwards | Petrositis, facial palsy, labyrinthitis | via the petrous cells, the facial canal and the labyrinth
Upwards and back | Intracranial | extradural and perisinus abscess, sinus thrombosis and beyond
!!
## Abscesses around the mastoid
### Subperiosteal (postauricular) abscess
The commonest. Pus breaks through the cribriform area into the space under the periosteum over the mastoid, displacing the pinna forwards and downwards.

**Treatment:** cortical mastoidectomy. In a small child, a small abscess may be aspirated or incised, with myringotomy and antibiotics; this succeeds in most children in recent series, and cortical mastoidectomy is advised if it fails or the abscess recurs.
### Zygomatic abscess
Infection of the zygomatic air cells produces swelling in front of and above the ear, with oedema of the upper eyelid.
### Bezold abscess
In an adult or older child with a pneumatised tip, pus breaks through the thin medial side of the tip and tracks under the sternocleidomastoid, presenting as a tender upper neck mass.

**Differential of the neck mass:** upper jugular lymphadenitis, a parotid mass, an infected branchial cyst, parapharyngeal abscess and jugular vein thrombosis.

**Treatment:** cortical mastoidectomy, finding the fistula at the tip, and drainage of the neck through a separate incision.
### Meatal (Luc) abscess
Pus from the antrum tracks between the posterior canal wall and the periosteum, presenting as a swelling in the deep canal.
### Citelli abscess
Pus from the tip tracks along the posterior belly of digastric to form an abscess behind the mastoid or in the digastric triangle.
!fig d12-4 d12-5 | First, abscesses around the mastoid: postauricular, zygomatic and Bezold, with Citelli's abscess. Second, a Bezold abscess, where pus bursts through the medial side of the tip into the neck.
!fig d12-3 | A mastoid abscess that has burst through the skin.
## Deep neck abscesses
Parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal abscesses, reached via the tip cells or petrous cells ([[t:deepneck]]).
## Petrositis
Spread through the petrous cells to the petrous apex ([[t:petrous]]).
## Facial palsy and labyrinthitis
Facial palsy ([[t:facial]]) and labyrinthitis ([[t:fistula]]). Like any complication, each is an indication for cortical mastoidectomy ([[9.4]]).
## Intracranial complications
Extradural and perisinus abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis and the other intracranial infections ([[t:icc]]). Masked mastoiditis may hide them; MRI with venography is the test ([[6.3]]).

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Treating an opaque mastoid on CT as mastoiditis without clinical signs.
- Relying on suprameatal tenderness alone.
- Missing masked mastoiditis in a child whose otalgia never settled.
- Forgetting the superficial facial nerve when incising behind an infant's ear.
