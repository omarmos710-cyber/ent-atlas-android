@stand The drum is thin, and it records what happens around it. A traumatic hole usually heals by itself; a retracted drum is a sign of a failing Eustachian tube; and a sudden pressure injury can reach through the middle ear into the inner ear. In every case, check for vertigo, facial weakness and sensorineural hearing loss.

# Summary | Injuries and scars of the drum.
This topic covers four conditions of the tympanic membrane, each in its own chapter:
- [[3|Traumatic perforation]]: most heal on their own.
- [[4|Retraction and atelectasis]]: the drum sucked in by a failing Eustachian tube.
- [[5|Atrophy and tympanosclerosis]]: scars of past disease.
- [[6|Barotrauma]] of the middle ear: the locked tube.

# Applied anatomy | The normal drum.
A three-layered membrane: outer squamous epithelium, a middle fibrous layer, and inner mucosa. The fibrous layer gives it strength and shape; when it is lost after healing, the drum becomes thin and floppy. On otoscopy the normal right drum shows the handle of malleus, the lateral process and a bright cone of light at five o'clock.
!fig d8-17 | A normal right tympanic membrane, with the cone of light at five o'clock.

# Traumatic perforation {dz} | Most heal on their own.
## Etiology
- **Direct:** cotton buds, hairpins, matchsticks, careless syringing or instrument use.
- **Pressure:** a slap on the ear, blast injury, a fall onto water, a kiss on the ear.
- **Temporal bone fracture** ([[t:tbfx]]).
- **Thermal:** welding sparks or hot slag, which heal poorly.
## Clinical features
Sudden pain, a little bleeding, deafness and tinnitus. The perforation is irregular or slit-like, often with blood clot and with its edges turned inwards.
!fig d8-19 | A traumatic perforation from blast, with ecchymosis (white arrow head) and tears of the drum (black arrows).
!flag Look deeper
Vertigo, sensorineural hearing loss or facial weakness suggests stapes subluxation, perilymph fistula ([[t:fistula]]) or a temporal bone fracture. Arrange urgent assessment and consider exploration.
!!
## Diagnosis
Tuning forks, nystagmus and the facial nerve; an audiogram when practical. CT if a fracture is suspected.
!imaging | Ossicular luxation after trauma.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Traumatic perforation: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Diagnosis | Otoscopy or microscopy | Irregular perforation with clot and in-turned edges
Every patient | Tuning forks, check for nystagmus and facial movement | Sensorineural loss or vertigo means inner-ear injury
When practical | Pure-tone audiogram, repeated after healing | A gap over about 30 to 40 dB suggests ossicular injury
Suspected fracture or ossicular injury | High-resolution CT of the temporal bones | Fracture line, ossicular dislocation, pneumolabyrinth
!!
## Management
### Keep dry, keep out
No drops, no syringing and no water in the ear. Antibiotics only if the ear becomes infected.
### Repair
Most perforations heal within weeks. Large ones with in-turned edges may be repositioned and splinted with a paper patch under the microscope. Those that persist beyond about three months are repaired by myringoplasty ([[t:earsurg]]).
!ops
!update Current evidence
**Spontaneous healing.** In a prospective series of traumatic perforations, most healed spontaneously within three months; small perforations closed faster than large ones, and a dry ear healed better than a wet one (Lou et al., *Clin Otolaryngol* 2011). In a randomised trial of perforations with inverted or everted edges, healing rates were similar with observation, gelfoam patching, and edge approximation plus patching, but closure was faster with intervention (Lou et al., *Clin Otolaryngol* 2011).
!!
### Drugs and doses
!table Traumatic perforation: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Every patient | Analgesia; keep water out (cotton wool with petroleum jelly when washing) | Until healed
Not routine | Prophylactic antibiotics or drops | Most heal dry within 3 months
Ear becomes infected or contaminated (water, blast) | Quinolone drops: ofloxacin 0.3% 10 drops twice a day (from 12 years), or ciprofloxacin drops | 7 to 10 days; avoid aminoglycoside drops in an open ear
Vertigo or sensorineural loss | Urgent ENT review; exploration for fistula or stapes subluxation | 
Persistent after about 3 months | Myringoplasty | 
!!
Doses from the BNF and product licences.
## Complications
Persistent perforation, infection, ossicular dislocation, perilymph fistula, and implantation cholesteatoma from squamous epithelium driven into the middle ear.

# Retraction and atelectasis {dz} | The drum sucked in.
## Etiology and pathophysiology
When the Eustachian tube fails ([[t:et]]), middle ear gas is absorbed and negative pressure draws the drum inwards. Healed or atrophic areas, which lack the fibrous layer, retract first.
## Clinical features
- A dull drum with a broken or absent light reflex.
- A prominent lateral process of the malleus.
- A foreshortened handle of malleus.
- Prominent anterior and posterior malleolar folds.
!fig d8-18 | Features of a retracted tympanic membrane.
## Subtypes and variants: staging
!table Stages of pars tensa retraction
Stage | Appearance
I | Retracted, not touching the incus
II | Touching the incus
III | Lying on the promontory (atelectasis), can still be lifted
IV | Adherent to the promontory (adhesive otitis), no middle ear space
!!
!fig d8-20 d8-21 | First, an atrophic drum with a retraction pocket and an adhesion from the malleus. Second, atelectasis with retraction pockets in the attic (white arrow) and the pars tensa (black arrow).
## Management
- **Treat the cause** of tubal failure (adenoids, sinusitis, allergy).
- **Grommet:** re-aerates the middle ear while the drum can still be lifted (stage III).
- **Deep pockets** that collect keratin, or that cannot be cleaned, are excised and reinforced with cartilage.
!update Current evidence
**Retraction pockets.** Grommets, cartilage reinforcement or excision are used for progressive or unstable pockets, but there are no good randomised trials.
!!
## Complications
A deep pocket that collects keratin becomes a cholesteatoma ([[t:chole]]).

# Atrophy and tympanosclerosis {dz} | Scars of past disease.
## Atrophic drum
A thin, transparent segment without a fibrous layer, after healed perforations or long-standing effusion. It moves excessively and retracts easily.
## Tympanosclerosis
### Etiology and pathology
Chalky white hyaline and calcified plaques in the fibrous layer of the drum and sometimes in the middle ear around the ossicles, after recurrent otitis media or grommets.
### Clinical features
Plaques on the drum usually cause no symptoms; deposits around the ossicles can fix them and cause a conductive loss that is difficult to correct surgically.
!fig d8-22 | Tympanosclerosis with a central perforation: chalky plaques lie in front of and behind the hole.

# Barotrauma {dz} | Aero-otitis media.
## Etiology
A cold, allergy or sleeping through descent in an aircraft make it more likely.
## Pathophysiology
On descent in an aircraft or when diving, outside pressure rises. If the Eustachian tube does not open, middle ear pressure falls relative to it. Once the difference reaches about 90 mm Hg, the soft tissues at the pharyngeal end of the tube are pushed together and the tube *locks*, so swallowing and Valsalva can no longer open it.
## Clinical features
Severe pain, deafness, tinnitus and sometimes vertigo. The drum is retracted and congested, with haemorrhage into it, and later an effusion or blood (haemotympanum), or a perforation.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Barotrauma: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Diagnosis | Otoscopy; history of flight or dive | Retracted, congested drum; haemotympanum; perforation
Every patient | Tuning forks; check for nystagmus | Conductive loss; inner-ear involvement
Inner-ear symptoms | Pure-tone audiogram | Sensorineural loss means inner-ear barotrauma or fistula
Persistent effusion | Tympanometry | Flat (type B) trace
!!
## Management
- **Mild cases:** decongestant drops or spray, analgesia and gentle Valsalva.
- **Persistent effusion or failure:** politzerisation or myringotomy, and a grommet for recurrent problems.
### Drugs and doses
!table Barotrauma: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Pain | Paracetamol or ibuprofen | 
Decongestant spray (adults and 12 years and over) | Xylometazoline 0.1%, 1 spray each nostril up to three times a day; or oxymetazoline 0.05% | No more than 7 days (rebound congestion)
Decongestant by mouth (adults) | Pseudoephedrine 60 mg every 4 to 6 hours (maximum 240 mg a day) | Avoid in hypertension, heart disease, MAOI use, pregnancy
Eustachian tube opening | Autoinflation (Valsalva, nasal balloon) | Several times a day until clear
Perforation | As for traumatic perforation: keep dry | Most heal
Inner-ear barotrauma (vertigo, sensorineural loss) | Bed rest, head up, avoid straining; urgent ENT review | Possible perilymph fistula
Prevention before flying or diving | Decongestant spray about 30 minutes before descent | Do not dive with a cold
!!
Doses from the BNF and product licences.
## Prevention
!pearl Prevention
Avoid flying with a cold, use a decongestant spray before descent, stay awake on descent, and swallow, yawn or perform Valsalva repeatedly.
!!
## Complications
### Inner ear barotrauma
Forceful Valsalva against a locked tube can rupture the round or oval window, causing sensorineural loss and vertigo: a perilymph fistula ([[t:fistula]]) that may need exploration.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Syringing or putting drops into an ear with a fresh traumatic perforation.
- Missing a perilymph fistula in a patient with vertigo after a slap or a dive.
- Ignoring a deep retraction pocket until it becomes a cholesteatoma.
