@stand The drum is thin, and it records what happens around it. A traumatic hole usually heals by itself; a retracted drum is a sign of a failing Eustachian tube; and a sudden pressure injury can reach through the middle ear into the inner ear. In every case, check for vertigo, facial weakness and sensorineural hearing loss.

# Overview | Injuries and scars of the drum.
## The normal drum
A three-layered membrane: outer squamous epithelium, a middle fibrous layer, and inner mucosa. The fibrous layer gives it strength and shape; when it is lost after healing, the drum becomes thin and floppy. On otoscopy the normal right drum shows the handle of malleus, the lateral process and a bright cone of light at five o'clock.
!fig d8-17 | A normal right tympanic membrane, with the cone of light at five o'clock.

## Conditions covered
- Traumatic perforation.
- Retraction, atelectasis and retraction pockets.
- Atrophic drum and tympanosclerosis.
- Barotrauma of the middle ear.

# Traumatic perforation | Most heal on their own.
## Causes
- **Direct:** cotton buds, hairpins, matchsticks, careless syringing or instrument use.
- **Pressure:** a slap on the ear, blast injury, a fall onto water, a kiss on the ear.
- **Temporal bone fracture.**
- **Thermal:** welding sparks or hot slag, which heal poorly.
## Clinical picture
Sudden pain, a little bleeding, deafness and tinnitus. The perforation is irregular or slit-like, often with blood clot and with its edges turned inwards.
!fig d8-19 | A traumatic perforation from blast, with ecchymosis (white arrow head) and tears of the drum (black arrows).
!flag Look deeper
Vertigo, sensorineural hearing loss or facial weakness suggests stapes subluxation, perilymph fistula or a temporal bone fracture. Arrange urgent assessment and consider exploration.
!!
## Management
### Assess
Tuning forks, nystagmus and the facial nerve; an audiogram when practical. CT if a fracture is suspected.
### Keep dry, keep out
No drops, no syringing and no water in the ear. Antibiotics only if the ear becomes infected.
### Repair
Most perforations heal within weeks. Large ones with in-turned edges may be repositioned and splinted with a paper patch under the microscope. Those that persist beyond about three months are repaired by myringoplasty.
!ops
## Complications
Persistent perforation, infection, ossicular dislocation, perilymph fistula, and implantation cholesteatoma from squamous epithelium driven into the middle ear.
!imaging | Ossicular luxation after trauma.

# Retraction and atelectasis | The drum sucked in.
## Why it happens
When the Eustachian tube fails, middle ear gas is absorbed and negative pressure draws the drum inwards. Healed or atrophic areas, which lack the fibrous layer, retract first.
## Signs of a retracted drum
- A dull drum with a broken or absent light reflex.
- A prominent lateral process of the malleus.
- A foreshortened handle of malleus.
- Prominent anterior and posterior malleolar folds.
!fig d8-18 | Features of a retracted tympanic membrane.
## Retraction pockets and atelectasis
!table Stages of pars tensa retraction
Stage | Appearance
I | Retracted, not touching the incus
II | Touching the incus
III | Lying on the promontory (atelectasis), can still be lifted
IV | Adherent to the promontory (adhesive otitis), no middle ear space
!!
!fig d8-20 d8-21 | First, an atrophic drum with a retraction pocket and an adhesion from the malleus. Second, atelectasis with retraction pockets in the attic (white arrow) and the pars tensa (black arrow).
## Management
Treat the cause of tubal failure (adenoids, sinusitis, allergy). A grommet re-aerates the middle ear while the drum can still be lifted (stage III). Deep pockets that collect keratin, or that cannot be cleaned, are excised and reinforced with cartilage before they become cholesteatoma.

# Atrophy and tympanosclerosis | Scars of past disease.
## Atrophic drum
A thin, transparent segment without a fibrous layer, after healed perforations or long-standing effusion. It moves excessively and retracts easily.
## Tympanosclerosis
Chalky white hyaline and calcified plaques in the fibrous layer of the drum and sometimes in the middle ear around the ossicles, after recurrent otitis media or grommets. Plaques on the drum usually cause no symptoms; deposits around the ossicles can fix them and cause a conductive loss that is difficult to correct surgically.
!fig d8-22 | Tympanosclerosis with a central perforation: chalky plaques lie in front of and behind the hole.

# Barotrauma | Aero-otitis media.
## Mechanism
On descent in an aircraft or when diving, outside pressure rises. If the Eustachian tube does not open, middle ear pressure falls relative to it. Once the difference reaches about 90 mm Hg, the soft tissues at the pharyngeal end of the tube are pushed together and the tube *locks*, so swallowing and Valsalva can no longer open it. A cold, allergy or sleeping through descent make this more likely.
## Clinical picture
Severe pain, deafness, tinnitus and sometimes vertigo. The drum is retracted and congested, with haemorrhage into it, and later an effusion or blood (haemotympanum), or a perforation.
## Inner ear barotrauma
Forceful Valsalva against a locked tube can rupture the round or oval window, causing sensorineural loss and vertigo: a perilymph fistula that may need exploration.
## Treatment
Mild cases: decongestant drops or spray, analgesia and gentle Valsalva. Persistent effusion or failure: politzerisation or myringotomy, and a grommet for recurrent problems.
!pearl Prevention
Avoid flying with a cold, use a decongestant spray before descent, stay awake on descent, and swallow, yawn or perform Valsalva repeatedly.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Syringing or putting drops into an ear with a fresh traumatic perforation.
- Missing a perilymph fistula in a patient with vertigo after a slap or a dive.
- Ignoring a deep retraction pocket until it becomes a cholesteatoma.
!!
