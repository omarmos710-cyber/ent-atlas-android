@stand Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs inhibit cyclo-oxygenase and reduce prostaglandin-mediated pain and inflammation. Ibuprofen is the standard partner to paracetamol after tonsillectomy and other ENT surgery, and a systematic review found that NSAIDs (other than ketorolac) do not increase the risk of post-tonsillectomy bleeding requiring a return to theatre. Their harms are on the stomach, the kidney, the heart, and in some patients with asthma.

# At a glance | The drugs on one page.
## Summary
!table NSAIDs
 | Summary
Agents | Ibuprofen; naproxen; diclofenac; ketorolac (parenteral); celecoxib and etoricoxib (COX-2 selective)
How they work | Inhibit cyclo-oxygenase 1 and 2, reducing prostaglandins and thromboxane; analgesic, anti-inflammatory and antipyretic
ENT uses | Post-operative analgesia (tonsillectomy, ear, nasal, thyroid); acute otitis media and sore throat; inflammatory conditions (salivary, TMJ)
Adult dose | Ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily (maximum 2.4 g a day); naproxen 250 to 500 mg twice daily; diclofenac 50 mg two or three times daily (maximum 150 mg a day)
Child dose | Ibuprofen 5 to 10 mg/kg three or four times daily (maximum 30 mg/kg a day, up to 2.4 g), from 3 months and 5 kg
Avoid | Active peptic ulcer or bleeding; severe heart failure; severe renal impairment; aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease; third trimester of pregnancy; diclofenac in ischaemic heart, peripheral arterial or cerebrovascular disease; dehydration (renal injury); chickenpox (serious skin infection reported)
!!

# Using them in ENT | Practice.
## Tonsillectomy and bleeding
The Cochrane review and subsequent work show that ibuprofen and similar NSAIDs, given with paracetamol, give good analgesia after tonsillectomy without a significant increase in bleeding needing surgery, and reduce the need for opioids, which is particularly important in children with sleep apnoea. Many guidelines (including the AAO-HNS) support ibuprofen after tonsillectomy.
## Asthma
Most asthmatics tolerate NSAIDs. Those with **aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease** (asthma, nasal polyps, and reactions to aspirin or NSAIDs) must avoid COX-1 inhibitors; COX-2 selective agents are usually tolerated.

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
- Dyspepsia, peptic ulceration and **gastrointestinal bleeding**: give a PPI with long-term use or in the elderly.
- **Acute kidney injury**, particularly with dehydration, ACE inhibitors and diuretics (the "triple whammy").
- Fluid retention, hypertension, heart failure, and increased cardiovascular events (diclofenac and COX-2 inhibitors more than naproxen and low-dose ibuprofen).
- Bronchospasm in aspirin-sensitive asthma.
## Interactions
Warfarin, DOACs, SSRIs and steroids (bleeding); ACE inhibitors and diuretics (renal failure); methotrexate and lithium (toxicity).

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Withholding ibuprofen after tonsillectomy and prescribing codeine instead.
- NSAIDs in a dehydrated child who is not drinking.
