@stand A temporal bone fracture comes with a head injury, so trauma care comes first and the ear second. Once the patient is safe, three questions decide what happens next: is the face moving, can the patient hear, and is there CSF leaking?

# Overview | A fracture through the hearing bone.
## Epidemiology
Most fractures follow road traffic accidents, then assaults and falls. Young adult men in their second to fourth decades are affected about three times as often as women. A fracture is found in a substantial share of patients with significant head injury.
## Why it matters
The temporal bone contains the cochlea, the labyrinth, the facial nerve, the ossicles, the carotid artery and the dura. A fracture can damage any of them, and early documentation of facial function and hearing shapes every later decision.

# Classification | Two systems, both still taught.
## Traditional: by the direction of the fracture line
### Longitudinal fracture
About 80%. Usually from a blow to the side of the head. The line runs along the long axis of the petrous bone, through the squamous part, the posterosuperior canal wall, the tegmen and the middle ear, and tends to spare the otic capsule.
### Transverse fracture
About 20%. Usually from a blow to the back of the head. The line runs across the petrous bone, through the vestibule, cochlea or internal auditory canal.
### Mixed
Elements of both, common in severe injuries.

## Modern: does it involve the otic capsule?
*Otic capsule sparing* fractures are by far the commonest; *otic capsule violating* fractures, only about 6%, are much more likely to cause facial palsy, sensorineural hearing loss, CSF leak and meningitis. This system predicts outcome better.

# Clinical features | Look, listen, test the face.
## General
Signs of head injury and of a base of skull fracture: *Battle's sign* (bruising over the mastoid, appearing after a day or two), periorbital bruising, and altered consciousness.
## The ear
Bleeding from the ear with a torn drum or a step in the canal wall (longitudinal), or a blue-black drum from blood in the middle ear (*haemotympanum*) behind an intact drum (transverse).
## Hearing
### Conductive loss
From haemotympanum (the commonest early cause), a perforation, or ossicular injury: incudostapedial joint separation first, then dislocation of the incus, then fracture of the stapes crura.
### Sensorineural loss
From fracture through the cochlea or a labyrinthine concussion; often permanent. Mixed losses are common.
## Vertigo
Severe vertigo with spontaneous nystagmus after a transverse fracture through the labyrinth; milder, positional vertigo after concussion or BPPV.
## Facial nerve
Record whether the palsy was immediate or delayed, and complete or incomplete. An immediate complete palsy suggests transection or a bone spicule in the nerve; a delayed palsy suggests oedema and usually recovers.
## CSF leak
Clear fluid from the ear (CSF otorrhoea) through a torn drum, or from the nose (CSF otorhinorrhoea) via the Eustachian tube behind an intact drum.

# Investigations | Image the bone and confirm the fluid.
## High-resolution CT of the temporal bones
Shows the fracture line, the otic capsule, the ossicles, the facial canal and the tegmen. CT angiography if the fracture crosses the carotid canal.
!imaging | Skull base fractures, and dural prolapse with CSF leak.
## Beta-2 transferrin
Confirms that the fluid is CSF.
## Audiogram
As soon as practical, and repeated at six to eight weeks when blood has cleared from the middle ear.
## Electroneuronography
For a complete facial palsy, to guide decisions on exploration.

# Management | Trauma care first.
## The first assessment
!ward Head injury with ear signs
1. Follow trauma protocols and complete a neurological assessment.
2. Document facial function as early as possible, before sedation if you can.
3. Look for haemotympanum, a canal step, CSF, vertigo and nystagmus.
4. Do not syringe, probe or pack the ear deeply.
5. Arrange HRCT of the temporal bones, and CT angiography if the carotid canal is involved.
!!
## CSF leak
Most stop within a week or two with the head raised, bed rest, stool softeners and avoiding nose blowing and straining. Routine prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended. A persistent leak, or meningitis, needs a lumbar drain or surgical repair.
## Facial palsy
Immediate complete palsy with evidence of nerve injury is explored and decompressed or repaired. Delayed palsy is managed with steroids and observation, with serial ENoG (see the facial nerve topic).
## Hearing
Haemotympanum clears in weeks. A conductive loss persisting at six to eight weeks suggests ossicular injury and is explored and reconstructed. Sensorineural loss is rehabilitated with aids or, if profound, a cochlear implant.
!ops
## Vertigo
Short-term vestibular sedatives, then vestibular rehabilitation; treat post-traumatic BPPV with repositioning manoeuvres.

# Complications | Early and late.
- **Meningitis,** early or years later, through an unhealed fracture.
- **Permanent sensorineural deafness.**
- **Facial palsy.**
- **Late cholesteatoma,** from skin trapped in the fracture line.
- **Chronic vertigo** and perilymph fistula.
- **Carotid injury** and dissection.
!update Figures and late problems (Oxford Textbook)
The temporal bone is involved in 30 to 70% of head injuries, but only 14 to 22% of these produce a true fracture, mostly from road traffic accidents and falls. The old longitudinal–transverse scheme fails to describe the many mixed fractures, which is why *otic capsule-sparing* versus *otic capsule-violating* is now preferred: it predicts sensorineural loss, facial palsy and CSF leak better. Ossicular injury most often dislocates the incudostapedial joint (about 80%) and gives a conductive loss of at least 30 dB. About a quarter of fractures cause facial palsy (half of transverse and a tenth of longitudinal), and in about three-quarters of these the onset is delayed by one to sixteen days, which predicts a good recovery. A CSF leak complicates about 17%.

Bone of the otic capsule heals only by fibrous union, so capsule-violating fractures leave a weak barrier: CSF leaks can recur with coughing or straining, meningitis can occur years later, and meningoceles or encephaloceles may present long after the injury with a conductive loss, an effusion or meningitis. Patients with these fractures need pneumococcal vaccination and advice to seek help for fever and headache. Audiometry is best done two to three days after injury (after the patient wakes) and repeated; the Sunnybrook scale records synkinesis better than House–Brackmann during recovery.

!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Longitudinal versus transverse:** the six-row table.
- **Otic capsule sparing versus violating,** and why it predicts outcome.

- **Ossicular injuries** in order of frequency.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Forgetting to record facial function before the patient is sedated.
- Syringing or packing a bleeding ear after head injury.
- Assuming a conductive loss is permanent before the blood has cleared.
- Missing late meningitis or cholesteatoma years after the injury.
!!
