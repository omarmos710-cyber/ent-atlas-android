@stand Intranasal corticosteroids are the single most effective treatment for allergic rhinitis and the foundation of medical treatment for chronic rhinosinusitis, with or without nasal polyps. They work on congestion, rhinorrhoea, sneezing and itch, and on the eye symptoms of allergic rhinitis. The modern molecules have very low systemic bioavailability, but they only work if they are used every day and sprayed correctly, and most treatment failures are failures of technique.

# How they work | Mechanism and handling.
## Mechanism
They suppress both the early and late phases of the allergic response, though their effect on the early phase builds over days rather than minutes, which is why they work best taken regularly and started before the pollen season.
## Systemic bioavailability
The molecules differ greatly in how much of the swallowed fraction survives first-pass metabolism: **mometasone and fluticasone** have under 1% to 2% systemic bioavailability, **budesonide** about a third and **beclometasone** almost half. Choose the low-bioavailability agents for children, for long-term use, and for patients also on inhaled steroids.

# Using them in ENT | Indications and technique.
## Technique
Teach it at every visit:
- Blow the nose first; shake the bottle; prime a new bottle.
- **Head bent forward** (looking at the toes), not tipped back.
- **Use the opposite hand** (right hand for the left nostril) so the nozzle points **laterally**, away from the septum, toward the outer corner of the eye.
- Sniff gently, not hard (hard sniffing sends the dose to the throat).
## Allergic rhinitis
First-line for moderate to severe or persistent symptoms, alone or with an antihistamine; more effective than oral antihistamines for congestion.
## Chronic rhinosinusitis
Regular long-term use in every patient, in addition to saline irrigation; in polyposis, a higher dose, drops or rinses for better distribution (see steroid drops and irrigations), and short courses of oral steroids for exacerbations. After sinus surgery the opened cavities let sprays reach the sinuses better.

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
- **Epistaxis** and crusting (up to 1 in 10), mostly from spraying onto the septum; rarely **septal perforation**.
- Dryness, irritation, headache, taste and smell disturbance.
- Systemic effects are rare with mometasone and fluticasone; small reductions in growth velocity have been seen with beclometasone in children. Adrenal suppression is possible when high nasal doses are combined with inhaled and other steroids.
## Interactions
**Ritonavir and cobicistat** (and other strong CYP3A4 inhibitors such as itraconazole) markedly raise fluticasone levels, causing Cushing syndrome and adrenal suppression: use beclometasone or budesonide with care, or avoid.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Mechanism and **onset**, and why regular use matters.
- **Bioavailability** differences between agents and which to choose in children.
- Correct **technique** (head down, opposite hand, lateral aim).
- The **ritonavir** interaction.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Labelling a patient a treatment failure without checking technique and adherence.
- Spraying at the septum, producing bleeding and crusting.
- Using beclometasone long term in a small child.
!!
