@stand Thyroid nodules are everywhere: one in twenty adults can feel one, and ultrasound finds them in half of older people. The task is to find the small fraction that are cancers without operating on everyone. The tools are history, ultrasound pattern, and ultrasound-guided FNA reported in Bethesda categories. Most thyroid cancers are papillary and have an excellent outlook. The surgeon's measure is two working recurrent laryngeal nerves and four living parathyroids at the end of the operation.

# Applied anatomy | The gland and the nerves around it.
## Embryology
The medial anlage starts at the *foramen caecum* of the tongue at about four weeks. It descends in front of the hyoid and larynx along the *thyroglossal tract* and reaches the root of the neck by seven weeks.
- **Remnants:**
  - Arrested descent gives a *lingual thyroid*, often the only thyroid tissue the patient has.
  - The lowest part of the tract persists as the *pyramidal lobe*, present in up to 40%.
  - Tract remnants form *thyroglossal cysts*.
- **Lateral contributions:** the lateral anlagen (fourth and fifth pouches) and the *ultimobranchial bodies* join the descending gland. The ultimobranchial bodies bring the neural-crest *C cells*, which concentrate in the upper and middle thirds of the lobes, where medullary cancers arise.
## The gland
- **Size and position:**
  - Two lobes joined by an isthmus over the second to fourth tracheal rings.
  - Weight 15 to 25 g. Each lobe is about 5 × 3 × 2 cm.
- **Capsules:** the *true capsule* sends septa into the gland. The *false capsule* (perithyroidal sheath) is formed by the visceral (pretracheal) layer of deep cervical fascia.
- **Suspensory ligaments:**
  - The *anterior* ligament runs from the isthmus to the trachea.
  - The *posterior* ligament, the **ligament of Berry**, binds the back of each lobe to the cricoid and first tracheal rings. This is why the gland rises on swallowing.
  - The RLN runs deep to or through Berry's ligament, beside small branches of the inferior thyroid artery. This is the commonest site of nerve injury, and thyroid tissue left here explains bed uptake and measurable thyroglobulin after "total" thyroidectomy.
- **Arteries:**
  - Superior thyroid artery: the first branch of the external carotid.
  - Inferior thyroid artery: from the thyrocervical trunk. It passes behind the carotid sheath and supplies the parathyroids.
  - Sometimes a *thyroidea ima* arises from the brachiocephalic trunk or the arch.
- **Veins:**
  - Superior and middle veins drain to the internal jugular. The middle vein has no companion artery and is the first vessel divided at surgery.
  - Inferior veins form a plexus that drains to the brachiocephalic veins.
- **Lymphatics:**
  - First to the central compartment, level VI: prelaryngeal (*Delphian*), pretracheal and paratracheal nodes.
  - Then to the superior mediastinum (level VII) and the lateral neck (levels II to IV, sometimes V).

## Nerves at risk
- **Recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN):** motor to all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid, and sensory below the folds.
  - The *right* RLN hooks round the subclavian artery and runs obliquely, lateral to medial.
  - The *left* RLN hooks round the aortic arch and runs more vertically in the tracheo-oesophageal groove.
  - It crosses the branches of the inferior thyroid artery, superficial to them, deep to them or between them.
  - It branches before entering the larynx in about a third of people.
  - It enters the larynx behind the inferior cornu of the thyroid cartilage.
  - It is found in the *RLN triangle* (trachea medially, retracted strap muscles laterally, lower pole above), or near the *tubercle of Zuckerkandl*.
- **Non-recurrent laryngeal nerve:**
  - Occurs in about 0.5 to 1% on the right, almost never on the left.
  - It runs directly from the vagus to the larynx.
  - It goes with an aberrant retro-oesophageal right subclavian artery (the cause of *dysphagia lusoria*).
- **External branch of the superior laryngeal nerve:** motor to cricothyroid, which tenses the cord for high pitch. It runs on the inferior constrictor close to the superior pole vessels and crosses them within 1 cm of the pole in about a fifth of people. Ligate the superior vessels individually, on the capsule, with the gland pulled down.
- **Ansa cervicalis:** supplies the strap muscles from below, so straps are divided high if needed.

## Lingual thyroid
- **Frequency:** about 1 in 3000 to 4000 patients with thyroid disease, mostly women.
- **Presentation:** a smooth, vascular mass at the tongue base. It may cause dysphagia, dysphonia, airway obstruction or bleeding, particularly at puberty or in pregnancy.
- **Before any treatment:** do a scintiscan and ultrasound of the neck to find whether other thyroid tissue exists.
- **Treatment:**
  - Thyroxine to shrink it.
  - Radioiodine ablation.
  - For obstruction, excision through a transoral or suprahyoid transpharyngeal approach. Lifelong replacement follows if it was the only thyroid.

# Physiology and function tests | Hormones and the axis.
## Hormone synthesis
- **Cell types:**
  - *Follicular cells* make thyroglobulin, thyroxine (T4) and tri-iodothyronine (T3).
  - *Parafollicular C cells* make calcitonin.
- **Synthesis, step by step:**
  1. Iodide is actively trapped.
  2. *Thyroid peroxidase* oxidises it and attaches it to tyrosine residues on thyroglobulin, forming MIT and DIT.
  3. Thyroid peroxidase couples them: DIT + DIT forms T4, and MIT + DIT forms T3.
  4. The hormones are stored as colloid.
  5. Colloid is taken back into the cell, thyroglobulin is cleaved, and the hormones are released.
  6. Uncoupled MIT and DIT are deiodinated so that their iodine is recycled.
- **T4 and T3 compared:**
  - The gland releases mostly T4 (half-life about a week).
  - Most T3 (several times more potent, half-life about a day) is made by peripheral deiodination.
  - Over 99% of circulating hormone is protein-bound.
- **The axis:**
  - Hypothalamic TRH stimulates pituitary TSH.
  - TSH drives hormone synthesis, gland growth and vascularity.
  - T4 and T3 feed back negatively.
!fig d66-4 d66-5 | First, thyroid follicles with follicular and parafollicular C cells. Second, the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis.
## Tests and terms
- **TSH:** the single best screening test. It is high in primary hypothyroidism and suppressed in hyperthyroidism.
  - Subclinical hypothyroidism: high TSH with normal T4.
  - Subclinical hyperthyroidism: low TSH with normal T4 and T3.
- **Free T4 and T3:** total levels vary with thyroid-binding globulin, which is high in pregnancy and with the pill.
- **Antibodies:**
  - TPO (antimicrosomal) antibodies are present in almost all Hashimoto disease and most Graves disease.
  - Thyroglobulin antibodies are found in autoimmune thyroiditis and interfere with thyroglobulin assays.
  - *TSH-receptor antibodies* (stimulating) cause Graves disease.
- **Thyroglobulin:** made only by thyroid tissue. It is useless for diagnosing a nodule. After total thyroidectomy and ablation it should be undetectable, so a rising level means recurrence.
- **Calcitonin and CEA:** markers of medullary carcinoma.
- **Imaging the thyroid:**
  - *Ultrasound* is the essential study.
  - *Scintigraphy* with technetium or I-123 shows hot and cold nodules.
  - *CT* (non-iodinated contrast if radioiodine may follow) is for retrosternal extension and invasion.

# Benign disease | Function and inflammation.
## Hypothyroidism
- **Causes:**
  - Iodine deficiency, the commonest cause worldwide.
  - *Hashimoto disease*, the commonest where iodine is sufficient.
  - Thyroidectomy and radioiodine.
  - Neck irradiation: almost half of patients after laryngeal or pharyngeal radiotherapy. Check TSH routinely.
  - Drugs: lithium, amiodarone, antithyroid drugs.
  - Inborn defects.
  - Central (pituitary) failure, which is rare.
- **Features:** fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, dry coarse skin and hair, constipation, menorrhagia, slow thinking, bradycardia, non-pitting *myxoedema*.
- **ENT features:** a hoarse, low voice from oedematous cords, a large tongue, sensorineural hearing loss, and serous otitis.
- **Congenital hypothyroidism (cretinism):** stunted growth, intellectual disability and deafness. Neonatal screening prevents it.
- **Treatment:** levothyroxine. Start low in the elderly and in heart disease.
- **Anaesthesia risk:** *myxoedema coma* can be precipitated by anaesthesia and sedatives in untreated patients.
## Hyperthyroidism
- **Features:** weight loss with good appetite, heat intolerance, sweating, tremor, palpitations, atrial fibrillation, diarrhoea, anxiety, proximal myopathy, oligomenorrhoea.
- **Causes:**
  - Graves disease, the commonest.
  - Toxic multinodular goitre, the commonest cause in the elderly.
  - Toxic adenoma.
  - Thyroiditis, which is transient.
  - Excess iodine (the *Jod–Basedow phenomenon* after contrast in a nodular goitre).
  - Exogenous hormone.
  - Rarely, a TSH-secreting pituitary tumour, struma ovarii or functioning cancer.

## Graves disease
An autoimmune disease in which stimulating TSH-receptor antibodies drive the gland. Women are affected five to ten times more often than men, usually in the third and fourth decades.
- **Features:** a diffuse vascular goitre with a bruit, thyrotoxicosis, *ophthalmopathy* (lid retraction, proptosis, diplopia, optic neuropathy), and dermopathy (pretibial myxoedema).
- **Treatment options:**
  - *Antithyroid drugs*:
    - Carbimazole or methimazole are first line.
    - Propylthiouracil is used in the first trimester; it carries a risk of liver failure.
    - All carry a risk of agranulocytosis: warn the patient to report a sore throat.
    - Relapse after stopping is common.
  - *Beta-blockers* control symptoms.
  - *Radioiodine*:
    - Definitive, but usually leads to hypothyroidism.
    - Avoided in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Conception is deferred for at least six months.
    - It can worsen eye disease, especially in smokers.
  - *Total thyroidectomy*. Indications:
    - Drug failure or side effects.
    - A large goitre, or nodules.
    - Active eye disease.
    - Pregnancy planning.
    - Patient preference.
- **Preparing for surgery:** the patient must be euthyroid on drugs to avoid *thyroid storm*. Lugol iodine for about ten days reduces vascularity (the Wolff–Chaikoff effect).
- **Graves orbitopathy:** in severe cases, orbital decompression by an endoscopic transnasal route.
!imaging orbital-decompression-in-graves-disease | Endoscopic orbital decompression in Graves disease.
## Thyroiditis
- **Hashimoto (chronic lymphocytic) thyroiditis:**
  - The commonest thyroid disease where iodine is sufficient, mostly in women.
  - A painless, firm, rubbery, symmetrical goitre.
  - TPO antibodies are raised. Patients become hypothyroid over time.
  - Histology: lymphoid follicles and Hürthle cell change.
  - Treat with thyroxine.
  - A discrete nodule still needs FNA. A rapidly growing mass raises the possibility of *lymphoma*.
- **Subacute granulomatous (de Quervain) thyroiditis:**
  - The commonest painful thyroid. It follows a viral infection.
  - Neck pain radiates to the jaw and ear. Fever, a tender gland and a high ESR.
  - A transient toxic phase with very low isotope uptake, then a hypothyroid phase, then recovery.
  - Treat with NSAIDs, steroids and beta-blockers.
- **Silent and postpartum thyroiditis:** painless and transient, but sometimes leads to permanent hypothyroidism.
- **Acute suppurative thyroiditis:**
  - Rare. Bacterial, with a tender, red swelling and sometimes an abscess.
  - In children it is usually on the *left*, from a *pyriform sinus fistula* (a third or fourth branchial remnant).
  - Treat with antibiotics and drainage, then look for the fistula with barium swallow, CT or endoscopy, and excise it or cauterise its opening.
- **Riedel thyroiditis:**
  - Very rare. A woody, fixed, non-tender gland from fibrosis spreading out of the gland, part of the IgG4-related spectrum.
  - Causes compression and RLN palsy, and mimics anaplastic carcinoma or lymphoma.
  - Treatment: biopsy, often by isthmusectomy, which also relieves tracheal compression. Steroids or tamoxifen.
## Goitre
Enlargement of the gland, diffuse or nodular.
- **Causes:** endemic iodine deficiency, goitrogens, inherited defects of hormone synthesis (*Pendred syndrome*: goitre with sensorineural deafness and an enlarged vestibular aqueduct), puberty and pregnancy, autoimmunity, and nodular hyperplasia.
- **Multinodular goitre:** evolves slowly and may become toxic. A sudden painful enlargement means haemorrhage into a nodule. About 5 to 10% harbour a carcinoma, usually a small papillary one.
- **Pressure symptoms:**
  - Cough, choking, positional or nocturnal breathlessness.
  - Stridor and dysphagia.
  - Superior vena caval obstruction, with a *positive Pemberton sign*: raising the arms causes facial plethora, distended neck veins and breathlessness.
- **Assessment:**
  - TSH.
  - Ultrasound, with FNA of suspicious or dominant nodules.
  - Laryngoscopy.
  - CT for retrosternal extension and tracheal compression. Flow–volume loops if needed.
- **Indications for surgery:**
  - Compression.
  - Substernal extension (tissue that can no longer be examined or sampled).
  - Suspicion of cancer.
  - Toxicity.
  - Cosmesis.
- **Retrosternal goitre:** almost always removed through the neck. Sternotomy is rarely needed, mainly for a primary mediastinal goitre or one extending below the aortic arch.
- **Endemic goitre:** prevented by iodised salt.

# The thyroid nodule | Finding the cancer.
## Causes
- **Benign:**
  - Colloid (adenomatous) nodule in a nodular goitre.
  - Follicular or Hürthle cell adenoma.
  - Cyst.
  - Focal thyroiditis or a regenerative nodule.
  - Toxic adenoma.
- **Malignant:**
  - Carcinoma, about 5 to 15% of nodules.
  - Rarely, a metastasis or lymphoma.
- **Masses that only look thyroid:** a node, a parathyroid cyst, a thyroglossal cyst, a laryngocele.
!table Clinical features raising concern for cancer
Lower concern | Higher concern
Long-standing, stable nodule | Age under 20 to 30 or over 60 years; male
Multinodular gland without a dominant nodule | Childhood neck irradiation; family history of thyroid cancer or MEN 2
Hashimoto or toxic features | Rapid growth, pain; hard, fixed nodule; size over 4 cm
Soft, mobile | Cervical lymphadenopathy; vocal fold palsy; stridor, dysphagia
!!
!flag Examine the larynx in every thyroid patient
A paralysed vocal fold can coexist with a normal voice. Preoperative laryngoscopy finds unsuspected palsy, which suggests invasive cancer and changes the surgical plan. It also documents the baseline before surgery.
!!
## Investigation
1. **TSH first:**
   - Low TSH: do a scintiscan. A hot nodule is almost never malignant and is not aspirated.
   - Normal or high TSH: go to ultrasound and FNA.
2. **Ultrasound of the thyroid and neck for everyone.** The pattern sets the risk:
   - *High suspicion*: a solid hypoechoic nodule with microcalcifications, irregular or infiltrative margins, a taller-than-wide shape, or extrathyroidal extension.
   - *Intermediate*: solid and hypoechoic with smooth margins.
   - *Low*: iso- or hyperechoic, or partly cystic with an eccentric solid part.
   - *Very low*: spongiform.
   - *Benign*: purely cystic.
   - Suspicious nodes show microcalcification, cystic change, a lost fatty hilum, a round shape and peripheral vascularity.
3. **Ultrasound-guided FNA:**
   - Nodules of 1 cm or more with high or intermediate suspicion.
   - 1.5 cm or more with low suspicion.
   - 2 cm or more with very low suspicion (or observe).
   - Pure cysts: none.
   - Also suspicious nodes, with thyroglobulin measured in the needle washout.
4. **Calcitonin** if there is a family history of medullary cancer or MEN 2.
5. **CT or MRI** for retrosternal extension, invasion or large nodal disease.
!table The Bethesda system for thyroid cytology
Category | Risk of cancer | Usual management
I. Non-diagnostic | 5 to 10% | Repeat ultrasound-guided FNA
II. Benign | 0 to 3% | Clinical and ultrasound follow-up
III. Atypia of undetermined significance (AUS/FLUS) | 10 to 30% | Repeat FNA, molecular testing, or lobectomy
IV. Follicular neoplasm (including Hürthle cell) | 25 to 40% | Molecular testing or diagnostic lobectomy

VI. Malignant | 97 to 99% | Surgery
!!
!pearl Why FNA cannot diagnose follicular carcinoma
Follicular carcinoma is diagnosed by *capsular or vascular invasion*. That means examining the whole capsule histologically, which an aspirate cannot do. A "follicular neoplasm" therefore needs a diagnostic lobectomy, and completion thyroidectomy if invasion is found. Hürthle cell neoplasms behave the same way. Molecular panels (BRAF, RAS, RET/PTC, PAX8/PPARγ, gene-expression classifiers) help sort indeterminate cytology.
!!

## Cystic nodules
About a quarter of nodules are wholly or partly cystic.
- **Aspirate colour:**
  - Brown fluid: old haemorrhage into a colloid nodule.
  - Water-clear fluid: a *parathyroid cyst*. Confirm with PTH in the fluid.
  - Bloody fluid that reaccumulates: raises suspicion of cystic papillary carcinoma. Aspirate the solid part under ultrasound.
- **Management:** cysts that recur repeatedly or exceed about 4 cm are excised. Ethanol ablation is an alternative for benign recurrent cysts.
!fig d66-7 | Management of a thyroid cyst according to the aspirate.
## Benign nodules
- **Follow-up:** examination and ultrasound at intervals. Repeat FNA if a nodule grows significantly or changes pattern.
- **TSH suppression:** with thyroxine it is little used because it is unpredictable and harms bone and heart.
- **Toxic adenoma:** treated by lobectomy or radioiodine.
- **Surgery for benign nodules:** for compression or cosmesis. Radiofrequency or laser ablation are options for selected nodules.

## Papillary carcinoma
The commonest type, and increasingly common because more small tumours are detected. Usually in women of 30 to 50 years. Also the usual childhood thyroid cancer, often with nodes and lung spread, but still an excellent prognosis.
- **Risk factors:**
  - *Ionising radiation* in childhood: old low-dose treatment of tonsils, acne and thymus, radiotherapy for Hodgkin disease, Chernobyl and atomic fallout. Latency is decades.
  - Family history.
  - Familial adenomatous polyposis, Gardner syndrome and Cowden syndrome.
  - Adequate iodine intake.
- **Pathology:**
  - Papillae with characteristic nuclei: ground-glass "*Orphan Annie eye*" nuclei, grooves and intranuclear inclusions.
  - *Psammoma bodies*.
  - Often multifocal.
  - The diagnosis is made on cytology.
  - Aggressive variants: tall cell, columnar, diffuse sclerosing and hobnail.
  - BRAF V600E is the commonest mutation and is associated with more aggressive behaviour.
- **Spread:**
  - Lymphatic: clinically evident nodes in about 30% of adults and many more children. Nodal deposits may be cystic and are sometimes the presenting lump, behind an occult primary.
  - Distant spread is uncommon.
- **Prognosis:** ten-year survival over 90%. Prognosis is worse with older age, male sex, larger size, extrathyroidal extension and distant metastases. Risk scores: *AGES* (age, grade, extent, size) and *AMES* (age, metastases, extent, size).
- **Papillary microcarcinoma (1 cm or less):** low-risk tumours may be watched by active surveillance or treated by lobectomy.
## Follicular carcinoma
- **Who:** an older age group (around 50) than papillary carcinoma, mostly women, and commoner in iodine-deficient areas.
- **Pathology:** usually unifocal. Diagnosis needs capsular or vascular invasion on histology.
  - *Minimally invasive* tumours behave well.
  - *Widely invasive* ones do not.
- **Spread:** by *blood* to lung and bone. Distant metastases are present in up to 15% at diagnosis. Nodal spread is uncommon.
- **Molecular changes:** RAS mutations and the PAX8/PPARγ rearrangement.
- **Prognosis:** worse with age over 50, size over 4 cm and distant disease.
## Hürthle cell (oncocytic) carcinoma
Large eosinophilic cells packed with mitochondria. More aggressive than follicular carcinoma: more often multifocal, with nodal and distant spread. It takes up *radioiodine poorly*, so surgery is all-important.
## Medullary carcinoma
From C cells. It secretes *calcitonin* and CEA, which are used for diagnosis and follow-up. Histology shows amyloid stroma. It is equally common in both sexes.
- **Sporadic form (about 75 to 80%):** unifocal, in the fifth decade.
- **Familial form (20 to 25%):**
  - Autosomal dominant germline *RET* mutations.
  - Preceded by C-cell hyperplasia.
  - Bilateral and multifocal.
  - Occurs in MEN 2A, MEN 2B, and familial medullary carcinoma (now counted as a variant of MEN 2A).
- **Clinical behaviour:** early spread to central and lateral nodes, then lung, liver and bone. Diarrhoea and flushing from hormone secretion.
- **Work-up:**
  - Calcitonin and CEA.
  - RET testing for *every* patient.
  - Exclude *phaeochromocytoma* (plasma or urinary metanephrines) before any surgery, and remove it first to avoid a hypertensive crisis.
  - Serum calcium for hyperparathyroidism.
- **Treatment:**
  - Total thyroidectomy with central (level VI) neck dissection.
  - Lateral neck dissection for nodal disease, or according to calcitonin level.
  - Radioiodine does not work.
  - Targeted RET and multikinase inhibitors (selpercatinib, vandetanib, cabozantinib) for progressive metastatic disease.
- **Prophylactic thyroidectomy in RET carriers:**
  - *Within the first year* for the highest-risk mutations (MEN 2B).
  - *By about five years* for high-risk MEN 2A mutations.
  - Later, guided by calcitonin, for moderate-risk mutations.

## Anaplastic carcinoma
One of the most lethal cancers, with median survival of about six months. It arises in old people, usually over 60, often by dedifferentiation of a long-standing differentiated cancer. It is always stage IV.
- **Presentation:** a rapidly enlarging, hard, often painful mass fixed to surrounding structures. Hoarseness, stridor, dysphagia, nodes, and distant metastases in half.
- **Biopsy:** core or open biopsy, enough tissue to exclude *lymphoma*, which is curable.
- **Treatment:**
  - Resection only if it can be complete.
  - Otherwise radiotherapy with chemotherapy.
  - BRAF-mutant tumours respond to dabrafenib with trametinib.
  - Airway palliation, which includes facing the hard decision about tracheostomy through tumour.
  - Supportive and palliative care.
## Lymphoma
- **Who:** usually older women, almost always arising in *Hashimoto thyroiditis*.
- **Type:** mostly B-cell non-Hodgkin (diffuse large B-cell or MALT).
- **Presentation:** a rapidly enlarging, painless, firm goitre with compressive symptoms, often in a hypothyroid patient.
- **Diagnosis:** core or open biopsy with immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry.
- **Treatment:** chemotherapy and radiotherapy, with surgery limited to biopsy or airway relief. The outlook is good.
## Metastases to the thyroid
From kidney, breast, lung, melanoma and head and neck squamous carcinoma.

# Treating differentiated cancer | Surgery, iodine, suppression.
## Extent of thyroidectomy
- **Lobectomy with isthmusectomy:** for low-risk, unifocal, intrathyroidal tumours up to about 4 cm without nodes or extrathyroidal extension.
- **Total thyroidectomy:**
  - Tumours over 4 cm.
  - Gross extrathyroidal extension.
  - Nodal or distant metastases.
  - Bilateral disease.
  - Previous irradiation.
  - When radioiodine is planned.
- **Completion thyroidectomy:** when lobectomy histology shows a higher-risk tumour.
## The neck
- **Therapeutic central (level VI) dissection:** for clinically involved central nodes.
- **Prophylactic central dissection:** debated. Consider it for T3 and T4 tumours and when lateral nodes are involved. It raises the risk of hypoparathyroidism.
- **Lateral nodes proven on FNA:** a compartmental selective neck dissection of levels II to IV, with V as needed. Never "berry picking".
!ops 67 | Central compartment neck dissection.
## Invasive disease
- **RLN:** a functioning nerve with tumour on its surface is shaved and kept. A paralysed or encased nerve is resected.
- **Strap muscles:** resected en bloc.
- **Airway and oesophagus:** invasion of the trachea, larynx or oesophagus is treated by shave or full-thickness (window or sleeve) resection, according to depth.
!ops 82 | Invasive thyroid cancer.
## After surgery
- **Radioactive iodine (I-131):**
  - Ablates the remnant and treats microscopic or metastatic disease in intermediate- and high-risk patients.
  - The patient is prepared by thyroxine withdrawal or recombinant TSH, and a low-iodine diet.
  - Avoid iodinated contrast beforehand.
  - Side effects: sialadenitis, xerostomia, taste change, nasolacrimal duct obstruction, and second cancers.
- **TSH suppression with levothyroxine:**
  - Below 0.1 mU/L for high-risk disease.
  - Low-normal for low-risk disease.
- **Surveillance:**
  - Thyroglobulin (with antibodies), checked against the baseline.
  - Neck ultrasound.
  - Diagnostic radioiodine scans.
  - PET-CT for thyroglobulin-positive, iodine-negative disease.
- **External beam radiotherapy:** for unresectable or gross residual disease, or tracheal invasion, in older patients.
- **Iodine-refractory progressive disease:** kinase inhibitors (lenvatinib, sorafenib), and targeted agents for RET, NTRK and BRAF changes.

# Thyroidectomy | The operation and its complications.
## Types

## Indications: the four Cs plus one
1. Cancer.
2. Suspicion of cancer.
3. Compression.
4. Cosmesis.
5. Hyperthyroidism.
## Preoperative work-up
- Thyroid function, with the patient euthyroid.
- Calcium and vitamin D.
- Laryngoscopy.
- Ultrasound of thyroid and neck, and FNA.
- CT for retrosternal extension.
- Calcitonin when medullary cancer is suspected.
- The general anaesthetic assessment.
## Technique
1. **Incision:** a collar incision in a skin crease about two fingerbreadths above the sternal notch.
2. **Flaps:** subplatysmal flaps raised.
3. **Strap muscles:** separated in the midline and lifted off the gland.
4. **Lateral mobilisation:** the middle vein divided, and the gland rotated medially.
5. **Superior pole:** the vessels divided individually on the capsule, in the avascular cricothyroid (Reeve) space, to spare the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve.
6. **The RLN:** identified and traced to its laryngeal entry. Nerve monitoring confirms and protects it, and loss of signal on the first side may stop a planned bilateral operation.
7. **Parathyroids:**
   - Each gland is identified and kept on its blood supply, by dividing inferior thyroid artery branches on the capsule.
   - A devascularised gland is confirmed by frozen section, minced and reimplanted into sternomastoid.
8. **Berry's ligament:** divided carefully. Cautery near the nerve is dangerous here.
9. **Closure:**
   - Haemostasis checked with a Valsalva manoeuvre.
   - Straps and platysma closed.
   - Drains are optional; they do not prevent haematoma.
10. **Minimal-access approaches:** video-assisted and remote-access robotic routes for selected patients.

## Complications
- **Haematoma:** under 2%, usually within hours.
!flag Neck haematoma after thyroidectomy
Swelling, tight neck, stridor or distress:
1. Open the skin and the strap muscles at the bedside.
2. Evacuate the clot.
3. Secure the airway.
4. Return to theatre.
Venous and lymphatic congestion makes the larynx oedematous, so intubation is difficult. Release of pressure comes first.
!!
- **RLN injury:**
  - Temporary in up to about 5 to 10%, permanent in about 1%. Risk is higher in reoperation, cancer and retrosternal goitre.
  - Unilateral injury: hoarseness and aspiration.
  - *Bilateral* injury: the voice may be near normal, but there is stridor on extubation, often needing reintubation and tracheostomy.
- **External branch of the superior laryngeal nerve:** loss of high pitch and vocal projection, important in singers.
- **Hypocalcaemia from hypoparathyroidism:**
  - Common and transient after total thyroidectomy. Permanent in about 1 to 3% in expert hands, more after central dissection.
  - Symptoms at 24 to 72 hours: perioral and fingertip tingling, cramps, Chvostek and Trousseau signs, tetany, laryngospasm, a long QT.
  - Measure calcium and PTH after surgery.
  - Treat with oral calcium and calcitriol, and intravenous calcium gluconate for severe symptoms.
- **Other complications:**
  - Thyroid storm.
  - Tracheomalacia, which is rare.
  - Hypothyroidism (expected after total thyroidectomy).
  - Wound infection, seroma, keloid.
  - Pneumothorax.
  - Chyle leak after lateral dissection.
!differ Size cut-offs and lobectomy
Dhingra: Microcarcinomas under 1.5 cm and intrathyroidal tumours without nodes may have lobectomy; follicular neoplasms over 4 cm in the elderly go straight to total thyroidectomy; no elective neck dissection.
Lee: Following the 2015 ATA guidelines, lobectomy suffices for low-risk intrathyroidal tumours up to 4 cm; total thyroidectomy for larger, extrathyroidal or metastatic disease or when radioiodine is planned; prophylactic central dissection is debated.
Current practice follows the ATA approach and uses risk stratification rather than size alone.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Surgical anatomy:**
  - Berry's ligament.
  - The course of the RLN on each side, and the non-recurrent nerve.
  - The external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve.
  - The parathyroids and their blood supply.
- **Lingual thyroid:** scan before excision.
- **Work-up of a nodule:** TSH, ultrasound pattern, FNA, Bethesda categories; why FNA cannot diagnose follicular carcinoma.
- **The five cancers:** their spread, markers, MEN syndromes, RET testing, and phaeochromocytoma first.
- **Extent of surgery,** radioiodine, TSH suppression and thyroglobulin follow-up.
- **Complications:** haematoma (open at the bedside), nerve palsies, hypocalcaemia.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Operating for medullary carcinoma without excluding phaeochromocytoma.
- Calling a cystic lateral neck node in a young adult a branchial cyst without considering metastatic papillary carcinoma.
- Excising a lingual thyroid without checking for other thyroid tissue.
- Giving iodinated contrast just before planned radioiodine.
- Failing to check the cords before and after surgery.
!!
