@stand Each hearing test answers a different question. Tuning forks give the type of loss at the bedside in seconds; the audiogram measures how much; the tympanogram shows what the middle ear is doing; and objective tests such as otoacoustic emissions and the brainstem response work when the patient cannot or will not respond.

# Overview | Which test answers which question.
## The toolkit
!tree Hearing tests
Bedside | Voice and tuning forks | conductive or sensorineural, roughly how much
Behavioural | Pure-tone and speech audiometry | thresholds and understanding
Middle ear | Tympanometry and stapedial reflexes | drum, ossicles, tube, reflex arc
Objective | OAE, ABR, ASSR, ECochG | cochlea and neural pathway without a response
!!
## Choosing a test
Forks at the bedside; audiogram and tympanogram in clinic; OAE and ABR in newborns and uncooperative patients; ASSR for profound loss, beyond the range of ABR.

# Sound and the decibel | The language of audiology.
## Decibel scales
- **dB SPL:** sound pressure relative to 20 µPa.
- **dB HL:** relative to average normal hearing at each frequency, so 0 dB HL is normal on the audiogram.
- **dB SL:** relative to the patient's own threshold.
## Everyday levels
Whisper about 30 dB, conversation about 60 dB, a shout about 90 dB, discomfort about 120 dB and pain about 130 dB.
## How the middle ear amplifies
The area ratio of the drum to the footplate (about 17 to 1) and the lever action of the ossicles (about 1.3 to 1) give a gain of about 22 times, around 25 dB. Without it, most sound energy would bounce off the fluid of the inner ear.
## Where frequencies are heard
The base of the cochlea hears high frequencies, the apex low frequencies. The canal resonates at about 3000 Hz.

# Bedside tests | Voice and tuning forks.
## Voice tests
A whisper heard at 6 m is taken as normal; the other ear is masked by rubbing the tragus.
## Tuning fork tests
The 512 Hz fork is the routine choice: lower forks are felt as vibration, higher ones fade too quickly.
!fig d4-1 | Tuning fork tests: (A) air conduction, (B) bone conduction, (C) the Weber test.
### Rinne test
Compares air conduction with bone conduction in the same ear. *Positive* (air louder) is normal, and also seen in sensorineural loss; *negative* (bone louder) means a conductive loss of at least about 20 dB.
### Weber test
The fork on the forehead or vertex lateralises to the poorer ear in a conductive loss and to the better ear in a sensorineural loss. It is sensitive, lateralising with quite small differences between the ears.
### Absolute bone conduction and Schwabach tests
Compare the patient's bone conduction with the examiner's (with the canal occluded in the ABC test). Reduced in sensorineural loss.
### Bing and Gellé tests
Bing: occluding the canal makes the tone louder in normal and sensorineural ears but not in a conductive loss. Gellé: raising canal pressure normally reduces hearing, but not with a fixed or broken chain; tympanometry has replaced it.
!table Reading tuning forks
Test | Normal | Conductive | Sensorineural
Rinne | Air louder | Bone louder | Air louder
Weber | Central | To poorer ear | To better ear
ABC | Same as examiner | Same | Reduced
Schwabach | Equal | Lengthened | Shortened
!!
### Estimating the gap from Rinne
A negative Rinne at 256 Hz only suggests a gap of about 20 to 30 dB; at 256 and 512 Hz, 30 to 45 dB; at all three forks, 45 to 60 dB.
!differ Gap that turns each fork negative
Dhingra: 15 dB at 256, 30 dB at 512 and 45 dB at 1024 Hz.
Lee: 15, 25 and 35 dB.
!!
!pearl False-negative Rinne
In a severe one-sided sensorineural loss, the fork on the mastoid is heard by the other cochlea, so bone seems louder. Mask the good ear with a Barany noise box, and use the Weber.
!!

# Pure-tone audiometry | Thresholds, frequency by frequency.
## How it is done
In a sound-treated room, the softest tone the patient hears at each frequency from 250 to 8000 Hz is recorded, first by air conduction through headphones, then by bone conduction with a vibrator on the mastoid.
!fig d4-2 | A two-room audiometry set-up: the audiologist watches the patient through a glass partition.
## The symbols
Frequency runs across the top from low (250 Hz) to high (8000 Hz), and loudness runs down the side, so a worse hearing threshold sits *lower* on the chart. By convention the right ear is red and the left ear blue. Air conduction is a circle (right) or a cross (left), joined by a line; bone conduction is an angle bracket pointing away from the ear tested, < for right and > for left, and a square bracket, [ or ], when the other ear was masked. An arrow below a symbol means no response at the machine's maximum output, which is about 120 dB for air but only about 60 to 70 dB for bone.
!draw aud-key aud-normal | The symbols, and a normal audiogram: every threshold sits in the shaded band of 20 dB HL or better, and air and bone conduction coincide.
## Reading it, step by step
Read every audiogram in the same order, so nothing is missed.
!path Reading a pure-tone audiogram
Check the chart | right and left, masked or unmasked, reliability noted by the tester
Air conduction | normal (20 dB or better) ; how much loss: mild, moderate, severe, profound ; which frequencies: flat, sloping, rising, notched, cookie-bite
Bone conduction | equal to air: sensorineural ; better than air by more than 10 dB: conductive component ; both raised with a gap: mixed
Compare the ears | symmetrical ; asymmetric by 15 dB or more at two adjacent frequencies, or 20 dB at one: MRI of the internal auditory meatus
Check masking | ears differ by 40 dB or more on air, or any air–bone gap: masked thresholds needed — was it done?
Fit the clinical picture | otoscopy, tuning forks and tympanometry must agree ; if they do not, repeat or think non-organic
!!
**Bone conduction is cochlear function.** The air–bone gap is the size of the conductive loss: a gap of up to about 60 dB is possible from the middle ear alone (complete ossicular discontinuity behind an intact drum), so a larger gap means the bone thresholds are wrong or the loss is mixed. Bone conduction can be *better than normal* in a third-window lesion such as superior canal dehiscence, giving an apparent conductive loss with normal tympanometry and present reflexes.
## Masking
Sound presented to one ear can cross the skull and be heard by the other, so the non-test ear is masked with narrow-band noise. Interaural attenuation is 40 to 65 dB for air conduction with headphones (more with insert earphones) and close to 0 dB for bone, because the vibrator shakes the whole skull. Mask whenever the ears differ by 40 dB or more on air conduction, and for bone conduction whenever there is an air–bone gap in the test ear.

The usual method is the **plateau (Hood) technique**: noise in the non-test ear is raised in 10 dB steps while the threshold in the test ear is re-measured; while the threshold keeps rising with the noise, the other ear was answering, and once it stays the same across several steps (the plateau), the true threshold has been found. Without masking, a dead ear produces a **shadow curve**, a threshold that mirrors the good ear about 40 to 60 dB lower for air and almost level with it for bone. The **masking dilemma** arises in large bilateral conductive losses, as in bilateral atresia, when the noise needed to mask one ear crosses over and masks the test ear too; insert earphones and ABR by bone conduction help.
!draw aud-dead | A right dead ear. Unmasked, the right ear seems to have a 65 to 80 dB loss with near-normal bone conduction: a shadow of the left ear. With the left ear masked, there is no response by bone conduction at the limits of the machine.
## Repeatability
Normal repeat audiograms agree within about 5 dB, so a change of 10 dB or more at two frequencies is significant. Grades of loss use the average of 500, 1000, 2000 and 4000 Hz in the better ear (see the hearing loss article).

# Patterns on the audiogram | Worked examples.
## Conductive and mixed losses
**Otosclerosis** produces a conductive loss, often worse in the low frequencies, with a characteristic dip of bone conduction of about 10 to 15 dB at 2 kHz, the **Carhart notch**. The notch is mechanical, not cochlear: it disappears after successful stapes surgery. Tympanometry is type A or As, and the stapedial reflexes are absent.
!draw aud-otosc | Left otosclerosis. Air conduction is about 40 to 50 dB; masked bone conduction is near normal except for the dip at 2 kHz (Carhart notch). Air–bone gap about 35 dB.
A **mixed loss** combines a raised bone conduction with an air–bone gap, as in chronic otitis media with cochlear damage from long-standing infection, otosclerosis with cochlear involvement, or a temporal bone fracture. Both components matter: the gap may be closed by surgery, but the sensorineural part sets the best possible result, so a patient with bone conduction of 40 dB will still need a hearing aid after a perfect tympanoplasty.
!draw aud-mixed | Right mixed loss. Masked bone conduction is 30 to 55 dB (the sensorineural part), and air conduction 60 to 85 dB (the gap of about 30 dB is the conductive part).
## Sensorineural patterns
The *shape* of a sensorineural loss points to its cause.
!table Sensorineural shapes and what they suggest
Shape | Typical cause | Clues
Bilateral, symmetrical, sloping to the high frequencies | Presbycusis, ototoxicity | Age; aminoglycosides, cisplatin
Notch at 3, 4 or 6 kHz with recovery at 8 kHz | Noise | Occupational or recreational noise; usually bilateral
Low-frequency (rising) loss, fluctuating | Ménière's disease, early hydrops | Episodic vertigo, fullness, roaring tinnitus
Mid-frequency "cookie-bite" (U-shaped) loss | Genetic, congenital | Family history; stable
Flat, severe, sudden | Sudden sensorineural loss, viral, vascular | Onset within 72 hours
Asymmetric | Vestibular schwannoma until proven otherwise; also Ménière's, sudden loss, noise from one side (shooting) | MRI of the internal auditory meatus
Dead ear | Labyrinthitis, meningitis, fracture, surgery | Shadow curve without masking

## When the audiogram does not fit
**Non-organic (functional) hearing loss** is suggested when thresholds vary between tests, the patient converses easily despite a "severe" loss, the speech reception threshold is much better than the pure-tone average, a unilateral "dead ear" shows no shadow curve, or acoustic reflexes are present at levels near or below the claimed threshold. Objective tests (otoacoustic emissions, ABR or cortical evoked response audiometry) establish the true thresholds; the Stenger test detects a feigned unilateral loss.

# Speech audiometry | Understanding, not just hearing.
## Speech reception threshold
The level at which half of two-syllable spondee words are repeated correctly; it lies within 10 dB of the pure-tone average.
## Speech discrimination
Single-syllable words presented 30 to 40 dB above the SRT. Good in conductive loss, reduced in cochlear loss, very poor in retrocochlear loss.
## Roll-over
Discrimination falling as loudness increases, a sign of a retrocochlear lesion.
## Reading the speech audiogram
The speech audiogram plots the percentage of words repeated correctly against loudness. In a **conductive loss** the curve has a normal shape but is shifted to the right: once the sound is loud enough, the patient understands everything. In a **cochlear loss** the curve is shifted and may never reach 100%, because the damaged cochlea distorts the sound. In a **retrocochlear loss** the maximum is poor and falls again at high levels (roll-over), and speech discrimination is much worse than the pure-tone thresholds would suggest. Speech scores matter clinically: they predict hearing aid benefit and help select candidates for cochlear implants, which are assessed with sentence tests in quiet and in noise.

# Impedance audiometry | The middle ear and reflexes.
## Tympanometry
A probe in the canal changes the pressure while measuring how much sound is reflected from the drum. The peak of compliance shows the middle ear pressure and the mobility of the system.
!fig d4-4 d4-5 | First, impedance audiometry in progress. Second, the principle of the impedance audiometer.
!table Tympanogram types
Type | Finding | Think of
A | Normal peak | Normal ear
As | Low peak | Otosclerosis, malleus fixation
Ad | High peak | Ossicular discontinuity, lax drum
B | Flat | Fluid, thick drum; perforation or patent grommet if the volume is high
C | Peak below −100 mm H2O | Retraction, tube dysfunction
!!
!fig d4-6 | Types of tympanogram.
!draw tymp-types | Reading a tympanogram: the peak height (compliance), the peak position (middle ear pressure) and the ear canal volume (ECV). The shaded box is the normal range, roughly −100 to +50 daPa and 0.3 to 1.5 ml.
## Reading a tympanogram
Read three numbers, not just the shape. The **peak pressure** shows the middle ear pressure; normal is about −100 to +50 daPa (1 daPa is about 1 mm H₂O). The **peak compliance** shows how mobile the drum and chain are; normal in adults is about 0.3 to 1.5 ml. The **ear canal volume** is the air between the probe and the drum; normal is about 0.6 to 1.5 ml in adults and 0.3 to 0.9 ml in children.
!table A flat tympanogram: what the volume tells you
Ear canal volume | Meaning
Normal | Middle ear fluid or a thickened, immobile drum
High (over about 2 ml adults, 1 ml children) | Perforation or a patent ventilation tube
Low | Wax or debris against the drum, or the probe against the canal wall
!!
An ear canal volume over about 2.5 mL in adults (2 mL in children) is a strong sign of a perforation or patent grommet. Infants under six months need a 1000 Hz probe tone, because the soft canal wall makes the standard 226 Hz test unreliable. A patulous Eustachian tube produces a trace that moves with breathing; a glomus tumour can produce pulsations in time with the heartbeat.
## Stapedial (acoustic) reflex
A loud sound, 70 to 100 dB above threshold, contracts the stapedius in both ears.
!fig d4-7 | The acoustic reflex pathway.
### What reflexes tell you
Reflexes are measured in four conditions: ipsilateral and contralateral for each ear. Record the **stimulus ear** and the **probe ear** carefully: a contralateral right reflex means sound in the right ear, response measured in the left.
- A reflex at only 40 to 60 dB above threshold: recruitment, a cochlear lesion.
- Reflex decay to half within 10 seconds: an eighth nerve lesion.
- Absent with normal hearing: a facial nerve lesion above the nerve to stapedius.
- Present on the same side but absent on the other: a brainstem lesion.
- Absent: stapes fixation or any middle ear disease.
!table Reflex patterns and the site of the lesion
Finding | Site
Absent whenever the probe is in the affected ear | Middle ear on that side (the probe cannot detect a reflex)
Absent whenever the affected ear is stimulated | Severe cochlear loss or eighth nerve lesion on that side
Absent whenever the probe is in the affected ear, with normal hearing | Facial nerve on that side, above the nerve to stapedius
Ipsilateral reflexes present, both contralateral reflexes absent | Brainstem lesion crossing the midline
Present with an apparent severe loss | Non-organic loss
!!

# Site-of-lesion tests | Cochlear or retrocochlear?
Older behavioural tests, now largely replaced by MRI:
- **Alternate binaural loudness balance:** recruitment in cochlear loss.
- **Short increment sensitivity index (SISI):** high scores in cochlear loss.
- **Tone decay:** over 25 dB in retrocochlear loss.
- **Bekesy audiometry.**

# Objective tests | When the patient cannot respond.
## Otoacoustic emissions
Sounds produced by the outer hair cells and recorded in the canal. Transient emissions disappear with losses over about 30 dB, distortion-product emissions over about 50 dB. Absent with middle ear fluid or debris; present in auditory neuropathy. Used for newborn screening.

Results are reported as **pass** (clear response) or **refer**. A refer is not a diagnosis of deafness: it may mean vernix or fluid in the canal, a noisy baby or environment, or a true loss, which is why screening repeats the test and then uses automated ABR. Because emissions reflect outer hair cell function, they can show early damage from noise or ototoxic drugs before the audiogram changes, and they monitor patients on cisplatin.
## Auditory brainstem response
Five to seven waves recorded from the scalp within 10 ms of a click: I and II from the eighth nerve, III the cochlear nucleus, IV the superior olive, V the lateral lemniscus and inferior colliculus (*E. COLI*). Used for thresholds in infants (pass at about 30 to 40 dB nHL) and to look for retrocochlear lesions, though it misses small tumours. Unaffected by sleep and sedation.
!fig d4-10 | Brainstem auditory evoked potentials.
## Reading an ABR
Look at three things. **Wave latencies:** wave V normally appears at about 5.5 to 6 ms with a loud click. **Interpeak intervals:** I–III about 2 ms, III–V about 2 ms, and I–V about 4 ms; the I–V interval measures conduction along the nerve and brainstem and is *independent of the middle ear*. **Threshold:** the softest level at which wave V can still be identified, used to estimate hearing in infants. In a conductive loss all the waves are delayed but the I–V interval is normal; in a cochlear loss wave I may be small or absent and the latency of V falls steeply as loudness rises (recruitment); in a retrocochlear lesion the I–V interval is prolonged or the waves after I are lost, and wave V on the affected side is delayed compared with the other ear.
!draw abr-traces | ABR traces. Conductive loss delays every wave; a retrocochlear lesion prolongs the I–V interval. Values are typical, not normative.
!differ Abnormal interaural wave V difference
Dhingra: Over 0.2 ms.
Lee: 0.4 to 0.5 ms.
!!
## Auditory steady-state response
Frequency-specific thresholds into the profound range, beyond the reach of ABR (about 80 dB).
## Electrocochleography
Records the summating potential, action potential and cochlear microphonic. An enlarged SP to AP ratio suggests Ménière's disease (see that topic).
!fig d4-9 | Electrocochleography: normal ear and an ear with Ménière's disease.
!draw ecochg | Reading electrocochleography: the summating potential (SP) is the shoulder before the sharp action potential (AP). An SP/AP ratio above about 0.4 to 0.5 suggests endolymphatic hydrops; a normal result does not exclude it.
## Auditory neuropathy
Present otoacoustic emissions and cochlear microphonic with an absent or abnormal ABR: the hair cells work but the nerve does not carry the signal synchronously.

# Testing children | By age.
- Birth to six months: behavioural observation, OAE and ABR.
- Six to thirty months: visual reinforcement audiometry.
- From about thirty months: play audiometry.
- Older children: conventional audiometry. Normal thresholds in children are 15 dB or better.

**Visual reinforcement audiometry** conditions the child to turn towards a sound, rewarded by a light or puppet, and uses insert earphones or free field; free-field results reflect the better ear only. **Play audiometry** turns the test into a game, such as putting a peg in a board each time a sound is heard. Tympanometry and otoacoustic emissions support behavioural tests at every age, and ABR under sleep or sedation is used when behavioural results are unreliable.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Reading a false-negative Rinne as a conductive loss.
- Forgetting to mask.
- Calling a flat tympanogram fluid without checking the canal volume.
- Taking a shadow curve for a real moderate loss in a dead ear.
- Planning stapes surgery for an air–bone gap with present reflexes before excluding a third window.
- Accepting a severe loss in a patient who follows quiet conversation.
!!
