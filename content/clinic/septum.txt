@stand The septum is the midline wall that holds up the lower nose and divides the airway. A deviated septum only matters when it blocks the patient. A swollen septum after a blow is the emergency: a haematoma drained the same day is a minor procedure, while one left alone becomes an abscess, a dead cartilage and a saddle nose.

# Overview | The partition of the nose.
## Applied anatomy
The septum has three parts: the *columellar septum* (the columella, with the medial crura of the alar cartilages), the *membranous septum* (skin between the columella and the septal cartilage), and the *septum proper*, made of cartilage and bone covered by mucoperichondrium and mucoperiosteum.
### The framework
- **Quadrilateral (septal) cartilage:** the front of the septum, supporting the nasal tip and dorsum.
- **Perpendicular plate of the ethmoid:** above and behind.
- **Vomer:** below and behind.
- **Small contributions:** the crest of the maxilla and palatine bones, the nasal spine of the frontal bone, and the rostrum of the sphenoid.
!fig d26-1 | Anatomy of the nasal septum: septal cartilage, perpendicular plate of the ethmoid and vomer.
!anat s105_43 | The septal cartilage and bones of the septum.
### Blood supply
Little's area on the anteroinferior septum, where four arteries meet, is the commonest site of epistaxis (see the epistaxis topic).
## Why it matters
Septal problems cause nasal obstruction, sinusitis, epistaxis and external deformity, and septal surgery is one of the commonest operations in ENT.

# Septal trauma | Fractures of the septum.
## How it happens
Blows from the front, side or below buckle, fracture, dislocate or crush the septum. The septal cartilage can dislocate from the vomerine groove without any fracture of the nasal bones. Mucosal tears cause profuse epistaxis; an intact mucosa traps blood as a haematoma.
## Named fractures
- **Jarjaway fracture:** from a frontal blow, running horizontally backwards just above the junction of the septal cartilage with the vomer.
- **Chevallet fracture:** from a blow from below, running vertically from the anterior nasal spine up to the junction of the bony and cartilaginous dorsum.
!fig d26-2 | Septal fractures: (A) Jarjaway type, (B) Chevallet type.
## Treatment
Drain any haematoma; reposition dislocated or fractured fragments, support them between the flaps with mattress sutures and packing, and treat any nasal bone fracture at the same time. Ignored septal injuries leave a deviated cartilaginous nose and an asymmetric tip, columella or nostril.

# Deviated nasal septum | When the partition is crooked.
## Causes
### Trauma
A lateral blow dislocates the cartilage from the vomerine groove and maxillary crest; a frontal blow buckles and telescopes it. Childhood injuries, and birth trauma from a difficult delivery, are often forgotten.
### Developmental
Unequal growth of the palate and the skull base buckles the septum. Mouth breathers with adenoid hypertrophy often have a high-arched palate and a deviated septum; it is also seen with cleft lip and palate and dental anomalies.
!fig d26-3 | Deviated septum with a high-arched palate.
### Racial and hereditary
Commoner in white populations, and often in several members of a family.
## Types
- **Anterior (caudal) dislocation:** the lower border of the cartilage projects into one nostril, best seen with the head tilted back.
- **C-shaped deviation:** a single curve; the wider side often shows compensatory turbinate hypertrophy.
- **S-shaped deviation:** two curves, which can block both sides.
- **Spur:** a shelf-like projection, usually at the junction of bone and cartilage, which may press on the lateral wall.
- **Thickening:** from an organised haematoma or overlapping fragments.
!fig d26-4 d26-6 | First, the types of septal deviation. Second, a C-shaped deviation nearly blocking the left side.
## Clinical features
### Nasal obstruction
One-sided or both; high deviations block more than low ones, because the main airflow passes through the upper nose. Paradoxical obstruction on the wider side comes from compensatory turbinate hypertrophy.
### Other symptoms
- **Headache,** from a spur pressing on the lateral wall.
- **Sinusitis,** from obstructed ostia and poor ventilation.
- **Epistaxis,** from crusting over the convexity or vessels stretched over a spur.
- **Reduced smell,** when air cannot reach the olfactory cleft.
- **External deformity** of the dorsum, tip or columella.
- **Middle ear infections.**

## The nasal valve
The narrowest part of the airway, bounded by the septum, the lower edge of the upper lateral cartilage (normally at an angle of about 10 to 15 degrees) and the head of the inferior turbinate. *Cottle's test*: pulling the cheek laterally opens the valve; if breathing improves, the valve is part of the problem.
!fig d23-3 d26-7 | First, the nasal valve area. Second, Cottle's test: pulling the cheek laterally opens the valve.
## Management
Minor, symptomless deviations need nothing. Treat coexisting rhinitis first. Operate when the deviation causes obstruction or the problems listed above.
### Septoplasty
The conservative operation: only the most deviated parts are removed; the rest is straightened (by scoring, morselising or repositioning) and put back. A flap is usually raised on one side only. It has largely replaced submucous resection, and can be done in children with severe obstruction because it preserves growth centres.
### Submucous resection
Flaps are raised on both sides and the deviated cartilage and bone are removed, leaving dorsal and caudal struts; now mostly historical (see the nose and sinus operations topic).
### Associated procedures
Turbinate reduction for hypertrophy, and septorhinoplasty when the external nose is deviated.

# Septal haematoma and abscess | The emergency.
## Septal haematoma
### Cause
Blood under the perichondrium or periosteum, after nasal trauma or septal surgery, or spontaneously in bleeding disorders.
### Clinical picture
Bilateral nasal obstruction, frontal headache and pressure over the bridge, with a smooth, rounded, soft swelling of the septum in both nostrils.
!fig d26-8 | Septal haematoma: smooth swellings of the septum in both nostrils.
### Treatment
Aspirate small collections with a wide-bore needle; incise larger ones parallel to the floor, excise a small strip of mucosa to keep it draining, pack both sides to prevent reaccumulation, and give antibiotics to prevent abscess.
## Septal abscess
### Cause
Usually an infected haematoma; occasionally after a furuncle of the nose or lip, or systemic infections such as typhoid or measles.
### Clinical picture
Severe obstruction, pain and tenderness over the bridge, fever and chills, a red, swollen nose, fluctuant septal swelling and tender submandibular nodes.
!fig d26-9 | Septal abscess.
### Treatment
Drain urgently at the most dependent point, excise a piece of mucosa, remove pus and dead cartilage, reopen daily for two or three days if needed, and give antibiotics guided by culture for at least ten days.
## Complications
- Cartilage necrosis and *saddle nose* (supratip depression), corrected by augmentation rhinoplasty after two or three months.
- Septal perforation.
- Meningitis and cavernous sinus thrombosis, rare but serious.
!flag Never send home a swollen septum
A child or adult with nasal obstruction after a blow needs the septum examined. A missed haematoma in a child may only show itself years later as a saddle nose.
!!

# Septal perforation | A hole in the partition.
## Causes
!tree Causes of septal perforation
Trauma | Septal surgery | repeated cautery, nose picking, piercing
Infection and inflammation | Septal abscess | myiasis, rhinolith or foreign body, tuberculosis, lupus, leprosy, syphilis
Destructive disease | Granulomatosis with polyangiitis | NK/T-cell lymphoma, carcinoma
Drugs and chemicals | Cocaine | long-term steroid sprays, chromium, arsenic, soda ash
Unknown | Idiopathic |
!!
Lupus, tuberculosis and leprosy perforate the cartilaginous septum; syphilis perforates the bony septum.
!fig d26-10 d26-11 | First, a septal perforation. Second, a septum deliberately perforated for an ornament.
## Clinical picture
Small anterior perforations whistle with breathing. Larger ones crust, obstruct and bleed when crusts are removed; very large ones can cause a saddle nose.
## Investigations
Look for the cause: blood tests (ANCA, ACE, syphilis serology), urine drug screen where relevant, and biopsy of granulation or of the edge of the perforation (taken from the posterosuperior edge, away from the healed anterior rim).
## Treatment
- **Conservative:** saline or alkaline douches and bland ointment to keep the nose free of crusts.
- **Septal button:** a silicone obturator.
- **Surgical closure:** for small, inactive perforations, with local rotation or advancement flaps and an interposition graft.
!fig d26-12 | A silicone septal button closing a perforation.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## On call
!ward A blocked nose after a blow
1. Look at the septum in every patient with nasal trauma.
2. A boggy, swollen septum is a haematoma: drain it the same day.
3. Pack both sides and give antibiotics.
4. Review for reaccumulation and abscess.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Missing a septal haematoma after trauma.
- Operating on a septum when rhinitis is the real cause of obstruction.
- Biopsying the healed anterior edge of a perforation.
!!
