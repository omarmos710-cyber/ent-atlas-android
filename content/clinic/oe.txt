# Summary | Infection of the ear canal skin.
Otitis externa is inflammation of the skin of the external auditory canal, bacterial, fungal or reactive. Each form has its own chapter below.
!tree Otitis externa
Bacterial | [[3|Acute diffuse otitis externa]] | [[4|furuncle]] (localised)
Fungal | [[5|Otomycosis]] | Aspergillus, Candida
Reactive | [[6|Eczematous, seborrhoeic]] | contact dermatitis from drops or moulds, neurodermatitis
Chronic | [[6|Chronic otitis externa]] | stenosis of the canal
Invasive | [[7|Necrotising (malignant) otitis externa]] | skull base osteomyelitis
!!

# Etiology and applied anatomy | Common to all forms.
## Why the canal gets infected
The canal is a blind, warm, dark tube lined by thin skin over bone and cartilage, and anything that breaks its defences (moisture, scratching, loss of wax) lets infection in.
## Applied anatomy
The outer third of the canal is cartilaginous, with hair follicles and sebaceous and ceruminous glands; the inner two-thirds is bony with thin, gland-free skin. Infection spreads from the canal through the *fissures of Santorini* in the cartilage and the tympanomastoid suture to the parotid, the skull base and the temporomandibular joint.
!fig d8-12 | Skin of the cartilaginous canal, with its hair follicles and sebaceous and ceruminous glands.
!anat s33_35 | Fissures of Santorini and the foramen of Huschke, routes from the canal to the parotid and skull base.

# Acute diffuse otitis externa {dz} | Swimmer's ear.
## Etiology
Moisture (swimming, humid climates), trauma from cotton buds and fingernails, hearing aids and earbuds, eczema and loss of protective wax. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *Staphylococcus aureus* are the usual organisms.
## Clinical features
### Symptoms
Pain that develops over a day or two, itching, a feeling of blockage and a scanty discharge.
### Signs
Pain on moving the tragus or pinna; a red, swollen canal with debris; tender nodes in front of or behind the ear. When visible, the drum moves normally on pneumatic otoscopy, unlike otitis media.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Acute otitis externa: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Diagnosis | Otoscopy with pneumatic check of the drum | Clinical diagnosis: tragal tenderness, a swollen canal, a mobile drum
Not routine | Swab for culture and sensitivity | Failure of first-line drops, recurrent or severe infection, or immunosuppression
Diabetes or recurrence | Blood glucose or HbA1c | Undiagnosed diabetes
Severe pain with granulation, or failed treatment | CRP or ESR, biopsy and imaging | To exclude necrotising otitis externa
!!
## Management
### Approach
!ward On call: a painful, swollen ear canal
1. Clean under vision; insert a wick if the canal is closed.
2. Antibiotic-steroid drops; analgesia; keep dry.
3. Check glucose in diabetics and anyone with recurrent infection.
4. Diabetic or immunocompromised with severe pain and granulation: biopsy, ESR, imaging and admit ([[7]]).
!!
### Aural toilet
Clean the canal under vision by suction or dry mopping. Debris collects in the anteroinferior recess, so look there.
!ops
### Wick
If the canal is swollen shut, insert an expanding wick soaked in antibiotic-steroid drops, or in an astringent such as aluminium acetate 8% or silver nitrate 3%.
!differ Changing the wick
Dhingra: Daily for two to three days.
Lee: Every three to five days.
In practice: a compressed sponge wick usually falls out as the swelling settles, and is reviewed or replaced at about two to three days.
!!
### Drops
Antibiotic-steroid drops for a week to ten days. Acidifying with dilute vinegar helps mild cases and prevention.
### Oral antibiotics
Not needed, unless there is spreading cellulitis or the patient is diabetic or immunocompromised.
### Keep it dry
No swimming until healed; cotton wool with petroleum jelly when bathing.
!update Current evidence
For acute otitis externa, the American guideline recommends topical drops as first-line treatment and advises against oral antibiotics unless infection spreads beyond the canal or the patient is at risk (Rosenfeld et al., *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2014), supported by a Cochrane review (Kaushik et al., 2010).
!!
### When drops fail
!pearl When drops fail
Think of contact allergy to neomycin, fungal superinfection ([[5]]), or an underlying necrotising infection ([[7]]).
!!
### Drugs and doses
!table Acute otitis externa: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Every patient | Analgesia (paracetamol or ibuprofen); aural toilet; keep the ear dry | Warmth helps pain
Mild, no discharge | Acetic acid 2% ear spray, 1 spray three times a day (12 years and over) | 7 days
First line (UK) | Neomycin with corticosteroid drops (for example neomycin–dexamethasone), 3 drops three times a day; or gentamicin–hydrocortisone, 2 to 4 drops three or four times a day | 7 days, up to 14 if needed; aminoglycosides with caution if the drum may be perforated
First line (US) or drum not intact | Ofloxacin 0.3% once daily (5 drops aged 6 months to 12 years, 10 drops from 13 years); or ciprofloxacin–hydrocortisone, 3 drops twice daily (from 1 year) | 7 days; quinolones are safe with a perforation or grommet
Canal closed by swelling | Expanding wick soaked in the same drops | Review at 2 to 3 days
Spreading cellulitis, diabetes or immunosuppression | Flucloxacillin: adults 500 mg four times a day; 1 month to 1 year 62.5 to 125 mg, 2 to 9 years 125 to 250 mg, 10 to 17 years 250 to 500 mg, four times a day | 7 days, with the drops; refer to exclude necrotising otitis externa
Penicillin allergy | Clarithromycin (adults 250 to 500 mg twice a day) or erythromycin | 7 days
Pseudomonas on culture with spread | Oral ciprofloxacin (adults 500 to 750 mg twice a day) | Specialist advice
!!
Doses from NICE CKS Otitis externa and regional NHS antimicrobial guidance (NHS Forth Valley, NHS Highland), the BNF and BNF for Children, product licences, and the American guideline (Rosenfeld et al., *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2014).

# Furuncle {dz} | A boil in the canal.
## Etiology
A staphylococcal infection of a hair follicle, so it occurs only in the hair-bearing outer cartilaginous canal.
## Clinical features
Pain is out of proportion to its size and worse on chewing; it can swell the postauricular groove and mimic mastoiditis.
## Differential diagnosis
Distinguishing it from [[t:mastoiditis]]: the drum is normal, there is no preceding otitis media and no mucoid discharge, and pinna movement is painful.
## Management
Analgesia, oral antistaphylococcal antibiotics, heat, and an ichthammol-glycerine pack; incise when it points. Recurrent furuncles call for testing for diabetes and treating nasal staphylococcal carriage.
### Drugs and doses
!table Furuncle: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
First line | Flucloxacillin: adults 500 mg four times a day; children 1 month to 1 year 62.5 to 125 mg, 2 to 9 years 125 to 250 mg, 10 to 17 years 250 to 500 mg, four times a day | 7 days; with analgesia and heat
Penicillin allergy | Clarithromycin: adults 250 to 500 mg twice a day | 7 days
Pointing abscess | Incision and drainage | Send pus for culture (MRSA)
Recurrent furuncles | Test for diabetes; treat nasal carriage (mupirocin 2% nasal ointment three times a day for 5 days) | 
!!
Doses from the BNF and BNF for Children and NHS antimicrobial guidance.

# Otomycosis {dz} | Itch and spores.
## Etiology
*Aspergillus niger* (black-headed spores), *Aspergillus fumigatus* (pale blue or green) and *Candida* (creamy white). Repeated antibiotic drops, humidity and swimming predispose.
## Clinical features
Intense itching, discomfort, blockage and a watery discharge with a musty smell. The canal contains a mass like *wet newspaper*, with black, grey or white spores.
!fig d8-11 | Endoscopic view of otomycosis with yellow (A) and black (B) fungal spores.
## Management
Meticulous cleaning, then a topical antifungal, continued for at least 14 days after the infection has cleared: clotrimazole for most, nystatin for *Candida*. Salicylic acid in alcohol and povidone iodine are alternatives.
### Drugs and doses
!table Otomycosis: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
First | Aural toilet: remove all fungal debris under the microscope | Repeat as needed
First line | Clotrimazole 1% solution, 2 to 3 drops two or three times a day | Continue for at least 14 days after the infection has cleared
Alternative | Clioquinol with flumetasone drops, 2 to 3 drops twice a day | 7 to 10 days; stains; avoid with a perforation
Candida | Nystatin preparations | 
Stop the cause | Stop antibiotic ear drops; keep the ear dry | 
!!
Doses from NICE CKS Otitis externa and the BNF.

# Chronic and reactive otitis externa {dz} | Itch, scaling and narrowing.
## Chronic otitis externa
### Clinical features
Long-standing itch and irritation with thick, dry, scaling skin, which may narrow the canal.
### Management
Treat underlying eczema or seborrhoeic dermatitis, use steroid drops or ointment, and stop the patient scratching.
!table Chronic otitis externa: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
First line | Steroid ear drops, such as betamethasone 0.1%, 2 to 3 drops every 2 to 3 hours, reducing as it settles | Short courses; treat any infection first
Eczema or seborrhoeic dermatitis | Treat the skin and scalp; avoid soaps and cotton buds | 
Itch-scratch cycle | Stop scratching; emollient | 
!!
Doses from the BNF.
## Reactive forms
Eczematous otitis externa from drops or hearing-aid moulds, seborrhoeic dermatitis with scalp involvement, and neurodermatitis from habitual scratching.
## Otitis externa haemorrhagica
Blood blisters on the deep canal and drum, often with influenza (bullous myringitis, [[t:myring]]).
## Chronic stenosing otitis externa
Fibrous narrowing of the deep canal ([[t:canal]]). Treated by excising the thick skin, widening the bony canal and grafting.

# Necrotising otitis externa {dz} | The dangerous one.
## Epidemiology
Most patients are older and diabetic, but about 1 in 10 has no obvious immunosuppression.
## Etiology
*Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is the usual organism (about 60% of cultures in recent series, with rising resistance); fungi such as *Aspergillus* grow in a significant minority, more often in HIV with low CD4 counts, and some cultures grow nothing.
## Pathophysiology
An invasive infection that spreads from the canal floor through the **fissures of Santorini** into the infratemporal fossa and the bone of the skull base: a skull base osteomyelitis.
## Clinical features
### Symptoms
Severe, deep, unrelenting pain, worse at night and out of proportion to what is seen, with a persistent discharge despite treatment.
### Signs
Granulation tissue on the floor of the canal at the bony-cartilaginous junction. Cranial nerve palsies mark spread: the facial nerve first at the stylomastoid foramen, then the ninth, tenth and eleventh at the jugular foramen, and the twelfth. Multiple palsies carry a worse prognosis.
!flag Suspect it when
- A diabetic or immunocompromised patient has severe pain and granulation in the canal.
- Otitis externa fails to respond after a week of proper treatment.
- A cranial nerve palsy appears with ear pain.
!!
## Diagnosis
### Approach
- **Bloods:** ESR or CRP (useful to follow), glucose and HbA1c.
- **Swab and granulation biopsy** for bacterial and fungal culture and histology.
- **Imaging:** contrast MRI or FDG PET-CT; CT for bone erosion; nuclear scans as below.
### Imaging
!table Which scan?
Test | Use | Limitation
Technetium-99 bone scan | Confirms early bone involvement | Stays positive for months
Gallium or indium scan | Follows response; repeated every few weeks | Less anatomical detail
CT | Bone erosion | Changes appear late
MRI | Soft tissue, marrow and dura | Not a guide to cure
!!
CT shows erosion only after about 30% demineralisation, so a normal early CT is falsely reassuring. Contrast MRI with fat suppression and diffusion shows soft tissue and marrow disease earlier and detects intracranial spread; **FDG PET-CT** is increasingly used to diagnose and monitor. CT and MRI changes persist for up to a year after cure, so they cannot decide when to stop.
!imaging | Necrotising otitis externa, canal stenosis and canal carcinoma.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Necrotising otitis externa: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Every patient | ESR and CRP; glucose and HbA1c; full blood count, renal function | Baseline; ESR and CRP are followed to judge response
Every patient (before antibiotics if stable) | Swab and biopsy of canal granulation | Organism and sensitivities; excludes squamous carcinoma
Initial imaging | CT of the temporal bones | Bone erosion of the canal floor and skull base; normal early in the disease
Imaging of choice for extent | Contrast MRI with fat suppression and diffusion | Soft tissue and marrow disease, intracranial spread and venous thrombosis
Early bone involvement | Technetium-99m bone scan | Very sensitive but stays positive for months
Response and end of treatment | Gallium or indium scan, or FDG PET-CT; repeat MRI | To decide when to stop
!!
## Differential diagnosis
Squamous carcinoma of the temporal bone ([[t:tumear]]) is the key differential, which is why granulation is always biopsied.
## Management
Admit for multidisciplinary care (otology, diabetes, microbiology, radiology, elderly care). Control the diabetes, clean the canal regularly, and start empirical intravenous antipseudomonal treatment at once.
### Antimicrobials
- **Empirical:** often **dual therapy** (piperacillin-tazobactam or ceftazidime with a fluoroquinolone), adjusted to cultures.
- **Early, limited disease:** oral ciprofloxacin alone.
- **Aspergillus:** voriconazole.
- **Duration:** no agreed duration, but consensus favours at least **six weeks**, often completed at home, guided by symptoms, inflammatory markers and imaging.
!differ Monitoring and duration
Dhingra: Gallium repeated every three weeks; treatment for six to eight weeks, including intravenous aminoglycoside combinations.
Lee: Gallium every four weeks; oral fluoroquinolone for about six weeks as first line.
In practice: There is no agreed regimen; most units follow the treatment above for at least six weeks, guided by pain, CRP and MRI or PET-CT, and use gallium scanning less.
!!
### Surgery
Limited to biopsy, deep sampling (for example at cortical mastoidectomy when there is no tissue in the canal) and drainage of abscesses or sequestra; debridement and nerve decompression have no proven benefit, and hyperbaric oxygen is unproven.
!update Current evidence
For necrotising otitis externa, a systematic review of 70 studies (2,274 patients, mostly retrospective) found 84% were diabetic, *Pseudomonas* grew in 62%, 14% grew no organism, and there was no evidence for the best imaging, regimen or duration (median 7.2 weeks) (Takata et al., *Clin Otolaryngol* 2023). Cure rates have fallen and culture-negative cases risen over time (Byun et al., *Otol Neurotol* 2020). A UK multidisciplinary guideline identifies high-risk patients for early intensive treatment (Hopkins et al., *J Laryngol Otol* 2020).
!!
### Drugs and doses
!table Necrotising otitis externa: drugs and doses
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Before antibiotics (if stable) | Swab and biopsy of granulation for bacterial and fungal culture | Then start treatment at once
Non-severe, or oral follow-on | Ciprofloxacin 750 mg by mouth twice a day | Total course usually 6 weeks, guided by pain, CRP and MRI
Moderate or severe (intravenous) | Piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g every 6 hours (or every 8 hours by extended 3 to 4 hour infusion); with oral ciprofloxacin in many units | Usually 1 to 3 weeks IV, then oral ciprofloxacin
Non-severe penicillin allergy | Ceftazidime 2 g IV every 8 hours (maximum 3 g a day over 80 years) | 
Aspergillus | Voriconazole: 6 mg/kg IV every 12 hours for 2 doses, then 4 mg/kg every 12 hours; or by mouth 400 mg every 12 hours for 2 doses, then 200 mg every 12 hours (40 kg and over) | Long courses with drug-level monitoring; infectious disease advice
Every patient | Tight diabetic control; regular aural toilet; topical anti-pseudomonal drops | 
!!
Regimens from NHS antimicrobial guidance (NHS Lothian and other trust guides), the UCLH standardised six-week pathway (Dhariwal et al., *J Int Adv Otol* 2023) and the BNF; local microbiology advice takes precedence.
## Complications and outcome
- **Cranial neuropathies:** VII, then IX, X, XI and XII.
- **Sigmoid sinus thrombosis** and **sepsis**.
- **Mortality** remains around 5 to 20%. Quinolone resistance is rising.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Prescribing oral antibiotics instead of cleaning and drops.
- Missing a necrotising infection in a diabetic.
- Neomycin contact allergy mistaken for treatment failure.
- Drops into a canal full of debris.
