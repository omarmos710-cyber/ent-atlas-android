@stand A newborn's airway is narrow, soft and quick to swell, and most of its congenital problems announce themselves as noise: a high-pitched crowing that is worse on feeding and crying and better lying prone. Most of these babies have laryngomalacia and will outgrow it. The art is to recognise the few who will not, the stenosis, the paralysed folds, the haemangioma, the cleft, and to look at the whole airway before deciding.

# Summary | The congenital larynx.
- Laryngomalacia (the commonest).
- Vocal fold paralysis (the second commonest).
- Congenital subglottic stenosis.
- Laryngeal web and atresia.
- Subglottic haemangioma.
- Laryngeal cysts and laryngocele.
- Laryngo-oesophageal cleft.
- Tracheomalacia and vascular rings (in the trachea, outside the larynx).
Each lesion has its own chapter, beginning with [[@Laryngomalacia]], the commonest.

# Diagnosis | Clues from the cry.
A *normal cry* with stridor points away from the glottis (laryngomalacia, subglottic stenosis, haemangioma); a *weak, hoarse or absent cry* points to the vocal folds (paralysis, web). Stridor eased by lying prone suggests laryngomalacia, micrognathia or macroglossia. Choking and cyanosis with feeds suggest a cleft, paralysis or a tracheo-oesophageal fistula.
## Investigations: what and when
!table Congenital stridor: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
First line | Awake flexible laryngoscopy in clinic | Laryngomalacia, vocal fold movement
Severe, atypical, or suspected second lesion | Microlaryngoscopy and bronchoscopy under spontaneous-breathing anaesthesia | Subglottic stenosis, haemangioma, cleft, web, tracheomalacia
Severity | Overnight oximetry or polysomnography; growth chart | Obstructive events, hypoxia

Vascular ring or mass | CT or MR angiography; echocardiography | Compression

# Laryngomalacia {dz} | Floppy supraglottis.
## Pathophysiology
Excessive flaccidity of the supraglottis, which is drawn into the airway on inspiration.
## Clinical features
Stridor appears at birth or within the first weeks, is inspiratory and fluttering, worse with crying, feeding, excitement and lying supine, and better prone; the cry is normal. Symptoms usually peak at six to eight months and resolve by 12 to 24 months. Reflux often coexists and aggravates it.
!fig d59-1 | Laryngomalacia: the epiglottis folded longitudinally into an omega.
## Diagnosis
On flexible endoscopy in the awake infant: an *omega-shaped*, elongated epiglottis curled on itself; short, tight aryepiglottic folds tethering it; and redundant mucosa over large arytenoids that prolapses into the glottis on inspiration. Vocal fold movement is normal.
It cannot be judged in a paralysed, anaesthetised child.
## Subtypes and variants
One classification grades the collapse as type 1 (short aryepiglottic folds), type 2 (redundant arytenoid tissue) and type 3 (posterior collapse of the epiglottis, often with neuromuscular disease).
## Management
- **Most:** reassurance and observation; positioning; anti-reflux treatment.
- **Severe disease** (failure to thrive, feeding difficulty, apnoeas, cyanosis, pulmonary hypertension): microlaryngoscopy and bronchoscopy to exclude a second lesion, then endoscopic *supraglottoplasty*: division of the tight aryepiglottic folds and trimming of redundant arytenoid mucosa with cold steel or laser.
- **Tracheostomy** rarely.
!ops 193 | Supraglottoplasty for laryngomalacia.
!update Current evidence
**Laryngomalacia.** The International Pediatric Otolaryngology Group recommends supraglottoplasty for severe disease (feeding difficulty with failure to thrive, apnoeas, cyanosis, pulmonary hypertension), screening for synchronous airway lesions in severe cases, and treating reflux when present (Carter et al., *Int J Pediatr Otorhinolaryngol* 2016). Supraglottoplasty also improves obstructive sleep apnoea due to laryngomalacia: in a meta-analysis the apnoea-hypopnoea index fell from about 14 to 3 events an hour (Camacho et al., *Laryngoscope* 2016).
!!

# Congenital vocal fold paralysis {dz} | The second commonest.
## Etiology
Birth trauma ([[t:vfp]]; stretching of the recurrent nerve in breech or forceps delivery), central nervous system anomalies (Arnold–Chiari malformation and hydrocephalus, usually bilateral), cardiac surgery and ligation of the ductus arteriosus, cardiomegaly and mediastinal lesions (usually left-sided), and idiopathic.
## Clinical features
- **Unilateral:** a weak, breathy cry, mild stridor, and choking or aspiration with feeds.
- **Bilateral:** a good cry but marked inspiratory stridor and respiratory distress, because both folds lie near the midline.
## Diagnosis
Flexible endoscopy in the awake child; imaging of the brain, neck and chest; and a swallowing assessment.
## Management
Observation for unilateral paralysis, with feeding support. Bilateral paralysis often needs a tracheostomy; definitive airway surgery is deferred, because many recover within the first year or two, and the larynx grows. Shunting for Arnold–Chiari malformation may restore movement.

# Subglottic stenosis {dz} | The narrow ring.
## Subtypes and variants
### Congenital
Thickening of the cricoid cartilage (cartilaginous) or of the submucosa (soft tissue) below the vocal folds. Diagnosed when the subglottic lumen is under *4 mm in a term neonate* (normal 4.5 to 5.5 mm) or under 3 mm in a premature baby.
### Acquired
Far commoner now ([[t:lxtrauma]]): from prolonged or repeated *intubation* in premature and sick neonates, especially with oversized tubes and movement; presenting as failure to extubate or stridor after extubation.
## Clinical features
The child may be well until a cold produces stridor, recurrent or prolonged croup; the cry is normal.
## Diagnosis: grading
Cotton–Myer: grade I up to 50% obstruction, II 51 to 70%, III 71 to 99%, IV no lumen; assessed by the largest endotracheal tube that allows a leak.
## Management
Many mild congenital stenoses improve as the larynx grows. Options: observation; endoscopic balloon dilatation and radial incision; anterior cricoid split (in neonates failing extubation); laryngotracheal reconstruction with costal cartilage grafts; and cricotracheal resection for severe stenosis; tracheostomy where needed.
!ops 194 | Subglottic stenosis surgery.
!update Current evidence
**Subglottic stenosis.** Endoscopic balloon dilation succeeds for many soft, acquired, low-grade stenoses; firm or high-grade (Myer–Cotton III to IV) stenoses usually need open laryngotracheal reconstruction or cricotracheal resection.
!!

# Laryngeal web and atresia {dz} | Incomplete recanalisation.
## Etiology and pathophysiology
Incomplete recanalisation of the larynx in the tenth week. Usually an anterior glottic web between the vocal folds with a concave posterior edge; associated with 22q11 deletion (velocardiofacial syndrome; [[t:syndr]]), which should be tested for.
## Clinical features
A weak or absent cry from birth, and airway obstruction when the web is large.
!fig d59-2 | A laryngeal web.
## Management
Thin webs are divided endoscopically with knife or CO2 laser; thick webs often have subglottic extension and need excision through a laryngofissure with a silicone keel and later dilatations.
## Laryngeal atresia
*Laryngeal atresia* causes complete obstruction at birth and death unless an emergency airway is made (or a tracheo-oesophageal fistula provides one).

# Subglottic haemangioma {dz} | Stridor at six weeks.
## Clinical features
Asymptomatic at birth; stridor appears at six weeks to six months as the haemangioma proliferates, often presenting as recurrent croup, with a normal cry; worse with crying because of venous engorgement. About half have a cutaneous haemangioma, especially in the beard distribution; some have mediastinal lesions.
## Diagnosis
Endoscopy shows a soft, compressible, reddish-blue swelling, usually posterolateral in the subglottis; biopsy is not needed and may bleed. MRI or CT shows the extent.
## Management
- **Propranolol** is now first-line and often produces dramatic involution.
- Systemic steroids (classically dexamethasone, then prednisolone for many months) and intralesional steroid, now second line.
- CO2 or KTP laser or open excision for resistant lesions.
- Tracheostomy while awaiting involution, which is the natural history.
!update Current evidence
**Haemangioma.** In a randomised trial, oral propranolol 3 mg/kg/day for six months cleared or nearly cleared infantile haemangioma in 60% against 4% with placebo (Léauté-Labrèze et al., *NEJM* 2015), and it has replaced steroids as first-line treatment for subglottic haemangioma.
!!

# Laryngeal cysts {dz} | Saccular and ductal.
## Clinical features
*Saccular cysts* (a mucus-filled saccule) and *ductal cysts* in the aryepiglottic fold, vallecula or subglottis appear as smooth, bluish, fluid-filled swellings that obstruct the airway.
Subglottic cysts follow intubation in premature infants.
## Management
Needle aspiration can provide an emergency airway; endoscopic deroofing or excision with laser is definitive.

# Laryngocele {dz} | Air in the saccule.
## Pathophysiology and subtypes
An air-filled dilatation of the saccule rising between the false fold and the thyroid lamina: *internal* (within the larynx, a supraglottic bulge), *external* (through the thyrohyoid membrane, a neck swelling that enlarges with Valsalva), or *combined*.
## Differential diagnosis and complications
Rare in infants; in adults ([[t:benign]]) linked to wind instrument players and glass blowers, and sometimes to a ventricular carcinoma blocking the saccule, which must be excluded. Infection forms a *laryngopyocele*.
## Management
Endoscopic excision for internal ones, external excision for external and combined.

# Laryngeal cleft {dz} | A gap at the back.
## Pathophysiology
Incomplete fusion of the posterior cricoid lamina and tracheo-oesophageal septum, leaving a communication between larynx and oesophagus.
## Subtypes and variants
!table Benjamin–Inglis classification
Type | Extent
I | Interarytenoid, above the vocal folds
II | Partly into the cricoid lamina
III | Through the whole cricoid, into the cervical trachea
IV | Into the thoracic trachea
!!
## Clinical features
Choking, coughing and cyanosis with feeds, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, a hoarse cry and stridor.
## Diagnosis
Diagnosed at microlaryngoscopy by *palpating* the interarytenoid area with a probe, since small clefts are hidden by redundant mucosa.
## Management
Type I clefts may be managed by thickened feeds or endoscopic injection or repair; deeper clefts need endoscopic or open repair.
!ops 195 | Laryngeal cleft repair.

# The trachea and beyond {dz} | Not always the larynx.
- **Tracheomalacia:** a soft, collapsing trachea, primary or after compression or tracheo-oesophageal fistula repair; expiratory stridor and a barking cough; most improve with growth; aortopexy, stenting or tracheostomy for severe cases.
- **Vascular rings and slings:** a double aortic arch, right arch with ligamentum, anomalous innominate artery or pulmonary artery sling compress the trachea and oesophagus, causing stridor and dysphagia. Contrast CT or MRI and a barium swallow; surgical division.
- **Tracheo-oesophageal fistula and oesophageal atresia.**
- **Cri-du-chat syndrome** (deletion of the short arm of chromosome 5): a cat-like cry with a narrow, diamond-shaped glottis, learning disability and hypotonia.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Labelling every noisy baby laryngomalacia without looking at the whole airway when symptoms are severe.
- Biopsying a subglottic haemangioma.
- Missing a cleft because the interarytenoid area was not palpated.
