@stand Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia is a genetic disease of blood vessels that presents to the rhinologist as nosebleeds that never stop coming back. The nose is the visible part of a systemic disorder: every patient also needs screening for silent arteriovenous malformations in the lungs and brain.

# Summary | A vascular disease that bleeds through the nose.
HHT, also called *Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome*, is an autosomal dominant disorder of angiogenesis. It produces fragile *telangiectasias* in the skin and mucous membranes and larger *arteriovenous malformations* (AVMs) in internal organs. Recurrent epistaxis causes chronic iron-deficiency anaemia and repeated transfusions, and treating it badly (repeated packing and aggressive cautery) makes it worse. Pulmonary AVMs cause paradoxical embolism, stroke and brain abscess; cerebral AVMs can bleed. The diagnosis rests on the Curaçao criteria; every patient is screened for pulmonary and cerebral AVMs.

# Epidemiology | Who gets it.
About 1 in 5,000 to 10,000 people, reported mainly in white populations, affecting men and women equally. Penetrance increases with age.

# Etiology | Genetics.
Mutations disturb transforming growth factor-beta signalling in endothelial cells, so the connective tissue and matrix that should support new vessels during angiogenesis fail to form. Together, the three genes below explain about 90% of clinically diagnosed cases, and genetic testing is mainly used to screen relatives. The types are in [[@Subtypes and variants]].

# Pathophysiology | A defect in vessel signalling.
Postcapillary venules dilate and connect directly to dilated arterioles, losing the capillary bed between them. The walls lack elastic tissue and smooth muscle, so they cannot contract after injury: minor trauma from dry air or a sneeze causes brisk bleeding that does not stop easily.

# Clinical features | Nose, skin and organs.
## Epistaxis
The commonest and earliest feature: recurrent, spontaneous bleeding from childhood, from both sides, becoming more frequent with age. About 80 to 90% have nosebleeds by age 21.
## Mucocutaneous telangiectasias
Small red spots that blanch with pressure, on the lips, tongue, palate, fingertips, face and nasal mucosa. About 80 to 90% develop them by their thirties or forties.

## Visceral arteriovenous malformations
!table Organ involvement in HHT
Organ | Frequency | What it causes
Lungs | About 30% | Hypoxia, paradoxical emboli, stroke, brain abscess, haemoptysis
Brain | 5–15% | Haemorrhage, seizures
Liver | 40–70% | Usually silent; high-output heart failure, portal hypertension
Gut | 20–25% | Bleeding and anaemia
Spine | Rare | Spinal haemorrhage
!!
## Anaemia
Chronic iron-deficiency anaemia from nasal and gut bleeding.

# Diagnosis | The Curaçao criteria.
## Criteria
- **Epistaxis:** spontaneous and recurrent.
- **Telangiectasias:** multiple, at characteristic sites (lips, mouth, fingers, nose).
- **Visceral lesions:** gastrointestinal telangiectasia, or pulmonary, hepatic, cerebral or spinal AVMs.
- **Family history:** a first-degree relative with HHT.
## Interpretation
Three or more criteria: *definite*. Two: *possible* or suspected. Fewer than two: unlikely. Genetic testing confirms the diagnosis in uncertain cases and in relatives.
## Work-up
- Full head and neck examination with nasal endoscopy.
- Full blood count and iron studies.
- **Contrast echocardiography** (bubble study) to screen for pulmonary AVMs, in every patient.
- **MRI brain with contrast** for cerebral AVMs.
- Endoscopy for symptomatic gastrointestinal bleeding; Doppler ultrasound or CT for the liver.
- Genetic counselling.
!flag Screen the lungs
A pulmonary AVM can cause a stroke or brain abscess in a patient who has only ever complained about nosebleeds. Screen every patient with contrast echocardiography and treat significant AVMs by embolisation.
!!

# Differential diagnosis | Other recurrent bleeders.
Bleeding disorders (von Willebrand disease, platelet disorders), anticoagulant use, and a bleeding nasal tumour in one-sided bleeding ([[t:epistaxis]]).

# Subtypes and variants | By gene.
- **HHT type 1:** mutation in *endoglin* (chromosome 9q34); pulmonary and cerebral AVMs are commoner.
- **HHT type 2:** mutation in *ACVRL1*, also called ALK1 (chromosome 12q); hepatic AVMs are commoner.
- **Juvenile polyposis–HHT overlap:** mutation in *SMAD4*; these patients need colonoscopy because of colonic polyps and cancer risk.

# Management | Reduce bleeding without damaging the mucosa.
## Principles
The goal is fewer and smaller bleeds and a better haemoglobin, not cure. Avoid trauma to the mucosa: repeated packing and aggressive cautery destroy healthy mucosa, encourage new telangiectasias and can cause septal perforation. Treat the nose and the haemoglobin together. Treatment is stepped up by severity, judged by bleeding frequency, the haemoglobin and transfusion needs; the Epistaxis Severity Score helps track it.
## The acute bleed
Bleeding is almost always from the anterior third, so posterior packs are rarely needed. Gentle pressure; if packing is needed, resorbable packs such as Nasopore or Surgicel, or a haemostatic matrix, because removing a conventional pack, or an expanding sponge, tears other lesions and restarts bleeding. Give antibiotic cover for any pack, and avoid cautery of large areas. If packing fails, embolise the facial and maxillary feeders without coils.
## Conservative and medical treatment
!ladder
Humidification and emollients | Saline sprays, ointments and humidified air keep the mucosa moist.
Iron and transfusion | Correct anaemia; intravenous iron when oral iron fails.
Tranexamic acid | Oral antifibrinolytic reduces the frequency and severity of bleeds.
Hormonal treatment | Oestrogen or tamoxifen convert the mucosa to a thicker squamous type.
Systemic anti-angiogenic drugs | Intravenous bevacizumab or oral pomalidomide reduce bleeding and transfusion needs in severe disease, and are now used by specialist HHT centres; topical bevacizumab spray does not work.
!!
## Procedures
Benefit lasts months after laser, years after septodermoplasty, and permanently after nasal closure.
### Photocoagulation
KTP, argon, Nd:YAG or diode laser, bipolar diathermy or coblation, aimed at individual lesions and repeated every few months as new ones appear. The first intervention is usually an examination under anaesthesia with **KTP or argon laser** to stage and treat the disease: these green wavelengths are absorbed by haemoglobin, each lesion is circled and then ablated, tranexamic acid and antibiotic cover are given at induction, and no pack is needed. Bipolar diathermy works better than laser in a bloody field. If laser controls bleeding for less than about three months, move to septodermoplasty or systemic treatment.
### Septodermoplasty
The bleeding anterior septal mucosa is replaced with a split- or full-thickness skin graft, which is tougher. It halves the number of later laser treatments.
### Young's procedure
Closure of the nostrils, one or both sides, by suturing flaps inside the vestibule. Bleeding stops in most patients, often permanently, at the price of nasal breathing and smell; it is offered to transfusion-dependent patients who accept that. The Lund modification uses three vestibular flaps. Rebleeding after reopening is troublesome.
### Embolisation
Gives only temporary benefit because new collaterals form; reserved for emergencies ([[@Management/The acute bleed]]).
!ops
## Visceral AVMs
Significant pulmonary AVMs are treated by embolisation.
!update Current evidence
**Guidelines.** The Second International HHT Guidelines recommend moisturising topical therapies, oral tranexamic acid for epistaxis not controlled by moisturisers, laser or radiofrequency ablation, septodermoplasty or nasal closure for refractory bleeding, and systemic anti-angiogenic treatment (intravenous bevacizumab) for severe disease; they advise screening every patient for pulmonary and cerebral arteriovenous malformations (Faughnan et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2020). **Topical drugs.** In the NOSE trial, topical bevacizumab, oestriol and tranexamic acid sprays were no better than placebo (Whitehead et al., *JAMA* 2016). **Pomalidomide.** In the PATH-HHT trial of 144 patients, oral pomalidomide reduced epistaxis severity scores significantly more than placebo over 24 weeks (Al-Samkari et al., *NEJM* 2024).
!!

# Complications | Of the disease and of bad treatment.
- **Anaemia** and transfusion dependence.
- **Paradoxical embolism, stroke and brain abscess** from pulmonary AVMs.
- **Haemorrhage** from cerebral AVMs.
- **Septal perforation** and new telangiectasias after repeated packing and wide cautery.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Treating every bleed as simple epistaxis without asking about family history and looking at the lips and fingers.
- Repeated packing and wide cautery.
- Forgetting to screen the lungs.
