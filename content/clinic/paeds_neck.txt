@stand Most neck lumps in children are reactive nodes. The congenital masses that remain are explained by embryology. A midline cyst that rises when the tongue is put out is a thyroglossal cyst. A smooth lump in front of the upper third of sternomastoid is a branchial cyst, and a pit along its lower anterior border is a branchial sinus. A soft, transilluminant, multilocular swelling in a baby's posterior triangle is a lymphatic malformation. Each is cured by removing the whole tract, and each recurs if the tract is left behind.

# Embryology | Arches, clefts and pouches.
## The branchial apparatus
In weeks four to eight, five (some count six) *branchial arches* form on each side of the primitive pharynx.
- **Clefts:** separate the arches on the outside and are lined by ectoderm.
- **Pouches:** separate them on the inside and are lined by endoderm.
- **Each arch:** contains a cartilage bar, an artery, a nerve and muscle-forming mesoderm.
- **Cervical sinus of His:** the second arch overgrows the third and fourth to form the smooth neck, enclosing a temporary space.
- **How remnants form:** failure of clefts, pouches or the cervical sinus to obliterate leaves a *cyst* (no opening), a *sinus* (one opening, inside or out) or a *fistula* (both openings). The skin openings always lie *anterior to sternomastoid*, and the tract is deep to platysma.

!pearl Why the inferior parathyroid is "3" and lies below "4"
The third pouch gives rise to both the inferior parathyroid and the thymus. As the thymus descends into the chest, it drags the third-pouch parathyroid below the fourth-pouch one. That long journey explains why inferior glands are so often ectopic.
!!
## The thyroid and tongue
- **Thyroid:** begins at the *foramen caecum* at about four weeks. It descends through the tongue base and passes in front of, through or behind the developing hyoid. The connecting *thyroglossal duct* normally disappears by the sixth to eighth week.
- **Tongue:**
  - The anterior two-thirds come from the first-arch lingual swellings and the tuberculum impar.
  - The posterior third comes from the copula (second to fourth arches).
  - The muscles come from occipital somites (hypoglossal nerve).

# Midline masses | Thyroglossal and dermoid.
## Thyroglossal duct cyst
The commonest congenital neck mass.
- **Age:** usually presents in childhood, but can appear at any age.
- **Signs:**
  - A smooth, round, cystic *midline* swelling, 2 to 4 cm, usually just *below or at the hyoid*, sometimes slightly to the left.
  - It *moves up on swallowing and on protruding the tongue*, because it is tethered to the foramen caecum.
- **Infection:** may become infected after an upper respiratory infection. If it ruptures or is incised, it leaves a thyroglossal sinus or "fistula".
- **Sites:** lingual, suprahyoid, at the hyoid, subhyoid (the commonest), and suprasternal.
- **Rarities:**
  - It may contain the *only functioning thyroid tissue*.
  - About 1% contain carcinoma, almost always papillary.
- **Investigation:** ultrasound to confirm a cyst and a *normal thyroid gland*, with thyroid function. Scintigraphy if the gland is not seen. FNA for solid areas in adults.
- **Treatment: Sistrunk operation.**
  - Excise the cyst and tract *with the central body of the hyoid bone*, plus a core of tongue-base muscle along the tract up to the foramen caecum.
  - Simple cystectomy recurs in up to half of cases. The Sistrunk operation reduces recurrence to under 5 to 10%.
  - Operate once infection has settled; aspiration or antibiotics only during infection.

## Dermoid cyst
A midline cyst lined by skin with adnexa, from ectoderm trapped at lines of fusion.
- **Sites:** usually submental or in the floor of mouth (the *sublingual dermoid*), or at the suprasternal notch.
- **Signs:** it does not move with tongue protrusion. A sublingual dermoid above mylohyoid can push the tongue up and must be told apart from a ranula.
- **Treatment:** excision, intraorally or through the neck according to its relation to mylohyoid.

## Other midline masses
- **Teratoma:** in neonates, sometimes huge, may obstruct the airway. Plan an EXIT procedure (ex-utero intrapartum treatment) when diagnosed antenatally.
- **Ectopic thyroid.**
- **Delphian node.**
- **Thymic cyst:** usually lateral, deep to the lower third of sternomastoid.
- **Bronchogenic cyst:** suprasternal.

# Branchial anomalies | Lateral cysts, sinuses and fistulae.
## Second cleft anomalies
The commonest branchial anomalies, about 90%.
- **Cyst:**
  - Usually appears in older children and young adults (second to third decade).
  - A smooth, fluctuant, non-tender swelling at the junction of the upper and middle thirds of the anterior border of sternomastoid.
  - Often enlarges with upper respiratory infections and may become infected.
  - The fluid contains cholesterol crystals.
- **Sinus or fistula:**
  - Seen from infancy as a pit on the *lower third of the anterior border of sternomastoid* that discharges mucus.
  - Course of the tract:
    - Up along the carotid sheath, deep to platysma.
    - *Deep to the second-arch derivatives* (external carotid, stylohyoid, posterior digastric).
    - *Superficial to the third-arch derivatives* (internal carotid, glossopharyngeal nerve), passing between the carotids.
    - Over the hypoglossal nerve.
    - Ending in the *tonsillar fossa*.
- **Treatment:**
  - Complete excision after infection has settled.
  - Fistulae are removed through an elliptical incision around the opening, with a second, higher *step-ladder* incision.
  - Recurrence follows incomplete excision or operating during infection.

!flag The adult "branchial cyst"
A new cystic lateral neck mass in an adult over about 40 is far more often a *cystic metastasis* from HPV-related oropharyngeal carcinoma or papillary thyroid carcinoma than a branchial cyst. Examine the tonsils and tongue base, aspirate under ultrasound, test p16 and thyroglobulin, and image before excision.
!!
## First cleft anomalies
Duplication of the external ear canal.
- **Work type I:** ectodermal, a duplicate canal running parallel to the external canal.
- **Work type II:** ectodermal and mesodermal, containing cartilage.
- **Presentation:** a cyst, sinus or fistula near the angle of the mandible or in the parotid. It drains into or near the ear canal, often with recurrent parotid-region abscesses or otorrhoea.
- **Treatment:** the tract runs close to, and sometimes deep to, the facial nerve. Excision requires a parotidectomy approach with the nerve identified first.
## Third and fourth pouch anomalies
- **Opening:** these open into the *pyriform sinus*, almost always on the left.
- **Presentation:**
  - Recurrent *left-sided neck abscesses*.
  - *Acute suppurative thyroiditis* in children.
  - Lateral cysts low in the neck.
- **Course of a third-arch tract:** deep to the internal carotid and glossopharyngeal nerve, superficial to the hypoglossal nerve. It pierces the thyrohyoid membrane above the internal laryngeal nerve.
- **Course of a fourth-arch tract:** below the superior laryngeal nerve, looping round the subclavian on the right or the aortic arch on the left.
- **Diagnosis:** barium swallow, CT, and direct laryngoscopy to see the pyriform opening.
- **Treatment:**
  - Endoscopic cauterisation of the sinus opening (chemical or electrocautery).
  - Or open excision with partial thyroidectomy.

# Lymphatic and vascular | Malformations and haemangiomas.
## Lymphatic malformation (cystic hygroma)
A malformation of lymphatic channels from sequestration of the primitive jugular lymph sacs.
- **Age and site:**
  - About half are present at birth, and 90% appear by two years.
  - Most often in the *posterior triangle*. They extend to the floor of mouth and tongue, the parotid, axilla and mediastinum.
- **Macrocystic lesions (cystic hygroma):** soft, multilocular, compressible, *brilliantly transilluminant*.
- **Microcystic lesions:** infiltrate the tongue and face. The mucosa shows clear or blood-stained *vesicles*.
- **Course:** they swell suddenly with infection or bleeding into the cysts. Large ones obstruct labour or the airway and feeding.
- **Imaging:** MRI shows the extent. Antenatal ultrasound may detect them; large lesions need an EXIT delivery.
- **Treatment:**
  - *Sclerotherapy* (bleomycin, doxycycline, OK-432, ethanol) for macrocystic disease.
  - *Surgical excision*, preserving nerves and vessels. Recurrence is low after complete excision and about half after partial excision. Several stages may be needed.
  - *Sirolimus* for extensive microcystic disease.
  - Laser for mucosal vesicles.
  - Tracheostomy for airway obstruction.

## Infantile haemangioma and other vascular anomalies
Infantile haemangioma is the commonest tumour of infancy.
- **Course:** it appears after birth, proliferates for about a year, then slowly involutes.
- **Associations:**
  - A *beard-distribution* lesion means a subglottic haemangioma until proved otherwise.
  - Large segmental facial lesions suggest *PHACES syndrome*.
- **Treatment:** propranolol.

Venous malformations, capillary malformations and AVMs grow with the child; they are covered with the vascular anomalies.

# Other paediatric masses | Nodes, glands, muscle.
- **Sternomastoid tumour (fibromatosis colli):**
  - A firm, spindle-shaped mass in the sternomastoid of a two- to four-week-old baby, often after a difficult delivery.
  - Causes *torticollis*: the head tilts towards the lesion and the chin turns away.
  - Ultrasound confirms it.
  - Physiotherapy with stretching resolves most. Persistent cases need tenotomy to prevent plagiocephaly and facial asymmetry.
- **Thymic cyst:** a cervical remnant along the line of thymic descent, from the angle of the jaw to the mediastinum, usually on the left. It may extend into the chest.
- **Reactive and infective nodes:**
  - The commonest childhood neck masses.
  - Suppurative adenitis.
  - Atypical mycobacteria.
  - Cat-scratch disease.
  - Kawasaki disease (fever over five days, conjunctivitis, mucosal changes, a rash, a large node). Do not miss it: coronary aneurysms follow untreated disease.
- **Malignancy in children:**
  - Lymphoma (Hodgkin in adolescents).
  - Rhabdomyosarcoma.
  - Neuroblastoma.
  - Thyroid carcinoma.
  - Nasopharyngeal carcinoma in endemic areas.
  - Warning signs: a hard, fixed or matted node over 2 to 3 cm, supraclavicular site, growth over two to six weeks despite antibiotics, and B symptoms. These need biopsy.
- **Salivary masses:** infantile haemangioma of the parotid, ranula, and juvenile recurrent parotitis.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Embryology:** the arches with their nerves and derivatives; the pouches and what they form.
- **Thyroglossal cyst:**
  - Clinical signs.
  - Check for a normal thyroid.
  - The Sistrunk operation and why the hyoid is removed.
- **Second branchial fistula:** its course and the step-ladder excision.
- **Third and fourth pouch sinuses:** recurrent left neck abscess and thyroiditis.
- **Cystic hygroma:** features and management.
- **The adult "branchial cyst":** a metastasis until proved otherwise.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Simple excision of a thyroglossal cyst without the hyoid and tract.
- Removing a thyroglossal "cyst" that is the patient's only thyroid tissue.
- Incising and draining a branchial cyst or atypical mycobacterial node.
- Missing a first cleft anomaly as a recurrent "parotid abscess" or discharging ear.
!!
