@stand Tumours of the middle ear and temporal bone are rare, and two of them must never be missed. The first is a red, pulsating mass behind the drum, a glomus tumour that bleeds torrentially if biopsied in clinic. The second is the chronic, painful, bleeding ear that is really a squamous cell carcinoma.

# Summary | Rare, but two are not to be missed.
Both key tumours present to the ear clinic looking like something common: pulsatile tinnitus or a polyp, and a discharging ear. Each tumour has its own chapter below.

# Diagnosis | The first steps for any ear mass.
!ward On call: a mass or granulation in the ear
1. Pulsatile red mass: do not biopsy; arrange CT and MRI.
2. Non-healing, bleeding granulation in a chronic ear: biopsy it.
3. Hypertension, sweating or palpitations with a glomus tumour: check catecholamines before any anaesthetic.
!!

# Differential diagnosis | Red, white or blue behind the drum.
!table Red, white or blue behind the drum
Colour | Think of | Key test
Red, pulsatile | Glomus tumour | CT and MRI
Pink, anterior | Aberrant internal carotid artery | CT, MR angiography
Blue, inferior | High jugular bulb | CT, MRI
Blue with dark fluid | Cholesterol granuloma ([[t:petrous]]) | CT, MRI
White | Congenital cholesteatoma | CT
!!
Pain is more typical of carcinoma than of a glomus tumour.

# Paraganglioma {dz} | The glomus tumour.
## Epidemiology
Usually in the fourth and fifth decades, with a strong female predominance (about 5 to 1).
## Etiology
### Origin
Paragangliomas arise from neural-crest *glomus bodies* along the tympanic branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve (Jacobson's nerve) and the auricular branch of the vagus (Arnold's nerve), and on the dome of the jugular bulb.
!anat s54_20 s25_19 | The tympanic plexus on the promontory (Jacobson's nerve), and the keel of bone between the carotid canal and jugular fossa.
### Genetics
Some are familial, from mutations in the succinate dehydrogenase genes. SDHD mutations cause multiple tumours and are expressed only when inherited from the father; SDHB mutations carry a higher risk of malignancy.
!differ How often
Dhingra: About 10% familial, about 3% secrete catecholamines, about 4% metastasise.
Lee: Up to 30% familial, 5% or fewer secrete, 2–4% metastasise.
In practice: Current series find about a third hereditary, so offer every patient genetic testing, and check for secretion before any anaesthetic.
!!
## Pathophysiology
They are benign, slow-growing and highly vascular. Histology: nests of chief cells (*zellballen*) surrounded by sustentacular cells in a vascular stroma.
## Clinical features
### Symptoms
Pulsatile tinnitus (about 80%), conductive hearing loss (about 60%), fullness, and later bleeding from the ear. Jugulare tumours add lower cranial nerve palsies (ninth to twelfth: hoarseness, dysphagia, wasting of the tongue and trapezius) and facial weakness.
### Signs
A red mass behind the drum, the *rising sun* when it emerges from the floor of the middle ear. Pneumatic pressure on the drum makes the mass pulsate, then blanch (*Brown's sign*). A bruit may be heard over the mastoid. In advanced cases a bleeding polyp fills the canal.
!fig d17-1 | Glomus jugulare: a red mass behind the drum.
!flag Never biopsy in clinic
A red, pulsatile mass behind the drum, or a polyp with pulsatile tinnitus, is a glomus tumour until imaged. Biopsy can cause catastrophic bleeding.
!!
### Secreting tumours
Hypertension, headache, palpitations and sweating mean catecholamine secretion: measure urinary or plasma metanephrines before any anaesthetic.
## Diagnosis
A glomus tympanicum is only certain when its whole rim is visible; otherwise image.
### CT
Erosion of the *caroticojugular spine* separates a jugulare tumour from a tympanicum. CT also excludes an aberrant carotid artery and a high jugular bulb.
### MRI
A *salt-and-pepper* appearance from flow voids; defines intracranial and neck extension.
!imaging glomus-tympanicum-paraganglioma glomus-jugulare-tumor-paraganglioma | Glomus tympanicum and glomus jugulare on CT and MRI.
### Angiography
Shows the feeding vessels (mainly the ascending pharyngeal artery), looks for multiple tumours, and allows embolisation. Balloon occlusion testing if the carotid may need to be sacrificed.
### Catecholamines and genetic testing
Urinary or plasma metanephrines before any anaesthetic; genetic testing for every patient.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Temporal bone paraganglioma: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Never in clinic | Biopsy of a pulsatile red mass | Risk of severe bleeding
Every patient, before any anaesthetic | Plasma free or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines (with 3-methoxytyramine) | Secreting tumour (a minority)
First imaging | High-resolution CT temporal bone | Caroticojugular spine erosion (jugulare); excludes aberrant carotid and high bulb
Gold standard for extent | MRI with contrast (and MR angiography) | Salt-and-pepper flow voids; neck and intracranial spread
Multiple or metastatic tumours | Functional imaging: 68Ga-DOTATATE PET-CT | Multifocal disease
Before surgery | Catheter angiography with embolisation; balloon occlusion test if the carotid may be sacrificed | Feeding vessels; collateral flow
Every patient | Germline genetic testing (SDHx and related genes) | About a third are hereditary
Every patient | Pure-tone audiogram; lower cranial nerve assessment | Baseline
!!
## Subtypes and variants
### Glomus tympanicum
Arises on the promontory and fills the middle ear.
### Glomus jugulare
Arises in the jugular bulb, eroding upwards into the middle ear and medially to the jugular foramen and skull base.
### Glomus vagale
Arises on the vagus below the skull base, presenting in the neck ([[t:vascneck]]).
### Staging
!table Glasscock-Jackson classification
Stage | Glomus tympanicum | Glomus jugulare
I | Limited to the promontory | Jugular bulb, middle ear and mastoid
II | Fills the middle ear | Extends under the internal auditory canal
III | Into the mastoid | Into the petrous apex
IV | Through the drum into the canal, or beyond | Beyond the apex into the clivus or infratemporal fossa
!!
The *Fisch* classification grades A (middle ear), B (tympanomastoid), C (infralabyrinthine and apex, C1 to C4 by carotid involvement) and D (intracranial).
## Management
### Surgery
The treatment of choice for most, after embolisation 24 to 48 hours before and alpha-blockade if the tumour secretes. Small tympanicum tumours whose whole margin can be seen are removed through the canal; larger tumours need hypotympanic, extended facial recess or mastoid-neck approaches; jugulare tumours need the Fisch infratemporal fossa approach.
!ops
### Radiotherapy
Stereotactic radiosurgery or fractionated radiotherapy arrests growth in about 90 to 94%, though the tumour remains. For the elderly, the unfit, residual disease and tumours where surgery would cost lower cranial nerves.
### Observation
Slow-growing tumours in elderly patients may simply be watched.
!update Current evidence
**Genetics.** About a third of head and neck paragangliomas are hereditary, so guidelines recommend genetic testing (SDHx and related genes) for every patient, with screening of relatives who carry a mutation. **Radiosurgery.** A meta-analysis found tumour control in about 95% of jugular paragangliomas after radiosurgery, with fewer new cranial neuropathies than surgery (Guss et al., *Int J Radiat Oncol Biol Phys* 2011), so radiosurgery or observation is increasingly preferred for large jugular tumours in older patients, and surgery for tympanic tumours and selected young patients.
!!
### Treatment steps
!table Temporal bone paraganglioma: treatment
Step | Treatment and dose | Course and notes
Secreting tumour | Alpha-blockade first (phenoxybenzamine, starting about 10 mg once or twice a day, or doxazosin), titrated; beta-blocker only after alpha-blockade | 7 to 14 days before surgery, with high salt and fluid intake
Before surgery | Embolisation | 24 to 48 hours before
Most fit patients | Surgical excision | Approach by Fisch class
Elderly, unfit, residual or high-risk tumours | Stereotactic radiosurgery or fractionated radiotherapy | Growth control about 90%
Slow tumour, elderly | Observation with serial MRI | 
!!
Based on the Endocrine Society guideline on phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma (Lenders et al., *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 2014) and the BNF.
## Complications
- **Catastrophic bleeding** after biopsy in clinic.
- **Lower cranial nerve palsies** from jugulare tumours, or from surgery.
- **Metastasis** in a few, more often with SDHB mutations.

# Squamous cell carcinoma {dz} | The chronic ear that bleeds.
## Epidemiology
Adults of 40 to 60, about three-quarters with a long history of chronic ear discharge.
## Clinical features
Bloodstained, foul discharge; deep, boring pain, especially at night; friable granulation or a polyp that bleeds on touch; and later facial palsy, vertigo, trismus and a neck or parotid mass. It is easily mistaken for chronic otitis media ([[t:csom]]) or otitis externa ([[t:oe]]).
## Diagnosis
Biopsy of any non-healing granulation in the canal or middle ear. CT of the temporal bone and neck, MRI for soft tissue and dura, and chest imaging.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Temporal bone squamous cell carcinoma: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Diagnosis (gold standard) | Biopsy of any non-healing granulation or polyp | Histology
Bone extent | High-resolution CT of the temporal bone | Erosion of canal, middle ear, facial canal, temporomandibular joint
Soft tissue, dura and nerves | Contrast MRI | Dural and perineural spread; parotid and infratemporal fossa
Staging | CT or MRI of the neck; CT chest or PET-CT | Nodes and distant spread
Every patient | Audiogram; facial nerve grading | Baseline
!!
## Staging
Staged by the modified Pittsburgh system, as for canal carcinoma ([[t:extca]]).
## Management
- **Limited to the canal without bone involvement:** lateral temporal bone resection; sleeve resection of the canal only for the most limited tumours.
- **Bony canal involved:** lateral temporal bone resection.
- **Middle ear involved:** subtotal temporal bone resection.
- **Parotidectomy and neck dissection** according to spread.
- **Postoperative radiotherapy** for most stages beyond the earliest, with chemotherapy for advanced disease.
!update Current evidence
**Temporal bone SCC.** The modified Pittsburgh staging system guides treatment (Moody et al., *Am J Otol* 2000): lateral temporal bone resection for early tumours, subtotal resection with parotidectomy and neck dissection for advanced ones, and postoperative radiotherapy for all but the earliest.
!!
### Treatment steps
!table Temporal bone squamous cell carcinoma: treatment
Stage (modified Pittsburgh) | Treatment | Notes
T1 | Lateral temporal bone resection (sleeve resection only for the most limited canal tumours) | Radiotherapy if margins are close
T2 | Lateral temporal bone resection with parotidectomy and selective neck dissection | Postoperative radiotherapy for most
T3 and T4 | Subtotal temporal bone resection, parotidectomy, neck dissection, reconstruction | Postoperative radiotherapy (about 60 to 66 Gy), with concurrent chemotherapy (cisplatin) for high-risk features
Unresectable or unfit | Radical radiotherapy with or without chemotherapy | Poor outlook
!!
Based on multidisciplinary head and neck practice (UK national multidisciplinary guidelines, *J Laryngol Otol* 2016) and published series.
## Prognosis
Outlook is poor once the middle ear and beyond are involved. The dura usually resists invasion.

# Rhabdomyosarcoma {dz} | The child with a polyp and a palsy.
## Epidemiology
The commonest malignant ear tumour in children.
## Clinical features
A polyp in a child's ear with early facial palsy.
!fig d17-2 | A four-year-old child with rhabdomyosarcoma of the right middle ear and mastoid, with facial palsy on the same side.
## Management
Chemoradiation, with resection when feasible ([[t:paeds_ca]]).

# Endolymphatic sac tumour {dz} | A Ménière's mimic.
## Etiology
A locally aggressive papillary tumour of the sac, sporadic or with von Hippel-Lindau disease (about 11% of patients, often bilateral).
## Clinical features
It mimics [[t:meniere|Ménière's disease]], or causes sudden hearing loss from bleeding into the inner ear.
## Management
Removed with the dura around the sac; VHL patients are screened by MRI every one to three years.

# Facial nerve schwannoma and haemangioma {dz} | Tumours of the nerve itself.
## Clinical features
A schwannoma causes slowly progressive facial palsy ([[t:facial]]) or hearing loss; facial schwannomas arise most often near the geniculate ganglion, and many patients keep good facial function for years. A haemangioma near the geniculate ganglion causes a palsy out of proportion to its small size.
## Management
- **Haemangioma:** should be removed early.
- **Schwannoma:** removal means sacrificing and grafting the nerve, and the best result after a graft is House–Brackmann III, so surgery is reserved for weakness worse than grade III; enlarging tumours with good function are treated by radiosurgery (control in about 90% at five years, with function preserved in 90%) or by bony decompression of the canal.

# Adenomatous neuroendocrine tumour {dz} | A mass behind an intact drum.
## Clinical features
A benign middle ear tumour presenting as a mass behind an intact drum with conductive loss.

# Chordoma and chondrosarcoma {dz} | The skull base.
## Clinical features
- **Chordoma:** arises in the midline clivus in older patients, with headache and sixth nerve palsy.
- **Chondrosarcoma:** arises off the midline at the petroclival junction.
## Diagnosis
Chordoma is bright on T2 MRI and contains physaliferous cells.
## Management
Both are treated by maximal resection followed by high-dose or proton radiotherapy ([[t:skullbase]]).
