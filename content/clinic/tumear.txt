@stand Tumours of the middle ear and temporal bone are rare, and two of them must never be missed. The first is a red, pulsating mass behind the drum, a glomus tumour that bleeds torrentially if biopsied in clinic. The second is the chronic, painful, bleeding ear that is really a squamous cell carcinoma.

# Overview | Rare, but two are not to be missed.
## The tumours

## Why it matters
Both key tumours present to the ear clinic looking like something common: pulsatile tinnitus or a polyp, and a discharging ear.

# Paraganglioma | The glomus tumour.
## Origin
Paragangliomas arise from neural-crest *glomus bodies* along the tympanic branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve (Jacobson's nerve) and the auricular branch of the vagus (Arnold's nerve), and on the dome of the jugular bulb. They are benign, slow-growing and highly vascular.
!anat s54_20 s25_19 | The tympanic plexus on the promontory (Jacobson's nerve), and the keel of bone between the carotid canal and jugular fossa.
## Who gets them
Usually in the fourth and fifth decades, with a strong female predominance (about 5 to 1).
## Pathology
Nests of chief cells (*zellballen*) surrounded by sustentacular cells in a vascular stroma.
## Genetics
Some are familial, from mutations in the succinate dehydrogenase genes. SDHD mutations cause multiple tumours and are expressed only when inherited from the father; SDHB mutations carry a higher risk of malignancy.
!differ How often
Dhingra: About 10% familial, about 3% secrete catecholamines, about 4% metastasise.
Lee: Up to 30% familial, 5% or fewer secrete, 2–4% metastasise.
!!
## Types
### Glomus tympanicum
Arises on the promontory and fills the middle ear.
### Glomus jugulare
Arises in the jugular bulb, eroding upwards into the middle ear and medially to the jugular foramen and skull base.
### Glomus vagale
Arises on the vagus below the skull base, presenting in the neck.
## Clinical features
### Symptoms
Pulsatile tinnitus (about 80%), conductive hearing loss (about 60%), fullness, and later bleeding from the ear. Jugulare tumours add lower cranial nerve palsies (ninth to twelfth: hoarseness, dysphagia, wasting of the tongue and trapezius) and facial weakness.
### Signs
A red mass behind the drum, the *rising sun* when it emerges from the floor of the middle ear. Pneumatic pressure on the drum makes the mass pulsate, then blanch (*Brown's sign*). A bruit may be heard over the mastoid. In advanced cases a bleeding polyp fills the canal.
!fig d17-1 | Glomus jugulare: a red mass behind the drum.
!flag Never biopsy in clinic
A red, pulsatile mass behind the drum, or a polyp with pulsatile tinnitus, is a glomus tumour until imaged. Biopsy can cause catastrophic bleeding.
!!
### Secreting tumours
Hypertension, headache, palpitations and sweating mean catecholamine secretion: measure urinary or plasma metanephrines before any anaesthetic.
## Investigations
### CT
Erosion of the *caroticojugular spine* separates a jugulare tumour from a tympanicum. CT also excludes an aberrant carotid artery and a high jugular bulb.
### MRI
A *salt-and-pepper* appearance from flow voids; defines intracranial and neck extension.
!imaging glomus-tympanicum-paraganglioma glomus-jugulare-tumor-paraganglioma | Glomus tympanicum and glomus jugulare on CT and MRI.
### Angiography
Shows the feeding vessels (mainly the ascending pharyngeal artery), looks for multiple tumours, and allows embolisation. Balloon occlusion testing if the carotid may need to be sacrificed.
## Staging
!table Glasscock-Jackson classification
Stage | Glomus tympanicum | Glomus jugulare
I | Limited to the promontory | Jugular bulb, middle ear and mastoid
II | Fills the middle ear | Extends under the internal auditory canal
III | Into the mastoid | Into the petrous apex
IV | Through the drum into the canal, or beyond | Beyond the apex into the clivus or infratemporal fossa
!!
The *Fisch* classification grades A (middle ear), B (tympanomastoid), C (infralabyrinthine and apex, C1 to C4 by carotid involvement) and D (intracranial).
## Differential diagnosis
!table Red, white or blue behind the drum
Colour | Think of | Key test
Red, pulsatile | Glomus tumour | CT and MRI
Pink, anterior | Aberrant internal carotid artery | CT, MR angiography
Blue, inferior | High jugular bulb | CT, MRI
Blue with dark fluid | Cholesterol granuloma | CT, MRI
White | Congenital cholesteatoma | CT
!!
## Treatment
### Surgery
The treatment of choice for most, after embolisation 24 to 48 hours before and alpha-blockade if the tumour secretes. Small tympanicum tumours whose whole margin can be seen are removed through the canal; larger tumours need hypotympanic, extended facial recess or mastoid-neck approaches; jugulare tumours need the Fisch infratemporal fossa approach.
!ops
### Radiotherapy
Stereotactic radiosurgery or fractionated radiotherapy arrests growth in about 90 to 94%, though the tumour remains. For the elderly, the unfit, residual disease and tumours where surgery would cost lower cranial nerves.
### Observation
Slow-growing tumours in elderly patients may simply be watched.
!pearl Pearls
- A glomus tympanicum is only certain when its whole rim is visible; otherwise image.
- Pain is more typical of carcinoma than of a glomus tumour.
!!

# Squamous cell carcinoma | The chronic ear that bleeds.
## Who gets it
Adults of 40 to 60, about three-quarters with a long history of chronic ear discharge.
## Clinical features
Bloodstained, foul discharge; deep, boring pain, especially at night; friable granulation or a polyp that bleeds on touch; and later facial palsy, vertigo, trismus and a neck or parotid mass. It is easily mistaken for chronic otitis media or otitis externa.
## Investigations
Biopsy of any non-healing granulation in the canal or middle ear. CT of the temporal bone and neck, MRI for soft tissue and dura, and chest imaging.
## Treatment
- **Limited to the canal without bone involvement:** sleeve resection of the canal.
- **Bony canal involved:** lateral temporal bone resection.
- **Middle ear involved:** subtotal temporal bone resection.
- **Parotidectomy and neck dissection** according to spread.
- **Postoperative radiotherapy** for most stages beyond the earliest, with chemotherapy for advanced disease.
Outlook is poor once the middle ear and beyond are involved. The dura usually resists invasion.

# Other tumours | Rarer, each with a clue.
## Rhabdomyosarcoma
The commonest malignant ear tumour in children: a polyp in a child's ear with early facial palsy. Treated with chemoradiation, with resection when feasible.
!fig d17-2 | A four-year-old child with rhabdomyosarcoma of the right middle ear and mastoid, with facial palsy on the same side.
## Endolymphatic sac tumour
A locally aggressive papillary tumour of the sac, sporadic or with von Hippel-Lindau disease (about 11% of patients, often bilateral). It mimics Ménière's disease, or causes sudden hearing loss from bleeding into the inner ear. Removed with the dura around the sac; VHL patients are screened by MRI every one to three years.
## Facial nerve schwannoma and haemangioma
A schwannoma causes slowly progressive facial palsy or hearing loss; a haemangioma near the geniculate ganglion causes a palsy out of proportion to its small size and should be removed early. Facial schwannomas arise most often near the geniculate ganglion, and many patients keep good facial function for years. Because removal means sacrificing and grafting the nerve, and the best result after a graft is House–Brackmann III, surgery is reserved for weakness worse than grade III; enlarging tumours with good function are treated by radiosurgery (control in about 90% at five years, with function preserved in 90%) or by bony decompression of the canal.
## Adenomatous neuroendocrine tumour
A benign middle ear tumour presenting as a mass behind an intact drum with conductive loss.
!imaging adenoma-of-the-middle-ear endolymphatic-sac-tumor malignancy-of-the-external-auditory-canal-squamo fibrous-dysplasia-1 pseudotumors-in-the-apex-of-the-petrous-bone | Middle ear adenoma, endolymphatic sac tumour, canal carcinoma, fibrous dysplasia and apical pseudotumours.
## Chordoma and chondrosarcoma
Chordoma arises in the midline clivus in older patients, with headache and sixth nerve palsy; it is bright on T2 MRI and contains physaliferous cells. Chondrosarcoma arises off the midline at the petroclival junction. Both are treated by maximal resection followed by high-dose or proton radiotherapy.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## On call
!ward A mass or granulation in the ear
1. Pulsatile red mass: do not biopsy; arrange CT and MRI.
2. Non-healing, bleeding granulation in a chronic ear: biopsy it.
3. Hypertension, sweating or palpitations with a glomus tumour: check catecholamines before any anaesthetic.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Biopsying a red mass behind the drum in clinic.
- Treating a bleeding, painful ear as chronic otitis media for months.
- Forgetting catecholamines before surgery.
!!
