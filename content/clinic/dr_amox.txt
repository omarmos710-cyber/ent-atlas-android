@stand Amoxicillin is a broad-spectrum aminopenicillin and the antibiotic an ENT doctor prescribes most often: first-line for acute otitis media in children, a choice in acute bacterial sinusitis, and the base on which co-amoxiclav is built. It is cheap, well absorbed and safe, with two traps: it is inactivated by beta-lactamase, and in a patient with glandular fever it causes a widespread rash.

# How it works | Mechanism and handling.
## Mechanism
Amoxicillin inhibits the transpeptidases (penicillin-binding proteins) that cross-link peptidoglycan, so dividing bacteria lyse. Killing is **time-dependent**: what matters is how long the free drug stays above the minimum inhibitory concentration, which is why it is given three times a day and why higher doses overcome intermediate pneumococcal resistance. Resistance comes from **beta-lactamase** (about a fifth to a third of H. influenzae and almost all Moraxella catarrhalis) and from altered penicillin-binding proteins in pneumococci.
## Absorption and handling
Oral absorption is about 90% and is not reduced by food, unlike ampicillin. It is excreted unchanged by the kidney; reduce the dose when the eGFR is below 30 ml/min. It penetrates middle ear fluid and the sinuses well but crosses the uninflamed blood-brain barrier poorly.

# Using it in ENT | Indications and doses.
## Acute otitis media
Most acute otitis media resolves without antibiotics, so NICE advises **no antibiotic or a delayed (back-up) prescription** for most children, with regular paracetamol or ibuprofen. Give an immediate antibiotic to children who are systemically unwell or at high risk of complications, those under 2 with bilateral disease, and children with otorrhoea from a perforation. Amoxicillin is first-line for **5 to 7 days**; clarithromycin or erythromycin if allergic; co-amoxiclav as second-line when symptoms do not improve after 2 to 3 days.
## Acute sinusitis
Symptoms for under 10 days need no antibiotic. For symptoms lasting more than 10 days, a back-up prescription can be considered; phenoxymethylpenicillin is the NICE first choice in adults, and amoxicillin is widely used elsewhere and in children. Co-amoxiclav is kept for patients who are systemically very unwell or have signs of a complication, who need assessment rather than a prescription.
## Other uses
- **Lyme disease** (erythema migrans, facial palsy): amoxicillin or doxycycline, for 14 to 21 days.
- **Dental infection** with spreading cellulitis: often with metronidazole.
- **Helicobacter pylori**: as part of triple therapy.

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
- **Diarrhoea**, nausea and candidiasis; Clostridioides difficile infection is less common than with co-amoxiclav or cephalosporins.
- **Rash.** A delayed, itchy maculopapular rash appears in a few per cent of patients, often not true allergy. In **infectious mononucleosis** (and with cytomegalovirus and lymphatic leukaemia) a widespread maculopapular rash is common; it is not IgE-mediated and does not prove penicillin allergy, but it frightens patients and clinicians into lifelong labels.
- **True hypersensitivity**: urticaria, angioedema and anaphylaxis (about 1 in 5,000 to 10,000 courses).
- Rarely interstitial nephritis, seizures at high doses in renal failure, and haemolytic anaemia.
## Contraindications and cautions
- **Penicillin allergy** of any type (immediate or severe delayed).
- **Glandular fever** suspected: avoid; use phenoxymethylpenicillin or clarithromycin for a sore throat with exudate when an antibiotic is truly needed.
- Renal impairment: dose adjustment.
## Interactions
Increases the effect of **warfarin** (check the INR). Reduces the excretion of **methotrexate**. Allopurinol increases the risk of rash.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Spectrum and why it fails against **beta-lactamase producers**.
- NICE approach to **acute otitis media**: no or back-up antibiotic for most; who needs one at once; 5 to 7 days.
- Why amoxicillin is avoided in a sore throat that may be **glandular fever**.
- Time-dependent killing and dosing frequency.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Giving amoxicillin for an exudative tonsillitis in a teenager without considering mononucleosis.
- Labelling a mononucleosis rash as lifelong penicillin allergy.
- Prescribing amoxicillin for an otitis externa or a perichondritis (staphylococci and Pseudomonas).
- Prescribing for a viral upper respiratory infection.
!!
