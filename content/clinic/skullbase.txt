@stand The skull base is the floor of the brain and the roof of the nose, sinuses, nasopharynx, ear and neck. Tumours here present to the ENT surgeon as a blocked nose, a unilateral middle ear effusion, facial numbness, diplopia, hoarseness or a lump behind the tonsil. Modern skull base surgery is a team effort with neurosurgeons. Endoscopic endonasal routes now reach the sella, clivus and much of the anterior skull base without a facial incision. The parapharyngeal space, just below, has a logic of its own: decide whether the mass lies in front of or behind the styloid.

# Applied anatomy | Three fossae and the spaces below.
## Anterior skull base
- **What it separates:** the frontal lobes from the nose, ethmoid sinuses and orbits.
- **Midline structures:**
  - The *cribriform plate*, the thinnest part, carrying the olfactory fila. Next to it is the thin *lateral lamella*, a common site of CSF leak.
  - The *crista galli*, where the falx attaches.
  - The *planum sphenoidale* behind.
- **Lateral structures:** the orbital roofs, the *fovea ethmoidalis* (part of the frontal bone), and the anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries crossing the ethmoid roof.
## Middle cranial fossa and the central skull base
- **Contents:** the temporal lobes.
- **The sphenoid body:** contains the sphenoid sinus, with the *sella turcica* above it holding the pituitary. *Cavernous sinuses* lie on each side.
- **Cavernous sinus contents:**
  - In the lateral wall: III, IV, V1 and V2.
  - Free in the sinus beside the internal carotid: VI. This is why VI is often the first nerve to fail.
- **Foramina:**
  - Superior orbital fissure (III, IV, V1, VI).
  - Optic canal.
  - Foramen rotundum (V2).
  - Foramen ovale (V3).
  - Foramen spinosum (middle meningeal artery).
  - Foramen lacerum and the carotid canal.
- **The clivus:** slopes from the dorsum sellae to the foramen magnum. Its upper part is the basisphenoid and its lower part the basiocciput.
- **Sphenoid wall landmarks:** bulges of the carotid and optic nerve, separated by the *optico-carotid recess*. The carotid wall is dehiscent in some people.
- **Onodi cell:** a posterior ethmoid cell that extends above the sphenoid. The optic nerve may run in it.
## Posterior fossa and lateral skull base
- **The petrous temporal bone:** contains the cochlea, labyrinth, facial nerve and internal carotid artery.
- **Petrous apex:** its anterior surface is the floor of the middle fossa (arcuate eminence, greater superficial petrosal nerve, *Kawase triangle*).
- **Posterior surface:** carries the internal auditory canal.
- **Jugular foramen:** carries IX, X and XI with the jugular bulb.
- **Hypoglossal canal:** carries XII, below and medial to the jugular foramen.
## Infratemporal and pterygopalatine fossae
- **Infratemporal fossa:** the space below the middle cranial fossa, behind the maxilla, deep to the ramus. It contains the pterygoid muscles, V3, the maxillary artery and the pterygoid venous plexus.
- **Pterygopalatine fossa:** a small, inverted cone behind the maxillary sinus. It communicates with:
  - The orbit (inferior orbital fissure).
  - The infratemporal fossa (pterygomaxillary fissure).
  - The nose (sphenopalatine foramen).
  - The middle cranial fossa (foramen rotundum, vidian canal).
  - The mouth (greater palatine canal).
  - It is the crossroads by which juvenile angiofibroma and perineural cancer spread.

# The parapharyngeal space | An inverted pyramid.
## Boundaries and compartments
- **Shape:** an inverted pyramid. Its base is the skull base and its apex the greater cornu of the hyoid.
- **Boundaries:**
  - *Medially*: the buccopharyngeal fascia and superior constrictor (the tonsil).
  - *Laterally*: the medial pterygoid, the ramus and the deep lobe of the parotid.
  - *Posteriorly*: the prevertebral fascia.
  - *Anteriorly*: the pterygomandibular raphe.
- **Compartments:** the *tensor–styloid fascia* (the styloid process and its muscles) divides it into two.
  - The *prestyloid compartment*: fat, the deep lobe of the parotid, minor salivary rests, the maxillary artery and branches of V3.
  - The *poststyloid (carotid) compartment*: the internal carotid artery, internal jugular vein, IX to XII, the sympathetic chain and nodes.

## Tumours
!table Parapharyngeal space tumours by compartment
Compartment | Common tumours | Imaging hint
Prestyloid | *Pleomorphic adenoma* (deep lobe or minor salivary), other salivary tumours, lipoma | Fat plane between tumour and parotid lost (deep lobe origin) or preserved (minor salivary); carotid pushed *posteriorly*
Poststyloid | *Schwannoma* (vagus, sympathetic chain), *paraganglioma* (vagal, carotid body), neurofibroma, nodes (metastases, lymphoma), meningioma | Carotid pushed *anteriorly* or splayed; flow voids in paragangliomas
!!
- **Frequency:** about half are salivary, mostly pleomorphic adenoma. About a fifth to a third are neurogenic. The rest are paragangliomas and others. Most (about 80%) are benign.
- **Presentation:**
  - Often silent until large.
  - A smooth bulge pushing the *tonsil and soft palate medially*, with normal overlying mucosa.
  - A fullness behind the angle of the jaw.
  - Muffled voice, sleep apnoea, eustachian tube obstruction, dysphagia.
  - Cranial nerve palsies, Horner syndrome or pain suggest malignancy or a neural tumour.
- **Investigation:**
  - MRI (and CT) to place the tumour and see the vessels.
  - Metanephrines when a paraganglioma is possible.
  - Image-guided FNA for prestyloid and nodal masses.
  - *No transoral biopsy* of a suspected pleomorphic adenoma (seeding) or paraganglioma (bleeding).
- **Approaches:**
  - *Transcervical*: through the submandibular triangle, dividing the stylomandibular ligament. The workhorse.
  - *Transparotid–transcervical*: for deep lobe tumours.
  - *Transcervical with mandibulotomy*: for large tumours reaching the skull base.
  - *Transoral robotic*: for selected prestyloid tumours.
  - The old blind transoral enucleation risks rupture, bleeding and incomplete excision.

# Skull base tumours | What arises here.
## Anterior skull base
- **Sinonasal malignancies:** squamous carcinoma, adenocarcinoma (woodworkers), adenoid cystic carcinoma, *olfactory neuroblastoma*, *sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma*, neuroendocrine carcinoma, mucosal melanoma.
- **Sarcomas and lymphoma.**
- **Benign lesions:** meningioma, inverted papilloma, juvenile angiofibroma, fibro-osseous lesions, mucoceles and encephaloceles.
## Sella and parasellar region
- **Pituitary adenoma:**
  - The commonest sellar tumour. Microadenomas are under 10 mm, macroadenomas larger.
  - Mass effect: *bitemporal hemianopia*, headache, hypopituitarism.
  - Hormone excess:
    - Prolactinoma: amenorrhoea, galactorrhoea, impotence. Treated first with *cabergoline or bromocriptine*.
    - Acromegaly.
    - Cushing disease.
  - Endocrine work-up: prolactin, IGF-1, cortisol and ACTH, thyroid, gonadotropins, sodium. Visual fields.
- **Rathke cleft cyst:** usually incidental and observed.
- **Craniopharyngioma:** two peaks, in children and in late middle age. Calcified and cystic. Hard to remove completely.
- **Meningioma:** of the tuberculum, planum and olfactory groove. Has a "dural tail".
- **Empty sella:** associated with idiopathic intracranial hypertension and spontaneous CSF leaks.
## Clivus and petrous apex
- **Chordoma:**
  - From notochord remnants, at the spheno-occipital synchondrosis, in the midline.
  - Bright on T2, with bone destruction.
  - Causes diplopia (VI palsy) and headache.
  - Treatment: maximal resection then high-dose (often proton) radiotherapy. Recurrence is common.
- **Chondrosarcoma:** at the petroclival synchondrosis, *paramedian*. Has a better prognosis than chordoma.
- **Petrous apex lesions:** cholesterol granuloma (bright on T1 and T2), cholesteatoma, mucocele, petrous apicitis (*Gradenigo syndrome*: otorrhoea, VI palsy and retro-orbital pain), and asymmetric pneumatisation or fluid that only *looks* like a lesion.

## Lateral skull base and posterior fossa
- **Paragangliomas:** glomus jugulare, tympanicum and vagale.
- **Vestibular schwannoma and other cerebellopontine angle lesions.**
- **Temporal bone carcinoma.**
- **Facial nerve and lower cranial nerve schwannomas.**
- **Meningiomas.**
- **Endolymphatic sac tumours** (von Hippel–Lindau syndrome).

## Infratemporal fossa
- **Primary tumours:** sarcomas, neurogenic tumours, and salivary tumours.
- **Spread from neighbours:** nasopharyngeal carcinoma, maxillary sinus cancer, juvenile angiofibroma, parotid cancer.
- **Clinical features:**
  - *Trismus*, from pterygoid invasion.
  - *Numbness of V3* (lower lip and chin).
  - Serous otitis media from eustachian tube invasion.
  - A mass deep to the ramus.

# Assessment | Nerves, images, tissue.
## Clinical picture
- **Nose:** unilateral obstruction, bleeding, anosmia.
- **Eye:** proptosis, diplopia, visual loss.
- **Face:** numbness (V2, V3) and pain, which suggest *perineural spread*.
- **Jaw:** trismus.
- **Ear:**
  - Unilateral conductive hearing loss from a *middle ear effusion*. In an adult, examine and biopsy the nasopharynx.
  - Pulsatile tinnitus.
- **Throat:** hoarseness, dysphagia and aspiration (IX to XI). Tongue weakness (XII).
- **CSF rhinorrhoea** and headache.
- **Examination:** a complete cranial nerve examination, nasal and nasopharyngeal endoscopy, otomicroscopy, the larynx and neck.
!pearl Jugular foramen syndromes
- **Vernet syndrome:** IX, X and XI together, from jugular foramen lesions (paragangliomas, schwannomas, metastases).
- **Collet–Sicard syndrome:** adds XII.
- **Villaret syndrome:** adds Horner syndrome, from the retroparotid space.
!!
## Imaging and tissue
- **CT:** bone detail, showing erosion, remodelling and foramen widening.
- **MRI with gadolinium and fat suppression:** soft tissue, dura, brain, orbit and perineural spread. CT and MRI are complementary, and both are needed.
- **Signs of invasion:**
  - *Dural invasion*: dural thickening of more than 5 mm, or nodular thickening.
  - *Periorbital invasion*: enhancement or enlargement of the extraocular muscles, stranding of the orbital fat, and a nodular interface.
- **Angiography:** for vascular tumours, embolisation, and *balloon test occlusion* of the internal carotid when it may have to be sacrificed.
- **PET-CT:** for staging and recurrence.
- **Tissue:** most lesions need it, often by *endoscopic biopsy*. Not all: paragangliomas, schwannomas, typical meningiomas and juvenile angiofibromas can be diagnosed on imaging.
## Unresectability
- **Generally unresectable:**
  - Invasion of the brainstem or spinal cord.
  - Both internal carotids.
  - Both cavernous sinuses.
  - Extensive brain parenchyma.
- **Relative contraindications:**
  - The optic chiasm.
  - Distant metastases.
  - Poor general health.
- **Non-surgical tumours:** lymphoma and other chemo- or radio-sensitive tumours are not resected.

# Approaches | Endoscopic and open.
## Endoscopic endonasal surgery
Through both nostrils (the "*binostril, four-handed*" technique), using a wide sphenoidotomy, removal of the posterior septum and, as needed, ethmoidectomy, medial maxillectomy, transpterygoid access or a Draf III. It reaches:
- *Transsphenoidal*: the sella, the standard route for pituitary surgery.
- *Transplanum and transtuberculum*: the suprasellar region.
- *Transcribriform*: the anterior skull base from the frontal sinus to the planum, between the orbits.
- *Transclival*: the clivus down to the foramen magnum and odontoid.
- *Transpterygoid*: the pterygopalatine and infratemporal fossae, Meckel cave and the petrous apex.

Advantages: no facial or scalp incisions, no brain retraction, magnified and angled views, and faster recovery. For selected sinonasal cancers, survival is comparable to open craniofacial resection.

Limits:
- Lateral extension beyond the mid-orbit.
- Involvement of the orbit, skin or the anterior or lateral wall of the frontal or maxillary sinus.
- Dural or brain invasion lateral to the orbit.
- Encasement of the carotid.

## Reconstruction
- **Small defects:** free grafts (fascia, fat, mucosa) in multiple layers, with an *inlay* graft under the dura and an *onlay* graft over the bone.
- **Large defects:** vascularised flaps.
  - The *nasoseptal (Hadad–Bassagasteguy) flap*, pedicled on the posterior septal artery from the sphenopalatine artery. It has reduced postoperative leak rates from over 20% to about 5%. A "rescue flap" incision preserves its pedicle in case it is needed.
  - Inferior or middle turbinate flaps.
  - The *pericranial flap*, open or endoscopically harvested.
  - The temporoparietal fascia flap.
  - Free flaps for open defects.
- **Supporting measures:** lumbar drains for selected high-flow leaks. Avoid nose blowing, straining and positive-pressure ventilation by mask.

## Open approaches
- **Craniofacial resection:**
  - A transfacial route (lateral rhinotomy or midface degloving) is combined with a bifrontal craniotomy, removing the cribriform plate, ethmoids and involved dura en bloc.
  - Reconstruction with a *pericranial flap* on the supraorbital and supratrochlear vessels.
  - Orbital exenteration if the periorbital fat, extraocular muscles or orbital apex are invaded.
- **Infratemporal fossa approaches (Fisch):**
  - *Type A*: the jugular foramen and infralabyrinthine region, with anterior rerouting of the facial nerve. Used for glomus jugulare.
  - *Type B*: the clivus and petrous apex.
  - *Type C*: the parasellar region, nasopharynx and pterygopalatine fossa.
  - These approaches close the ear canal and remove the middle ear.
- **Lateral (preauricular) and middle fossa approaches:** the subtemporal–infratemporal approach, removing the zygoma and condyle.
- **Far lateral approach:** to the foramen magnum and lower clivus.
- **Approaches to the temporal bone, internal auditory canal and cerebellopontine angle:** translabyrinthine, retrosigmoid, middle fossa, transotic and transcochlear. They are described with vestibular schwannoma and temporal bone tumours.
- **Transfacial access routes:** maxillary swing, transpalatal and midface degloving to the nasopharynx.

## Complications
- **CSF leak:** the commonest.
- **Meningitis.**
- **Tension pneumocephalus:** sudden confusion and a falling conscious level. A "*Mount Fuji sign*" on CT.
- **Carotid injury:**
  - Pack with pressure, then a crushed muscle patch.
  - Then urgent angiography (pseudoaneurysm).
- **Stroke and cranial nerve deficits.**
- **Visual loss.**
- **Hypopituitarism and diabetes insipidus** after sellar surgery. Monitor sodium and urine output.
- **Nasal crusting, synechiae and anosmia.**
- **Trismus.**
- **Wound and flap failure.**

Overall five-year survival for skull base malignancy is about 50%. Clear margins are the strongest predictor of survival, and postoperative radiotherapy is given for high-risk features.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Contents of the cavernous sinus;** why VI fails first; the skull base foramina.
- **The pterygopalatine fossa:** its communications and what spreads through it.
- **The parapharyngeal space:**
  - Prestyloid versus poststyloid.
  - Medial displacement of the tonsil.
  - Never biopsy through the mouth.
  - The transcervical approach.
- **Adult unilateral middle ear effusion:** examine the nasopharynx.
- **Chordoma versus chondrosarcoma;** the MRI table for petrous apex and cerebellopontine angle lesions.
- **Endoscopic endonasal approaches,** their limits, and the nasoseptal flap and its pedicle.
- **Craniofacial resection;** the Fisch infratemporal fossa approaches.
- **Complications:** CSF leak, pneumocephalus, carotid injury.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Taking a transoral biopsy of a parapharyngeal mass.
- Treating a prolactinoma surgically before trying a dopamine agonist.
- Missing a VI palsy or facial numbness as signs of skull base spread.
- Calling an asymmetric petrous apex "a lesion" without MRI.
!!
