@stand Laryngeal cancer is a smoker's disease with an unusual mercy: when it starts on the vocal fold it makes the voice hoarse while it is still small and has almost no lymphatics to spread through, so it is often found early and cured. Above or below the folds it is silent, spreads to the neck, and is found late. The rule is simple: hoarseness for more than three weeks in an adult, above all a smoker, means the larynx must be seen. Treatment then balances cure against voice, swallowing and the stoma.

# Overview | Who and why.
## Epidemiology
The second commonest cancer of the upper aerodigestive tract and about 1 to 5% of all malignancies; men are affected four to eight times as often as women, mostly in the sixth and seventh decades. In India it accounts for about 4% of male cancers. Glottic cancer is the commonest in most series (about 60%), supraglottic about 35 to 40%, and subglottic only 1 to 2%; supraglottic cancer predominates in some populations.
## Causes
- **Tobacco:** the dominant cause; current smokers carry a ten- to twentyfold risk, which falls substantially after ten years of stopping.
- **Alcohol:** especially for supraglottic cancer; with tobacco the effects multiply (about fifteenfold).
- **Others:** previous radiation to the neck, papillomatosis (HPV 6 and 11, rarely malignant), occupational exposures (asbestos, nickel, mustard gas, wood dust, paints, diesel fumes), reflux, and genetic susceptibility.
## Premalignant lesions
Hyperplasia, keratosis, mild, moderate and severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ form a spectrum. Mild to moderate dysplasia progresses in about 7 to 11%, severe dysplasia in 18 to 30%; severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ behave alike. Excisional biopsy (microflap or laser) is the diagnosis and usually the treatment; radiotherapy for multifocal disease or the frail; lifelong follow-up.

# Pathology and regions | Where it starts determines how it behaves.
## Histology
Squamous cell carcinoma in 85 to 95%; glottic tumours tend to be well differentiated, supraglottic ones less so. Others: *verrucous carcinoma* (1 to 2%; exophytic, well-differentiated, with pushing margins; does not metastasise; treated by excision rather than radiation), spindle cell and basaloid carcinoma, neuroendocrine tumours, salivary carcinomas, chondrosarcoma (usually the cricoid) and lymphoma.
## Regions
!fig d62-1 | The regions of the larynx (AJCC): the glottis extends from the ventricle to 1 cm below the free edge of the fold; the subglottis from there to the lower border of the cricoid.
- **Supraglottis:** suprahyoid and infrahyoid epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds, arytenoids, false folds and ventricles. Embryologically from the third and fourth arches without midline fusion, with rich, bilateral lymphatics.
- **Glottis:** the true folds with the anterior and posterior commissures, extending 1 cm below the free edge. From the sixth arch, with scanty lymphatics.
- **Subglottis:** to the lower border of the cricoid.
!anat s240_17 s245_26 | Supraglottis, glottis and subglottis, and the paraglottic space.
## Barriers and pathways
The conus elasticus and the quadrangular membrane resist spread; the *pre-epiglottic space* (reached through pits in the epiglottis) and the *paraglottic space* (lateral to the membranes) carry tumour silently across the ventricle (*transglottic* cancer) and up to the thyroid cartilage. At the anterior commissure, *Broyles' ligament* inserts into thyroid cartilage without perichondrium, a route into the cartilage.

# Supraglottic cancer | Silent and node-seeking.
## Sites
Epiglottis, false folds and aryepiglottic folds, in that order. Suprahyoid epiglottic tumours tend to be exophytic, infrahyoid ones ulcerative.
!fig d62-2 d62-3 | First, a supraglottic cancer of the epiglottis and right aryepiglottic fold. Second, a supraglottic cancer also invading the right piriform fossa (arrow).
## Spread
To the vallecula, base of tongue, piriform fossa and pre-epiglottic space, and through the thyroid cartilage. Nodes (levels II to IV) appear early: 25 to 50% at presentation, often bilateral, with occult nodes in 20 to 40% of N0 necks.
## Symptoms
Often silent for a long time: discomfort or pain in the throat, a lump feeling, painful swallowing, referred otalgia, a neck mass; hoarseness, dyspnoea, halitosis and weight loss come late.

# Glottic cancer | Loud and contained.
## Site
The free edge and upper surface of the anterior and middle thirds of the fold.
!fig d62-4 d62-5 | First, cancer of the larynx: (A) supraglottic, (B) glottic, (C) subglottic. Second, a cancer involving the supraglottic, glottic and subglottic areas on the left, on rigid endoscopy.
## Spread
Forwards to the anterior commissure and opposite fold; backwards to the vocal process and arytenoid; upwards to the ventricle and false fold; downwards to the subglottis; deeply into the thyroarytenoid muscle and paraglottic space. *Impaired mobility* means deep invasion of muscle, and *fixation* means invasion of the paraglottic space, cricoarytenoid joint or nerve: a bad prognostic sign. Because the folds have almost no lymphatics, node metastases are rare (under 5% in T1 and T2) until the tumour leaves the membranous fold.
## Symptoms
*Hoarseness* early, because even a tiny lesion alters vibration; stridor when the tumour enlarges or fixes the fold.

# Subglottic cancer | Rare and late.
It starts on one side, spreads around the front and down into the trachea, through the cricothyroid membrane into the thyroid gland and strap muscles; nodes go to the prelaryngeal (Delphian), pretracheal, paratracheal (level VI), lower jugular and superior mediastinal groups. Stridor and dyspnoea are often the first symptoms; hoarseness is late, meaning spread to the undersurface of the fold, the thyroarytenoid or the recurrent nerve. It is usually advanced and poorly differentiated, and prone to stomal recurrence.

# Diagnosis and staging | Seeing and mapping.
## History and examination
Any patient of cancer age with hoarseness for more than two to three weeks needs laryngoscopy. Also ask about sore throat, otalgia, dysphagia, aspiration, dyspnoea, haemoptysis, weight loss, smoking and drinking, and lung function.
- **Laryngoscopy:** mirror, flexible or rigid video laryngoscopy shows the appearance (a nodule, ulcer, thickening or granular mass), mobility of each fold, and extent to the vallecula, base of tongue and piriform fossae. Narrow-band imaging helps separate malignant from benign lesions; office biopsy is possible with flexible scopes. *Stroboscopy* shows a stiff, non-vibrating segment in invasive cancer.
- **Neck:** extralaryngeal spread (a midline swelling over the cricothyroid membrane, tender cartilage from perichondritis, thyroid and strap muscle involvement, widening of the thyroid cartilage) and nodes (number, size, side, fixity).
!fig d62-6 | Flexible fibreoptic laryngoscopy in the clinic.
## Imaging
- **CT** of the neck with contrast: pre-epiglottic and paraglottic space invasion, cartilage destruction, extralaryngeal spread and nodes.
- **MRI:** more sensitive for cartilage invasion and for recurrence after radiotherapy.
- **CT of the chest:** lung metastases and second primaries (a solitary lung nodule is more likely a second primary).
- **PET-CT** for advanced disease and after chemoradiation (at 10 to 12 weeks) to detect residual disease.
## Endoscopy under anaesthesia
Direct laryngoscopy shows the *hidden areas* (infrahyoid epiglottis, anterior commissure, ventricle, subglottis), maps the extent and allows biopsy; microlaryngoscopy for small fold lesions; panendoscopy for second primaries. Toluidine blue staining once guided biopsy of keratotic patches.

# Treatment | Cure, then function.
## Principles
Early cancers (stage I and II) are treated by a single modality, radiotherapy or surgery, with similar control; the choice turns on voice, swallowing, access, fitness and preference. Advanced cancers (stage III and IV) need combined treatment, with organ preservation where it is safe and total laryngectomy where it is not.
## Radiotherapy
For early tumours with mobile folds and no cartilage invasion or nodes: about 60 to 70 Gy over six weeks. T1 glottic cancer is controlled in about 90% or more with good voice; T2 in about 85% with normal mobility but much less (about 63 to 70%) when mobility is impaired. It spares the voice and avoids surgery but causes mucositis, oedema, dryness and occasional chondronecrosis, makes recurrence harder to detect, and cannot be repeated. Poor results with fixed folds, subglottic extension, cartilage invasion and nodes.
## Transoral surgery
*Transoral laser microsurgery* (CO2 laser under the microscope), and more recently transoral robotic surgery, has largely replaced open partial laryngectomy for T1, T2 and selected T3 tumours: cordectomy for glottic cancer, supraglottic resection for supraglottic cancer. It is cheaper and quicker, avoids tracheostomy, keeps radiotherapy in reserve, and gives similar control to radiotherapy, but the voice may be poorer after wide cordectomy. It needs good exposure, and is contraindicated by fold fixation, interarytenoid involvement and poor lung function.
## Open conservation surgery
Now used mainly in selected patients and after radiotherapy failure:
- **Cordectomy through a laryngofissure.**
- **Vertical partial (frontolateral, hemi-) laryngectomy:** one fold with the anterior commissure and adjacent thyroid cartilage.
- **Supraglottic (horizontal partial) laryngectomy:** removes the epiglottis, pre-epiglottic space, false folds and aryepiglottic folds above the ventricles; for T1 and T2 supraglottic cancer with mobile folds, clear of the anterior commissure, with good lung function, because every patient aspirates at first and must relearn swallowing.
- **Supracricoid partial laryngectomy:** removes both folds, the paraglottic spaces and the thyroid cartilage, rebuilt by cricohyoidopexy or cricohyoidoepiglottopexy; for selected T2 and T3 tumours with at least one mobile arytenoid.
## Total laryngectomy
Removal of the whole larynx with the hyoid, pre-epiglottic space and upper trachea; the pharynx is closed and the trachea brought out as a permanent end stoma. Dhingra's indications:
- T3 and T4 tumours (fold fixed, cartilage invaded, extralaryngeal spread).
- Invasion of the thyroid or cricoid cartilage; both arytenoids; posterior commissure.
- Transglottic tumours with fold fixation.
- Failure after radiotherapy or conservation surgery.
- A non-functioning larynx with intractable aspiration.
Combined with neck dissection and postoperative radiotherapy as indicated; contraindicated by distant metastases.
!fig d62-7 | Treatment of T2N0 glottic cancer: radiotherapy when the fold is mobile and the commissure and arytenoid are spared; conservation laryngectomy when mobility is impaired or the commissure or arytenoid is involved; total laryngectomy for failure.
## Organ preservation
The *VA Larynx trial* (1991) showed that induction chemotherapy (cisplatin and fluorouracil) followed by radiotherapy in responders gave the same survival as laryngectomy, with two-thirds keeping their larynx. *RTOG 91-11* (2003) showed that concurrent cisplatin with radiotherapy gave the best laryngeal preservation and locoregional control, although at ten years overall survival did not differ. Concurrent chemoradiation is now standard for most T3 and node-positive T2 cancers, with salvage laryngectomy for failure; cetuximab or carboplatin for those unfit for cisplatin. T4a tumours through cartilage, a non-functioning larynx and severe airway obstruction are better treated by laryngectomy and adjuvant therapy, which gives better control.

## By site
- **Carcinoma in situ:** transoral laser excision (or stripping) with histology; radiotherapy if multifocal or invasive.
- **Glottic T1:** radiotherapy or laser cordectomy; with anterior commissure involvement, radiotherapy or a frontolateral laryngectomy.
- **Glottic T2:** radiotherapy if the fold is mobile and the commissure and arytenoid are free; otherwise conservation surgery; total laryngectomy if it recurs. The N0 neck is not treated (occult nodes under 5 to 25%).
- **Glottic T3 and T4:** chemoradiation for selected T3; total laryngectomy with neck dissection and postoperative radiotherapy for T4a.
- **Supraglottic T1 and T2:** radiotherapy, laser resection or supraglottic laryngectomy, always with *bilateral elective treatment of levels II to IV*.
- **Subglottic:** radiotherapy for early lesions; total laryngectomy with hemithyroidectomy or total thyroidectomy and level VI and paratracheal dissection, and radiotherapy including the superior mediastinum, for advanced lesions.
## The neck
Clinically positive nodes need neck dissection or radiotherapy. After chemoradiation, a complete response on PET-CT at about twelve weeks allows surveillance instead of planned neck dissection. Postoperative chemoradiation is given for positive margins or extranodal extension.

## Other rehabilitation
- **Smell:** lost because air no longer passes through the nose; the *polite yawning* manoeuvre (lowering the jaw and tongue with the lips closed) draws air through the nose.
- **Stoma care:** humidifying heat-moisture exchangers, protection from water.
- **Swallowing** and **thyroid function** (hypothyroidism in a fifth to two-thirds after treatment).
## Complications
Pharyngocutaneous fistula (more after radiotherapy; reduced by a pectoralis major or free flap), stomal stenosis and stomal recurrence, pharyngeal stricture, chondronecrosis after radiotherapy, and a non-functioning larynx after chemoradiation.
## Follow-up and recurrence
Every one to three months in the first year, less often thereafter, lifelong, looking for recurrence and second primaries. Recurrence shows as new oedema, pain, worse voice or reduced mobility; PET-CT and biopsy confirm it; about half are salvageable, usually by total laryngectomy (selected small recurrences by conservation surgery).
## Prognosis
Five-year survival is about 90% for T1 glottic cancer, falling with stage; supraglottic and subglottic cancers do worse than glottic. Nodes, extranodal extension, cartilage invasion and subglottic extension are adverse.

## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Regions and subsites,** and why glottic cancer rarely spreads to nodes.
- **Pre-epiglottic and paraglottic spaces,** transglottic spread, Broyles' ligament.
- **Symptoms** by site; the hoarseness rule.
- **T staging** and the significance of fold mobility.
- **Treatment:** radiotherapy versus laser for early cancer; organ preservation (VA and RTOG 91-11); indications for total laryngectomy.
- **Voice rehabilitation** after laryngectomy.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Treating persistent hoarseness in a smoker with antibiotics and voice rest.
- Missing the anterior commissure, ventricle or subglottis without direct laryngoscopy.
- Forgetting bilateral neck treatment in supraglottic cancer.
- Offering organ preservation to a T4a tumour through cartilage.
!!
