@stand Up to half of people with HIV have head and neck problems, and an ENT finding is often the first clue to the diagnosis. Examples are bilateral parotid cysts, oral hairy leukoplakia, persistent thrush, necrotising gingivitis, generalised nodes, and a purple lesion on the palate. Effective antiretroviral therapy has made the dramatic opportunistic diseases less common, but the ENT surgeon still needs to recognise them, test for HIV, and protect the theatre team with universal precautions and post-exposure prophylaxis.

# The virus and the disease | Biology, course, diagnosis.
## HIV
HIV is an enveloped, single-stranded RNA retrovirus of the lentivirus genus. **HIV-1** causes most infections worldwide and is the more pathogenic; **HIV-2** is mainly West African and less virulent.

The virion is a lipid bilayer carrying the envelope glycoproteins *gp120*, which binds CD4, and *gp41*, which mediates fusion. Inside lie a matrix (p17), a conical capsid (*p24*) and two copies of RNA, together with the three enzymes *reverse transcriptase*, *integrase* and *protease*. Each enzyme is a drug target.
!fig d75-1 | Structure of the HIV-1 virion.
## Life cycle
1. gp120 binds CD4 and a co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4) on helper T cells, macrophages and dendritic cells.
2. gp41 fuses the envelope with the cell membrane.
3. Reverse transcriptase copies the RNA into DNA.
4. Integrase inserts it into the host genome as a *provirus*.
5. The provirus directs new viral RNA and polyproteins, which protease cleaves into functional proteins.
6. New virions bud off.

The progressive loss of *CD4 helper T cells* is what destroys cell-mediated immunity, and it explains the pattern of disease that follows.
## Transmission
HIV spreads by sexual contact, through blood and blood products, by shared needles, and from mother to child during pregnancy, delivery or breastfeeding. The infectious fluids are blood, semen, vaginal fluid and breast milk, together with the deep body fluids: CSF and pleural, peritoneal, pericardial and amniotic fluid. Saliva, tears, sweat, urine and faeces carry a negligible risk unless they are visibly bloody.
## Course
**Primary (acute) infection** appears two to four weeks after exposure as a glandular-fever-like illness, with fever, pharyngitis, cervical nodes, a rash, mouth and genital ulcers, and sometimes meningoencephalitis. Viral load is very high at this stage and the patient is highly infectious.

A **clinical latency** of about ten years on average follows. The patient is well, sometimes with *persistent generalised lymphadenopathy*, but the virus keeps replicating in lymphoid tissue, including the tonsils and adenoids, while CD4 counts fall.

**AIDS** is defined by a CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ (normal 500 to 1500) or by an AIDS-defining illness. These include Kaposi sarcoma, *Pneumocystis* pneumonia, oesophageal candidiasis, extrapulmonary tuberculosis, cryptococcosis, cerebral toxoplasmosis and certain lymphomas.
## Diagnosis
The first-line test is a **fourth-generation combined antigen and antibody immunoassay**, which detects p24 antigen and antibodies and becomes positive at about two to six weeks. A positive result is confirmed with a second, differentiating assay; the Western blot has largely been replaced. **HIV RNA** by PCR (the viral load) diagnoses acute infection and monitors treatment, and the **CD4 count** measures immune damage and predicts opportunistic disease. Patients are staged clinically by the WHO stages 1 to 4, or by the CDC stages based on the CD4 count.
## Treatment
**Antiretroviral therapy (ART)**, formerly called HAART, combines usually three drugs, often in a single daily tablet. It is started *at diagnosis, whatever the CD4 count*, because immediate treatment halves serious illness and death (the START and TEMPRANO trials). A person with an undetectable viral load does not transmit HIV sexually: *undetectable equals untransmittable*.

For prevention, *pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)* with tenofovir and emtricitabine protects people at high risk, and *post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)* is described below.
!table Classes of antiretroviral drugs
Class | Action | Examples
Nucleoside and nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTI) | Chain termination of viral DNA | Tenofovir, emtricitabine, lamivudine, abacavir, zidovudine
Non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTI) | Bind and block reverse transcriptase | Efavirenz, nevirapine, rilpivirine
Integrase inhibitors | Block integration into the host genome | Dolutegravir, bictegravir, raltegravir
Protease inhibitors | Block cleavage of polyproteins | Darunavir, atazanavir (with ritonavir boosting)
Entry and fusion inhibitors | Block CCR5 or gp41 | Maraviroc, enfuvirtide

# ENT manifestations | Head to neck.
## Categories of disease
Four kinds of disease account for almost everything the ENT surgeon sees. **Infections**, both opportunistic and ordinary, become more severe and more often complicated as the CD4 count falls; they may be bacterial, viral, fungal, mycobacterial or protozoal. **Malignancies** are either *AIDS-defining*, namely Kaposi sarcoma and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, or *non-AIDS-defining*, such as Hodgkin lymphoma, skin cancers and HPV-related oropharyngeal cancer. **Neurological disease** includes HIV encephalopathy, neuropathies and cranial nerve palsies, especially of the facial nerve. Finally there are **inflammatory and lymphoproliferative conditions**, chiefly lymphoepithelial cysts and diffuse infiltrative lymphocytosis.
## Ear
In the **external ear**, seborrhoeic dermatitis and otitis externa are common, including *necrotising (malignant) otitis externa* from *Pseudomonas* or *Aspergillus*, and Kaposi sarcoma may involve the pinna and canal.

In the **middle ear**, serous otitis media follows adenoid hyperplasia or a nasopharyngeal mass. A unilateral effusion in an adult with HIV therefore needs a nasopharyngeal biopsy to exclude lymphoma or Kaposi sarcoma. Recurrent and chronic otitis media are frequent, especially in children, and *Pneumocystis* or mycobacterial otitis occurs.

The **inner ear and nerves** suffer sensorineural hearing loss from HIV itself, from opportunistic infections (CMV, syphilis, cryptococcal meningitis) and from ototoxic drugs. Herpes zoster oticus (*Ramsay Hunt syndrome*) and facial palsy are commoner than in the general population.
## Nose and sinuses
Rhinosinusitis is common. While the CD4 count is above 200 the usual organisms are found, but at low counts infections become atypical and complicated, with *Pseudomonas*, CMV and *invasive fungal sinusitis* from *Aspergillus* or mucor. Herpes simplex can ulcerate the nose, and the tumours to remember are Kaposi sarcoma and B-cell, Burkitt and plasmablastic lymphoma.
## Mouth and pharynx
**Oral candidiasis** is the commonest oral lesion. It takes four forms: *pseudomembranous* thrush, with white plaques that wipe off; *erythematous* (atrophic) candidiasis; *angular cheilitis*; and *hyperplastic* candidiasis, whose plaques do not wipe off. Topical nystatin or clotrimazole suffices when the CD4 count is above 200; systemic fluconazole is used when it is lower or when odynophagia suggests oesophageal disease. Resistance develops with repeated courses.

**Oral hairy leukoplakia** produces white, vertically corrugated, hair-like plaques on the *lateral border of the tongue* that cannot be wiped off. It is caused by *EBV*, is asymptomatic and is not premalignant, but it is a strong marker of HIV that predicts progression to AIDS and should prompt testing. Biopsy it if in doubt, because the differential includes leukoplakia, candida, lichen planus and carcinoma.

**Periodontal disease** ranges from *linear gingival erythema* to *necrotising ulcerative gingivitis and periodontitis*, which is painful, foul and bleeding, with loss of the interdental papillae and bone. It is treated with chlorhexidine, debridement, metronidazole and dental care.

**Ulcers** need particular care. *Giant aphthous ulcers* are large, deep and painful; biopsy them to exclude herpes, CMV, tuberculosis, histoplasmosis and lymphoma before giving topical or intralesional steroids or thalidomide. Chronic herpes simplex ulcers lasting more than a month are AIDS-defining, as is oral histoplasmosis with its granulomatous ulcers.

In adults, **adenotonsillar hypertrophy** causes nasal obstruction and middle ear effusion and must be biopsied to exclude lymphoma. Kaposi sarcoma and lymphoma also arise on the palate, gingiva, tongue and tonsil.
## Larynx
The larynx may be infected by candida, herpes, CMV or tuberculosis, and epiglottitis occurs. Kaposi sarcoma and lymphoma here can obstruct the airway.
## Salivary glands
**Benign lymphoepithelial cysts (BLEC)** make up about three-quarters of HIV parotid lesions. They present as painless, soft, often *bilateral* parotid swellings (up to 80% are bilateral on imaging) with cervical lymphadenopathy, and they may be the presenting sign of HIV, especially in children. They are thought to arise from intraparotid nodes. Ultrasound or CT shows multiple thin-walled cysts, and FNA confirms the diagnosis. The cysts regress on ART; serial aspiration, *doxycycline or alcohol sclerotherapy* and low-dose radiotherapy are alternatives, and parotidectomy is reserved for disfiguring lesions or suspected malignancy.

**Diffuse infiltrative lymphocytosis syndrome (DILS)** resembles Sjögren's syndrome, with bilateral parotid enlargement and sicca symptoms, but the infiltrate is of CD8 lymphocytes, Sjögren antibodies are negative, and lymphocytic interstitial pneumonitis often coexists. It is treated with ART and symptomatic measures. Xerostomia (often drug-related), parotitis and lymphoma complete the salivary picture.
## Neck
Most patients have **cervical lymphadenopathy**, and persistent generalised lymphadenopathy is a reactive follicular hyperplasia. Nodes that are asymmetric, rapidly growing, painful or matted, or that come with B symptoms, need FNA or excision. The causes then include *tuberculosis* (the commonest serious cause in endemic areas), atypical mycobacteria, histoplasmosis, toxoplasmosis, bacillary angiomatosis, Kaposi sarcoma and *lymphoma*. Deep neck abscesses are also more frequent.

# HIV-associated tumours | Kaposi, lymphoma and others.
## Kaposi sarcoma
Kaposi sarcoma is an angioproliferative tumour caused by *human herpesvirus 8* (KSHV). Four variants are described: the classic form in elderly men of Mediterranean or Eastern European origin, the endemic African form, the transplant-related form, and the AIDS-associated form, which is the commonest and most aggressive.

The head and neck are involved in most AIDS cases: the skin of the face, nose and ears; the oral cavity, especially the *hard palate*, gingiva and tongue; and the pharynx, larynx and nodes. The lesions are violaceous macules, plaques and nodules that do not blanch, and oral lesions may ulcerate, bleed, loosen teeth and obstruct.

Biopsy shows spindle cells, slit-like vascular channels, extravasated red cells and haemosiderin; the differential includes haemangioma, pyogenic granuloma and bacillary angiomatosis. Stage the disease by looking for visceral spread to the gut and lungs and by checking the CD4 count and viral load.

The foundation of treatment is *ART*, under which many lesions regress. Local lesions respond to radiotherapy, to which they are very sensitive, or to intralesional vinblastine, cryotherapy or laser. Extensive disease is treated with liposomal doxorubicin or paclitaxel, and surgery is used only to palliate or to secure the airway.
## Lymphoma
**Non-Hodgkin lymphoma** is the second commonest malignancy in HIV. It is mostly of aggressive B-cell types: diffuse large B-cell, Burkitt and *plasmablastic*, the last often in the oral cavity. It is frequently EBV-driven, extranodal and advanced at presentation with B symptoms. In the head and neck it affects Waldeyer's ring, the sinonasal tract, oral cavity, orbit, salivary glands and nodes, and *primary CNS lymphoma* occurs when the CD4 count is very low. Diagnosis needs an excision or core biopsy, because FNA is usually not enough to classify it. Treatment is chemotherapy (R-CHOP, R-EPOCH) together with ART.

**Hodgkin lymphoma** is the commonest non-AIDS-defining cancer in HIV. It presents at a more advanced stage, usually as the mixed cellularity or lymphocyte-depleted types.
## Other cancers
Basal and squamous cell carcinomas and melanoma of the skin are commoner and more aggressive, and HPV-related oropharyngeal, anal and cervical carcinomas are more frequent. The practical rule is simple: keep a low threshold to biopsy any lesion that does not heal.

# Protecting the team | Precautions and prophylaxis.
## Risk of occupational infection
After a percutaneous injury from an HIV-positive source the risk of transmission is about 0.3%, or 1 in 300; after mucous membrane exposure it is about 0.09%. The risk is higher with a deep injury, a hollow-bore needle that has been in a vessel, visible blood on the device, and a source with a high viral load.
## Universal (standard) precautions
Every patient's blood and body fluids are treated as potentially infectious. Wash hands before and after patient contact, wear gloves, and *double-glove* in surgery. An impermeable gown, mask and eye protection are needed wherever splashes are likely, as in tracheostomy, endoscopy, drilling, laser and oral surgery.

Sharps are passed in a dish through a "hands-free" zone, needles are never recapped, and sharps go straight into rigid containers; blunt suture needles are used where possible. Soiled linen and specimens are handled as infectious, and staff with weeping dermatitis should avoid direct patient care.
## After an exposure
1. Wash the wound with soap and water and let it bleed. Do not scrub or squeeze. Irrigate mucous membranes and eyes with water.
2. Report the injury at once and assess the risk.
3. Test the source patient (with consent) for HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C.
4. Take baseline blood from the injured worker.
5. *Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)*: start as soon as possible, ideally within hours and no later than 72 hours. Take three drugs for 28 days, typically tenofovir with emtricitabine (or lamivudine) plus dolutegravir or raltegravir.
6. Follow-up HIV testing at about 6 weeks and 3 to 4 months. Protect sexual partners and avoid blood donation meanwhile.
!update PEP regimens
Older texts give two NRTIs (such as zidovudine and lamivudine) for low-risk exposures and add a third drug for high-risk ones. Current guidance uses a three-drug regimen built around an integrase inhibitor for every exposure that warrants PEP, because it is better tolerated and more often completed. Diagnosis has moved from ELISA and Western blot to fourth-generation antigen and antibody assays.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Treating a unilateral adult middle ear effusion with grommets without examining and biopsying the nasopharynx.
- Excising bilateral parotid cysts that would regress on ART.
- Relying on FNA alone to diagnose lymphoma.
- Delaying PEP beyond the first hours after a needlestick.
!!
