@stand Acute inflammation of the larynx ranges from the hoarse voice that follows a cold to the child sitting bolt upright, drooling and silent, whose swollen epiglottis may close the airway within the hour. The difference between croup and epiglottitis is one of the oldest lessons in paediatrics and still one of the most important: the barking child can usually be soothed; the drooling child must not be upset, examined or sent to radiology without someone who can secure the airway.

# Summary | Six conditions, one airway.
This topic covers the acute inflammatory and oedematous conditions of the larynx. Each has its own chapter below, laid out in the same order: epidemiology, etiology, pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnosis, management and complications.

# Acute laryngitis {dz} | The commonest.
## Etiology
- **Infective:** usually viral after a cold (parainfluenza, influenza, rhinovirus), followed by bacterial invasion (pneumococcus, *H. influenzae*, *Moraxella*, streptococci, staphylococci); with measles, chickenpox and whooping cough; candida in inhaled steroid users.
- **Non-infective:** vocal abuse (shouting, cheering), allergy, inhaled irritants, thermal and chemical burns, reflux, and intubation.
## Clinical features
### Symptoms
Abrupt hoarseness, sometimes complete loss of voice; pain in the throat after talking; a dry, irritating cough, worse at night; and the features of a cold.
### Laryngoscopy
- **Early:** the epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds, arytenoids and false folds are red and swollen while the true folds stay white, standing out against the redness.
- **Later:** the folds become red and swollen, with sticky mucus between them and subglottic swelling.
- **After vocal abuse:** submucosal haemorrhage in a fold.
!flag Breathlessness is not laryngitis
Breathlessness is *not* a feature of simple laryngitis and suggests another diagnosis.
!!
## Management
- **Voice rest:** the most important measure; forcing the voice delays recovery and risks haemorrhage and nodules.
- No smoking or alcohol; hydration; steam inhalation.
- Cough suppressants, mucolytics and analgesics.
- **Antibiotics** only for secondary bacterial infection with fever or purulent sputum.
- **Proton pump inhibitors** where reflux is suspected.
- **Steroids** are not routine in uncomplicated viral laryngitis; they are kept for laryngitis after thermal or chemical burns, and for professional voice users who must perform.
### Treatment: what and when
!table Acute laryngitis: treatment
Step | Treatment | Notes
Every patient | Voice rest (not whispering), hydration, humidification, stop smoking; paracetamol | Usually settles in 1 to 2 weeks
Not recommended | Routine antibiotics or steroids in uncomplicated viral laryngitis | Cochrane review: no meaningful benefit; steroids are kept for burns and urgent performance
Professional voice users with an urgent performance | Short oral steroid course on specialist advice | After laryngoscopy
Hoarseness over 3 weeks, or in a smoker | Laryngoscopy (urgent referral if over 45 with persistent hoarseness, NICE NG12) | 
!!
## Course and follow-up
Most settle in one to two weeks; hoarseness persisting beyond three weeks needs laryngoscopy.
## Subtypes and variants
### Acute membranous laryngitis
Pyogenic membrane on the larynx, often spreading from the pharynx; it must be distinguished from diphtheria ([[6]]) by swab and culture.

# Acute epiglottitis {dz} | Supraglottitis.
## Epidemiology
Classically in children of two to seven years; since Hib vaccination it is rare in children and now seen mostly in adults.
## Etiology
- **Children:** *H. influenzae* type b.
- **Adults:** streptococci, staphylococci and other organisms.
## Pathophysiology
Acute inflammation of the supraglottis (epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds and arytenoids), whose loose mucosa swells until it obstructs the airway; the swollen epiglottis acts like a ball valve.
## Clinical features
- **Abrupt onset,** rapid progression over hours, high fever and toxicity (septicaemia).
- **Children:** stridor and difficulty breathing are the presenting features; the child sits upright, leaning forward with the chin out (tripod position), drooling because swallowing hurts, with a muffled voice and usually no cough.
- **Adults:** severe sore throat and painful swallowing out of proportion to a normal-looking oropharynx, a muffled "hot-potato" voice, and tenderness over the hyoid.
!flag Do not upset the child
No tongue depressor, no throat examination, no cannula, no lying down, no unaccompanied trip to radiology. Keep the child with the parent, give oxygen without a struggle, and take them to theatre where an experienced anaesthetist and an ENT surgeon ready for tracheostomy can secure the airway under gaseous induction.
!!
## Diagnosis
- **Lateral soft tissue radiograph** of the neck (only if the patient is stable and accompanied): a swollen epiglottis, the *thumb sign*, with ballooned hypopharynx.
- **Flexible endoscopy** in adults is usually safe and shows a cherry-red, swollen epiglottis and arytenoids.
- **Blood cultures** (more often positive than surface swabs) and **epiglottic swabs**, after the airway is secure.
- **CT** for a suspected epiglottic abscess.
!imaging | Supraglottic swelling on imaging.
### Investigations: what and when
!table Acute epiglottitis: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Children | No investigations before the airway is secured in theatre | Calm child, senior anaesthetist and ENT
Adults (stable) | Flexible nasendoscopy | Swollen, cherry-red epiglottis and arytenoids; airway size
After the airway is safe | Blood cultures, epiglottic swab, full blood count, CRP | Organism (Hib, streptococci, *S. aureus*)
Abscess suspected | Contrast CT of the neck | Epiglottic abscess needing drainage
Stable patient, diagnosis uncertain | Lateral neck X-ray | Thumb sign
!!
## Management
!ward On call: acute epiglottitis
1. **Airway:** intubation (or tracheostomy if intubation fails) in theatre for children and for adults with stridor or rapid progression; selected adults presenting later without stridor can be watched closely in high-dependency care.
2. **Intravenous antibiotics** at once: a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone or cefotaxime), or ampicillin in sensitive strains.
3. **Steroids** to reduce oedema; humidified oxygen; intravenous fluids.
4. **Contacts:** rifampicin prophylaxis for household contacts of Hib cases, and vaccination.
!!
Intubation is usually needed for 24 to 72 hours, until the epiglottis has shrunk.
!update Current evidence
In adult epiglottitis, a meta-analysis of 30 studies (10,148 patients) found about 1 in 9 need an airway intervention since Hib vaccination, with an epiglottic abscess, stridor or diabetes predicting the need (Sideris et al., *Laryngoscope* 2020).
!!
### Drugs and doses
!table Acute epiglottitis: drugs and doses
Patient | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Adults | Ceftriaxone 2 g IV once daily (some units add metronidazole 500 mg every 8 hours) | Oral step-down (for example co-amoxiclav 625 mg three times a day) when improving; 7 to 10 days in total
Children | Ceftriaxone 50 to 80 mg/kg IV once daily (maximum 4 g) or cefotaxime | After the airway is secured
Penicillin anaphylaxis | Microbiology advice (for example a quinolone or vancomycin-based regimen) | 
Oedema | Dexamethasone (adults 8 to 10 mg IV, then 4 to 8 mg every 8 hours) | Evidence limited
Household contacts of Hib cases | Rifampicin once daily for 4 days: adults 600 mg; children 1 month to 11 years 20 mg/kg (maximum 600 mg); under 1 month 10 mg/kg | Plus vaccination of unvaccinated children under 10
!!
Doses from NHS Fife hospital ENT guidance, the BNF and BNF for Children, and UKHSA guidance on Haemophilus influenzae type b.
## Complications
- **Airway obstruction:** the ball-valve epiglottis may close the airway within the hour.
- **Septicaemia.**
- **Epiglottic abscess:** suspect it on CT; it predicts the need for an airway intervention in adults.

# Croup {dz} | Acute laryngotracheobronchitis.
## Epidemiology
The commonest cause of acute stridor in children, between six months and three years, more in boys and in autumn and winter.
## Etiology
Usually *parainfluenza* viruses (types 1 and 2), also RSV, influenza and adenovirus, with secondary bacterial infection.
## Pathophysiology
The loose areolar tissue of the subglottis swells inside the rigid cricoid ring; thick secretions and crusts add to the obstruction.
## Clinical features
### Typical course
A day or two of a cold, then hoarseness, a harsh *barking (seal-like) cough* and inspiratory or biphasic stridor, worse at night; fever is usually low or moderate and the child is not toxic.
### Signs of increasing obstruction
Suprasternal, intercostal and subcostal recession, tachypnoea and agitation.
!flag Late, ominous signs
Restlessness, a rising pulse, pallor and cyanosis, and finally exhaustion or drowsiness.
!!
## Diagnosis
An AP radiograph of the neck shows the *steeple sign*. How croup differs from epiglottitis is set out in [[8]].
### Investigations: what and when
!table Croup: investigations
Role | Test | When and what it shows
Diagnosis | Clinical: barking cough, stridor, coryzal prodrome; severity by Westley score | No tests needed in typical croup
Avoid | Throat examination with a tongue depressor in a distressed child; distressing procedures | Can precipitate obstruction
Atypical (toxic, drooling, no response) | Senior airway assessment; AP neck film only if stable | Epiglottitis, tracheitis, foreign body (steeple sign in croup)
Recurrent, under 6 months, or persistent | Microlaryngoscopy and bronchoscopy when well | Subglottic stenosis, haemangioma, cyst
!!
## Management
- **Mild:** a single dose of oral dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg (doses up to 0.6 mg/kg are used; the lower dose appears as effective), or nebulised budesonide if the child is vomiting or cannot take oral medicine, and observation; most go home.
- **Moderate to severe:** humidified oxygen, steroids and *nebulised adrenaline*, which works within minutes but wears off in about two hours, so observe for rebound; admit.
- **Severe or failing:** intubation in theatre with a tube smaller than usual for age; tracheostomy rarely now.
- **Antibiotics** only for suspected secondary bacterial infection.
!update Current evidence
A Cochrane review of 43 trials (4,565 children) found glucocorticoids improved croup within two hours, with the effect lasting at least 24 hours, halved return visits and readmissions (risk ratio 0.52) and shortened hospital stay by about 15 hours (Gates et al., 2018). Nebulised adrenaline improved croup scores at 30 minutes but not at two to six hours, so children are observed for rebound (Bjornson et al., 2013).
!!
### Drugs and doses
!table Croup: drugs and doses
Severity | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Every child (mild, moderate or severe) | Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg by mouth, single dose (up to 0.6 mg/kg in severe croup); or prednisolone 1 to 2 mg/kg | Repeat after 12 to 24 hours if needed
Vomiting or cannot take oral | Nebulised budesonide 2 mg | Single dose
Moderate to severe (stridor at rest, distress) | Nebulised adrenaline 1:1000, 400 micrograms/kg (0.4 ml/kg), maximum 5 mg (5 ml) | Works in minutes, lasts about 2 hours: observe for at least 2 to 3 hours for rebound; admit
Hypoxia | Humidified oxygen | 
Not helpful | Antibiotics (unless secondary infection), steam, cough medicines | 
!!
Doses from NICE CKS Croup, the BNF for Children and the Cochrane review of glucocorticoids for croup (Gates et al., 2018).
## Subtypes and variants
### Spasmodic croup
Sudden nocturnal attacks of barking cough and stridor in an otherwise well child without fever, settling by morning; often allergic or reflux-related.
### Recurrent croup
A child with repeated attacks, attacks under six months, or croup that will not settle needs microlaryngoscopy and bronchoscopy when well, to look for subglottic stenosis, a subglottic cyst or haemangioma, or a laryngeal cleft ([[t:lxcong]]).
## Complications
**Bacterial tracheitis** can follow viral croup ([[5]]).

# Bacterial tracheitis {dz} | Croup that is not croup.
## Etiology
A rare, serious bacterial infection (*Staphylococcus aureus*, streptococci, pneumococcus), often following viral croup.
## Clinical features
High fever, toxicity, stridor and a cough with thick secretions, failing to respond to croup treatment.
## Diagnosis
Bronchoscopy shows purulent tracheitis with oedema, sloughed mucosa and membranes.
## Management
- **Bronchoscopy** to remove the membranes, often repeatedly.
- **Intubation.**
- **Intravenous antibiotics.**
### Drugs and doses
!table Bacterial tracheitis: drugs and doses (children)
Step | Drug and dose | Course and notes
Empirical IV | Ceftriaxone 50 to 80 mg/kg once daily plus flucloxacillin 50 mg/kg every 6 hours (or clindamycin) | Covers *S. aureus*, streptococci, pneumococcus and *Moraxella*
MRSA risk | Add vancomycin (dose by level) | 
Duration | 10 to 14 days in total, with oral step-down | Guided by culture
Airway | Intubation; bronchoscopy to remove membranes | Often repeated
!!
Doses from the BNF for Children; regimens from published paediatric reviews.
## Complications
Pneumonia frequently follows.

# Laryngeal diphtheria {dz} | The membrane in the airway.
## Epidemiology
Unimmunised children under ten.
## Pathophysiology
Usually secondary to faucial diphtheria ([[t:tonsil]]). The membrane extends from the pharynx into the larynx and trachea; the exotoxin acts on the heart and nerves.
## Clinical features
- **Onset:** insidious, with low fever but marked toxaemia (tachycardia, thready pulse).
- **Airway:** hoarseness, croupy cough, inspiratory stridor and progressive obstruction.
- **Membrane:** adherent and grey-white on the tonsils, palate and pharynx; it bleeds when lifted and extends into the larynx and trachea.
- **Neck:** a bull neck.
## Diagnosis
Smears and cultures, but treatment starts on clinical suspicion.
## Management
!ward On call: laryngeal diphtheria
1. Treat on clinical suspicion; take smears and cultures.
2. Antitoxin, 20,000 to 100,000 units intravenously after a test dose, according to severity and duration (laryngeal disease sits at the severe end; the doses for faucial diphtheria are in [[t:tonsil]]).
3. Benzylpenicillin or erythromycin for 7 to 10 days.
4. Airway: intubation after laryngoscopy and removal of membrane, or tracheostomy.
5. Strict bed rest for two to four weeks because of myocarditis; cardiac monitoring.
6. Isolation, immunisation of contacts.
!!
## Complications
- **Airway obstruction** from the membrane.
- **Myocarditis** from the exotoxin: the reason for strict bed rest and cardiac monitoring.
- **Paralysis** from the exotoxin: palatal, pharyngeal, ocular and diaphragmatic.

# Laryngeal oedema {dz} | Many causes, one danger.
## Pathophysiology
Oedema collects where the mucosa is loose, in the supraglottis and subglottis, and rarely in the vocal folds.
## Etiology
- **Infection:** epiglottitis, croup, tuberculosis, syphilis; nearby quinsy, retropharyngeal abscess and Ludwig's angina ([[t:deepneck]]).
- **Trauma:** surgery of the tongue and floor of mouth, laryngeal trauma, foreign bodies, endoscopy (especially in children), intubation, burns, caustics and irritant gases.
- **Neoplasm:** ulcerating cancers of the larynx and laryngopharynx.
- **Allergy:** angioneurotic oedema, anaphylaxis; ACE inhibitor and hereditary angioedema ([[t:pharyngitis]]).
- **Radiotherapy.**
- **Systemic:** nephritis, heart failure, myxoedema.
## Management
### Airway first
Inspiratory stridor and respiratory distress call for an airway: intubation or tracheostomy.
### Then treat the cause
- **Allergy:** intramuscular adrenaline (0.5 mL of 1:1000 in adults, repeated after five minutes if needed), antihistamines and steroids.
- **Infective, traumatic and post-radiation oedema:** steroids.
- **Hereditary angioedema:** C1 esterase inhibitor or icatibant.
- **ACE inhibitor angioedema:** stop the ACE inhibitor.

# Differential diagnosis | Telling them apart.
!table Croup and epiglottitis in children
Feature | Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) | Acute epiglottitis
Organism | Parainfluenza virus | *H. influenzae* type b
Age | Six months to three years | Two to seven years
Site | Subglottis | Supraglottis
Prodrome | Cold for a day or two | None
Onset | Gradual | Sudden
Fever | Low or moderate | High
General state | Not toxic | Toxic
Cough | Barking | Usually absent
Voice | Hoarse | Muffled
Drooling and painful swallowing | Absent | Present
Posture | Any | Upright, leaning forward
Radiograph | Steeple sign on an AP view | Thumb sign on a lateral view
Treatment | Steroids, nebulised adrenaline, humidified oxygen | Airway in theatre, intravenous antibiotics
!!
- **Croup that fails to respond, with high fever and toxicity:** think of bacterial tracheitis ([[5]]).
- **A membrane on the larynx:** acute membranous laryngitis or diphtheria; swab and culture decide ([[2.5.1]], [[6]]).
- **Breathlessness with a hoarse voice:** not simple laryngitis ([[2.2]]).
- **Recurrent or early croup:** look for a structural cause ([[4.7.2]]).

!pit
- Using a tongue depressor on a drooling, stridulous child.
- Sending a child with epiglottitis to radiology alone.
- Discharging a child soon after nebulised adrenaline.
- Missing epiglottitis in an adult with severe throat pain and a normal oropharynx.
