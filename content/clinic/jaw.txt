@stand Most jaw lesions are found by chance on a dental radiograph, and the radiograph does most of the diagnostic work. Is the lesion radiolucent or radio-opaque? Single or multiple? Well defined or ragged? Unilocular or multilocular? Related to a tooth crown, a root apex, or neither? A periapical cyst on a dead tooth needs a root canal treatment. A keratocyst in a young adult raises the question of Gorlin syndrome. A multilocular "soap-bubble" lesion at the angle is an ameloblastoma until biopsy says otherwise.

# Approach | Reading the jaw.
## Clinical assessment
- **Swelling:** painless bony expansion of the jaw, felt as "egg-shell crackling" when the cortex is thin.
- **Teeth:** displaced or loose, or unerupted (missing).
- **Pain, numbness and infection:**
  - Pain and infection usually mean secondary infection.
  - *Numbness of the lower lip* (inferior alveolar nerve) suggests malignancy or osteomyelitis.
- **Tooth vitality:** test with cold or an electric pulp tester. *Every jaw cyst except the periapical (radicular) cyst is associated with vital teeth.*
- **Aspiration before incisional biopsy:**
  - Blood: a vascular lesion (AVM, aneurysmal bone cyst). *Never biopsy a pulsatile or blood-filled lesion blind*, because torrential bleeding may follow.
  - Straw-coloured fluid: a dentigerous or radicular cyst.
  - Cheesy keratin: an odontogenic keratocyst.
  - Nothing: a solid tumour or a traumatic bone cavity.
## Imaging
- **Panoramic radiograph (OPG) and intraoral films:** the first step.
- **CT or cone-beam CT:** for size, cortical breach, relation to the inferior alveolar canal and sinus, and suspected malignancy.
- **MRI:** for soft-tissue extension.

# Cysts of the jaws | Odontogenic and non-odontogenic.
A true cyst has an epithelial lining. Odontogenic cysts arise from the remnants of tooth-forming epithelium:
- The *rests of Malassez*: radicular cysts.
- The *reduced enamel epithelium*: dentigerous cysts.
- The *dental lamina (rests of Serres)*: keratocysts and lateral periodontal cysts.
## Radicular (periapical) cyst
The *commonest jaw cyst*.
- **Cause:** a non-vital tooth. Pulp necrosis leads to a periapical granuloma, which becomes a cyst.
- **Imaging:** a well-defined, round lucency *at the root apex* of the dead tooth. It may present with a sinus to the gum or skin.
- **Treatment:** root canal treatment, or extraction with enucleation.
- **Residual cyst:** one that persists after the tooth has been extracted. Enucleate it.
## Dentigerous (follicular) cyst
The second commonest. It forms around the *crown of an unerupted tooth*, attached at the amelocemental junction, usually a lower third molar or upper canine, in patients aged 10 to 30.
- **Clinical:** painless expansion. It may displace the tooth a long way, for example into the ramus or the antrum.
- **Treatment:**
  - Enucleation with removal of the tooth.
  - Large cysts in children: *marsupialisation* or decompression, which lets the tooth erupt and the cyst shrink.
- **Rare complications:** ameloblastoma or carcinoma arising in the wall.
- **Eruption cyst:** the soft-tissue version over an erupting tooth in a child. A bluish gum swelling that resolves as the tooth erupts.
## Odontogenic keratocyst (OKC)
- **Classification:** called a "keratocystic odontogenic tumour" in 2005 and reclassified as a cyst in 2017, but aggressive.
- **Who and where:** usually the *posterior body and ramus of the mandible*, in young adults.
- **Growth:** extends along the medulla with little expansion, so it is found late.
- **Histology:** a thin, *parakeratinised* lining with a palisaded, hyperchromatic basal layer and *daughter cysts* in the wall.
- **Aspirate:** cheesy keratin.
- **Recurrence:** up to 30%, because of the friable lining and satellite cysts.
- **Treatment:**
  - Decompression of large cysts.
  - Then enucleation with *peripheral ostectomy* or Carnoy solution.
  - Resection for multiple recurrences.
  - Long follow-up.
- **Gorlin syndrome:** multiple OKCs, especially in a child, mean naevoid BCC syndrome. It is autosomal dominant, from a *PTCH1* mutation. Features:
  - Multiple basal cell carcinomas.
  - Palmar and plantar pits.
  - Calcified falx.
  - Bifid ribs.
  - Frontal bossing and hypertelorism.
  - Medulloblastoma.
  - Avoid radiation.
- **Orthokeratinised odontogenic cyst:** looks similar but has an orthokeratinised lining without palisading. It rarely recurs and has no syndrome.
## Other odontogenic cysts
- **Lateral periodontal cyst:** a small lucency between the roots of vital lower premolars and canines in adults.
- **Glandular odontogenic cyst:** anterior mandible. It can be confused with central mucoepidermoid carcinoma, recurs, and may need resection.
- **Calcifying odontogenic cyst (Gorlin cyst):** "ghost cells" with calcification.
- **Paradental (buccal bifurcation) cyst:** buccal to an erupting lower molar in a child.
## Non-odontogenic cysts
- **Nasopalatine (incisive canal) cyst:** the commonest non-odontogenic cyst. A *heart-shaped* lucency between the upper central incisors, which are vital. Swelling behind the incisors. Enucleate.
- **Nasolabial (nasoalveolar) cyst:**
  - A soft-tissue cyst beneath the ala, filling the nasolabial fold and lifting the nasal floor, mostly in women.
  - Lined by respiratory epithelium.
  - Removed by a sublabial approach, or marsupialised endoscopically into the nose.
- **Pseudocysts (no epithelial lining):**
  - *Traumatic (simple) bone cavity*: an empty cavity in teenagers that scallops between the roots and heals after surgical exploration.
  - *Aneurysmal bone cyst*: a blood-filled, expansile "soap bubble". Curettage.
  - *Stafne defect*: a lingual depression below the inferior alveolar canal at the angle, containing submandibular gland. No treatment.

# Odontogenic tumours | Most are benign.
## Ameloblastoma
The commonest clinically significant odontogenic tumour. Benign but *locally aggressive*.
- **Who and where:** adults in the third to fifth decades. About 80% in the mandible, mostly the *molar–ramus region*.
- **Clinical:** slow, painless expansion. Tooth displacement, mobility and root resorption.
- **Imaging:** a *multilocular "soap-bubble"* or honeycomb lucency with cortical expansion and *knife-edge root resorption*.
- **Histology:** islands of odontogenic epithelium. The peripheral columnar cells show palisading with *reverse polarisation* of nuclei away from the basement membrane. Patterns: follicular, plexiform, acanthomatous and desmoplastic.
- **Molecular:** *BRAF V600E* in most mandibular tumours. BRAF-targeted therapy is used for unresectable or recurrent disease.
- **Variants:**
  - *Unicystic*: young patients, often mimicking a dentigerous cyst. The luminal type can be enucleated with close follow-up. The mural type behaves like a solid tumour.
  - *Peripheral (extraosseous)*: a gum mass.
  - *Metastasising ameloblastoma*: benign histology but lung or nodal metastases.
  - *Ameloblastic carcinoma*.
- **Treatment:**
  - *Resection with about 1 to 1.5 cm margins beyond the radiological edge*, including a soft-tissue margin where the cortex is breached.
  - In the mandible this means marginal or segmental mandibulectomy. In the maxilla, partial maxillectomy.
  - Reconstruction with a fibula free flap and dental implants.
  - Enucleation and curettage give recurrence rates of 50 to 90%. Maxillary tumours can reach the skull base and are more dangerous.
- **Follow-up:** long-term, because recurrence may appear after decades.

## Other odontogenic tumours
- **Odontoma:**
  - The commonest odontogenic tumour overall. It is a hamartoma.
  - *Compound* odontomas contain small tooth-like structures (anterior maxilla).
  - *Complex* odontomas are a disorganised mass of enamel and dentine (posterior mandible).
  - Radio-opaque with a lucent rim. It often prevents a tooth from erupting.
  - Enucleate.
- **Adenomatoid odontogenic tumour:** teenage girls, anterior maxilla, around an unerupted canine. Lucency with fine calcifications. Enucleate.
- **Calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumour (Pindborg):** adults, posterior mandible. Amyloid with concentric *Liesegang ring* calcifications. Resect with a margin.
- **Ameloblastic fibroma:** young patients, posterior mandible. Enucleate.
- **Odontogenic myxoma:**
  - "Soap bubble" or "tennis racket" lucency.
  - Stellate cells in myxoid stroma.
  - Unencapsulated and infiltrative.
  - Resect. Recurs in about a quarter.
- **Cementoblastoma:** a radio-opaque mass fused to a lower molar root, with a lucent halo. Extract the tooth with the mass.

# Non-odontogenic lesions | Bone, fibro-osseous, systemic.
## Fibro-osseous lesions
- **Fibrous dysplasia:**
  - Bone replaced by fibrous tissue and woven bone, from a *GNAS* mutation.
  - Monostotic (about 80%) or polyostotic.
  - *McCune–Albright syndrome*: polyostotic disease, café-au-lait macules and endocrine overactivity (precocious puberty).
  - Painless facial asymmetry in a child or teenager, the maxilla more than the mandible. Growth usually slows after puberty.
  - Imaging: *ground-glass*, ill-defined expansion that blends with normal bone.
  - Treatment: observation, or recontouring for deformity once growth stops. Optic canal decompression if vision is threatened.
- **Ossifying fibroma:**
  - A true, well-demarcated neoplasm, mixed lucent and opaque, in the mandible of young women.
  - Juvenile trabecular and psammomatoid types are aggressive in children and the sinuses.
  - Enucleate or resect.
- **Cemento-osseous dysplasia:** periapical, focal or florid, in middle-aged women of African or Asian descent. Leave it alone. Infection follows extraction.

## Giant cell lesions
- **Central giant cell granuloma:**
  - Under 30 years, anterior mandible, may cross the midline.
  - Multinucleated giant cells.
  - *Exclude hyperparathyroidism*: a *brown tumour* is histologically identical. Check calcium and PTH.
  - Treatment: curettage, or intralesional steroids, calcitonin or denosumab.
- **Cherubism:**
  - Autosomal dominant.
  - Painless *bilateral* swelling of the mandibular angles (and maxillae) from the age of two to five, giving the "cherub" face with upturned eyes.
  - Multilocular lucencies.
  - Regresses after puberty.
## Bone tumours
- **Osteoma:** a dense, well-defined mass. Multiple osteomas with colonic polyposis and soft-tissue tumours make up *Gardner syndrome* (APC mutation). Colonoscopy is mandatory because polyps become malignant.
- **Osteoblastoma:** painful.
- **Chondroma:** rare. Any cartilaginous jaw tumour should be treated as a possible chondrosarcoma.
- **Osteosarcoma of the jaw:**
  - Usually a decade or two older than limb osteosarcoma (third and fourth decades).
  - Presents with swelling, pain, loose teeth and lip numbness.
  - Imaging: a *sunburst* periosteal reaction and *symmetrical widening of the periodontal ligament space* (Garrington sign).
  - Also follows radiotherapy and Paget disease.
  - Treatment: radical resection with negative margins, and chemotherapy.
- **Chondrosarcoma:** in older adults, the anterior maxilla more than the mandible. Radical resection; resistant to radiotherapy and chemotherapy.
- **Metastases:**
  - From breast, lung, prostate, kidney and thyroid.
  - Usually the posterior mandible.
  - Present with pain, lip numbness or a non-healing extraction socket. The "*numb chin*" can be the first sign of cancer.
- **Myeloma and lymphoma:** punched-out lesions.
- **Langerhans cell histiocytosis:** children, punched-out lucencies around the roots ("*floating teeth*"). Curettage, steroids, low-dose radiotherapy or chemotherapy.
- **Squamous carcinoma from the overlying mucosa:** invades the mandible through the occlusal ridge or tooth sockets.
## Paget disease
Disordered remodelling in older people.
- **Features:**
  - Enlarging maxilla and skull, with "*cotton-wool*" radiodensities.
  - Hypercementosis.
  - Raised alkaline phosphatase.
  - Cranial nerve compression and hearing loss.
- **Treatment:** bisphosphonates.

# Osteonecrosis and infection | Dead bone in the jaw.
- **Osteomyelitis:**
  - Usually the mandible, from dental infection or fracture. Diabetes and immunosuppression predispose.
  - Pain, swelling, pus, sequestra, and lip numbness (*Vincent sign*).
  - Treatment: debridement, sequestrectomy and prolonged antibiotics.
- **Osteoradionecrosis:** after radiotherapy (above 60 Gy). Prevention by dental care before radiotherapy. Treatment ranges from debridement and pentoxifylline–tocopherol to resection with a free fibula flap.
- **Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ):**
  - Exposed bone for over eight weeks in a patient taking antiresorptive drugs (bisphosphonates, denosumab) or antiangiogenic drugs, with no history of radiotherapy.
  - Risk is highest with intravenous treatment for cancer. Usually follows an extraction.
  - Prevention: dental treatment before the drugs are started.
  - Treatment: chlorhexidine, antibiotics, conservative debridement, and resection for advanced disease.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Systematic reading of a jaw radiograph,** and why aspiration comes before biopsy.
- **Radicular versus dentigerous cyst:** the tooth relationship and vitality.
- **Odontogenic keratocyst:**
  - Its lining.
  - Why it recurs.
  - Gorlin syndrome.
- **Ameloblastoma:** age, site, soap-bubble picture, reverse polarity, resection margins, BRAF.
- **Fibrous dysplasia** (ground glass) versus ossifying fibroma (well-defined).
- **Giant cell granuloma:** exclude hyperparathyroidism.
- **Osteosarcoma signs;** the numb chin.
- **Osteoradionecrosis and MRONJ.**

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Biopsying a jaw lucency without aspirating first, and meeting an AVM.
- Curetting an ameloblastoma.
- Missing Gorlin syndrome in a young patient with several keratocysts.
- Calling a central giant cell lesion without checking calcium and PTH.
- Extracting teeth in an irradiated jaw or in a patient on intravenous bisphosphonates without precautions.
