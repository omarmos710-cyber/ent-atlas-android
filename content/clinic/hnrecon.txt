@stand A head and neck resection is only half the operation; the other half is putting back what was taken, and the patient lives with the second half. Reconstruction starts with a defect analysis: which tissues are missing (skin, lining, muscle, bone, nerve), what each one did, and what the bed is like. Choose the simplest method that restores both function and appearance. Today that is often a free flap at the same operation. Facial reanimation follows the same logic: what is missing, and for how long?

# Principles | Defect analysis and the ladder.
## What is missing
- **Tissues:** skin, mucosa (lining), muscle, bone, cartilage, nerve.
- **Functions to restore:**
  - A skeleton for occlusion, contour and *dental implants*.
  - Cover for vital structures: the carotid, dura and brain.
  - Oral competence and a sphincter (lips).
  - Pliable, mobile tissue for tongue movement, speech and swallowing.
  - Volume, sensation, and secreting mucosa.
- **The bed:** irradiation, infection, fistula, and the vessels available. Irradiated or infected beds need *vascularised* tissue.
- **The patient:** health, prognosis and wishes.
## The reconstructive ladder (and the elevator)
1. **Healing by secondary intention:** good for *concave* sites (medial canthus, temple, conchal bowl, nasolabial groove, glabella). Bad near *free margins* (eyelid, ala, lip), which contract and distort.
2. **Primary closure:** along relaxed skin tension lines. A fusiform excision of length about 3 times its width.
3. **Grafts:** skin, cartilage, bone, nerve and composite grafts.
4. **Local flaps.**
5. **Regional (pedicled) flaps.**
6. **Free tissue transfer.**
7. **Prostheses.**

Climbing the ladder step by step is not compulsory. The surgeon may take the "elevator" straight to the option that gives the best result, often a free flap for major defects.

# Grafts | Tissue without its own blood supply.
## Skin grafts
- **How they take:**
  1. Plasmatic *imbibition* for about 48 hours.
  2. *Inosculation* (vessel connection) and capillary ingrowth at 3 to 5 days.
- **What makes them fail:** haematoma, seroma, *shearing* and infection. Prevent with a bolster or tie-over dressing, quilting sutures, pie-crusting, or negative pressure.
- **Beds:** they do not take on bare bone or cartilage without periosteum or perichondrium, nor on irradiated beds.
- **Split-thickness grafts:**
  - Taken with a dermatome from the thigh or buttock.
  - They take more reliably but *contract more* and match colour poorly.
  - Uses: lining of the mouth, maxillectomy cavities, the scalp over periosteum, and free flap donor sites.
- **Full-thickness grafts:**
  - From postauricular, preauricular, upper eyelid or supraclavicular skin, defatted.
  - Less contraction and better match.
  - Uses: the nose, eyelids and ear.
## Cartilage, bone and nerve grafts
- **Cartilage:**
  - *Septal*: leave a 1 to 1.5 cm L-strut.
  - *Conchal*: curved, for the ala and ear.
  - *Costal*: plentiful, for the dorsum and microtia. It can warp.
  - *Composite* skin–cartilage grafts from the ear: for alar rim and columella defects up to about 1 to 1.5 cm.
- **Bone:**
  - *Cortical*: split calvaria, rib, iliac crest. Used for orbital floor, nasal dorsum and facial buttresses.
  - *Cancellous*: iliac crest. Used for alveolar clefts and small mandibular gaps.
  - All free bone grafts need a well-vascularised, unirradiated bed and rigid fixation, and they resorb.
- **Nerve:**
  - *Great auricular*: in the field, up to about 10 cm.
  - *Sural*: up to 30 to 35 cm, for cross-face grafts.
  - *Medial antebrachial cutaneous*.

# Local flaps | Like for like.
## Principles
- **Blood supply:** random-pattern flaps live on the subdermal plexus. A length-to-width ratio of up to about 3:1 on the face, more in the well-vascularised face than on the trunk.
- **Design:**
  - Place scars in the relaxed skin tension lines and at the borders of *aesthetic subunits*.
  - Borrow from areas of skin laxity (cheek, glabella, neck).
  - Close the secondary defect without distorting free margins.
- **Hazards:** smoking, tension, irradiation and haematoma.
## Types
- **Advancement:** V-Y, unipedicle or bipedicle (the forehead, the lip with crescentic perialar excision).
- **Rotation:** cervicofacial and scalp flaps.
- **Transposition:**
  - *Rhomboid (Limberg)*.
  - *Bilobed (Zitelli)*: for the lower third of the nose, up to about 1.5 cm.
  - *Nasolabial (melolabial)*: for the ala.
  - *Note flap*.
- **Interpolated flaps:** pass over or under intact skin and need division later.
  - The *paramedian forehead flap* on the *supratrochlear artery*: the workhorse for large or full-thickness nasal defects, divided at three weeks.
  - The nasolabial interpolated flap.
  - The *Abbe* cross-lip flap.
- **Full-thickness nasal reconstruction:** needs *three layers*: lining (local mucosal flaps or a turnover flap), framework (cartilage grafts), and cover (the forehead flap).

## Lip reconstruction
!table Full-thickness lip defects
Defect | Reconstruction
Under a third (up to about a half of the lower lip) | Primary (V or W) closure
A third to a half | Abbe cross-lip flap (on the labial artery, divided at about three weeks); Estlander flap if the commissure is involved
A half to two-thirds | Karapandzic (neurovascular, sensate, innervated rotation–advancement); bilateral advancement
Over two-thirds to total | Bernard–Burow (cheek advancement), Gillies fan flap, or a free radial forearm flap with a palmaris longus sling
!!
Restoring the *orbicularis sphincter*, the vermilion line and the commissure matters most. *Microstomia* is the price of large local flaps.

# Free flaps | Microvascular transfer.
## Principles
- **Recipient vessels:**
  - Arteries: the facial, superior thyroid and lingual arteries, or the transverse cervical artery.
  - Veins: the internal jugular (end-to-side), external jugular or facial veins.
  - In a previously treated neck, a contralateral or vein-grafted approach may be needed.
- **Technique points:**
  - Two teams.
  - Short ischaemia time: bowel is most sensitive, then muscle and bone.
  - Careful pedicle geometry: no kinking, twisting or tension.
- **Monitoring:**
  - *Most failures occur within 72 hours*, usually venous thrombosis.
  - Monitor colour, capillary refill, temperature, pinprick bleeding and a hand-held or implantable Doppler, hourly at first.
  - Keep the patient warm, well filled and normotensive. Avoid vasoconstrictors where possible. Give aspirin or heparin according to unit policy.
- **The failing flap:**
  - *Venous congestion*: purple, swollen, brisk dark bleeding.
  - *Arterial insufficiency*: pale, cool, no bleeding.
  - Return to theatre urgently. Salvage is far more likely within hours.
- **Success rates:** about 95 to 99%.

## Choosing the flap
- **Mandible:**
  - Lateral (segmental) defects: a fibula, or sometimes a plate with a soft-tissue flap in poor-prognosis patients.
  - Anterior arch defects must be rebuilt with bone, or the "*Andy Gump*" deformity follows: a collapsed chin, drooling, and loss of oral competence.
- **Maxilla:** obturator prosthesis or free flap (fibula, scapula, anterolateral thigh), according to the size of the defect, the palate and the orbital floor.
- **Tongue:**
  - Partial glossectomy: a radial forearm flap (thin, mobile).
  - Total glossectomy: a bulky flap (anterolateral thigh, rectus) to fill the mouth and touch the palate.
- **Pharynx:**
  - Partial defects: a patch of radial forearm or anterolateral thigh.
  - Circumferential defects: a tubed anterolateral thigh or radial forearm flap, jejunum, or a gastric pull-up when the oesophagus is removed.

# Facial reanimation | Time decides the method.
## Questions to ask
1. What caused the palsy, and is the nerve in continuity?
2. Is the proximal stump available?
3. *How long has the face been paralysed?* Facial muscles survive denervation for about 18 to 24 months, after which motor end plates are lost and muscle atrophies.
4. Which regions are affected, and what does the patient want: eye protection, symmetry at rest, a smile?
## Protect the eye first
- **Medical:** lubricating drops by day and ointment at night, taping, a moisture chamber.
- **Upper eyelid loading:** a *gold or platinum weight* in the upper lid.
- **Lower lid procedures:** lateral tarsal strip or canthoplasty for a lax lower lid.
- **Brow lift** for brow ptosis.
- **Tarsorrhaphy** if the cornea is at risk.
- **Botulinum toxin** to the levator to produce a temporary protective ptosis.
## Restoring the nerve (within about 18 to 24 months)
- **Primary repair:** tension-free neurorrhaphy, epineurial suture, at the time of injury. *Any transection in the temporal bone or at parotidectomy is repaired immediately.*
- **Interposition grafting:** great auricular or sural nerve, when the gap is 1 cm or more. Function returns at 6 to 12 months, usually with some *synkinesis*. Branches medial to a vertical line through the lateral canthus are usually not repaired, because of cross-innervation.
- **Nerve transfer**, when the proximal stump is unavailable but the distal nerve and muscle are viable:
  - *Masseteric nerve to facial nerve* (V to VII): now the commonest. Fast, strong, little morbidity. The smile is driven by biting, and is usually not spontaneous.
  - *Hypoglossal to facial nerve* (XII to VII): a partial (split or jump-graft) technique limits tongue atrophy. Good tone, with synkinesis.
  - *Cross-face nerve grafting*: contralateral buccal branches through a sural graft. Stage two follows when Tinel sign shows the axons have crossed (about 8 to 12 months). The only method giving a *spontaneous, emotional smile*, but weaker. Often combined with a masseteric transfer as a "*babysitter*" to keep the muscles alive.
## Long-standing palsy (muscle lost)
- **Dynamic muscle transfers:**
  - *Free functional gracilis transfer*, powered by the masseteric nerve (one stage) or a cross-face graft (two stages). The gold standard for smile reanimation. Also used for *Möbius syndrome*.
  - *Temporalis transfer*: orthodromic (lengthening temporalis myoplasty from the coronoid) or the older turnover over the arch.
- **Static procedures:**
  - Fascia lata or acellular dermal slings to lift the commissure and support the nasal valve.
  - Brow lift.
  - Lower lid tightening.
## Synkinesis and hyperkinesis
- **What it is:** after aberrant regeneration (Bell palsy, repaired nerve), there are involuntary co-contractions (eye closes when smiling) and hypertonicity.
- **Treatment:**
  - *Neuromuscular retraining* (facial physiotherapy).
  - *Botulinum toxin* to the overactive muscles on the affected side, and to the normal side for symmetry.
  - Selective neurectomy or myectomy.

# Other options | Prostheses and beyond.
- **Prostheses:**
  - Auricular, nasal and orbital prostheses on *osseointegrated implants* or adhesives.
  - Palatal *obturators* after maxillectomy.
  - Good for the elderly, the irradiated, after failed reconstruction, and for surveillance of the cavity.
- **Tissue expansion:** for the scalp and neck.
- **Virtual surgical planning:** CT-based 3D models, cutting guides and custom plates for mandibular and maxillary reconstruction.
- **Facial allotransplantation:** composite facial transplants for devastating defects. Lifelong immunosuppression, rejection and ethics remain limiting.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **The reconstructive ladder;** sites that heal well by secondary intention.
- **Split- versus full-thickness grafts:** take, contraction, uses.
- **Bilobed, rhomboid and paramedian forehead flaps:** blood supply, three-layer nasal repair.
- **Lip reconstruction by size of defect:** Abbe, Estlander, Karapandzic.
- **The pectoralis major flap:** its pedicle and reach.
- **Radial forearm, anterolateral thigh and fibula flaps:** pedicles, uses, and pre-operative checks (Allen test, leg vessel imaging).
- **Free flap monitoring** and the failing flap.
- **Facial reanimation by time since injury;** masseteric versus hypoglossal versus cross-face grafting; the gracilis flap; the eye first.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Skin grafting onto irradiated tissue or bare bone.
- Leaving an anterior mandibular defect unreconstructed.
- Raising a radial forearm flap without an Allen test.
- Delaying return to theatre for a congested free flap.
- Waiting beyond two years before trying to reinnervate the face.
!!
