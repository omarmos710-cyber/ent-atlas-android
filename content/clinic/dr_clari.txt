@stand Clarithromycin is the macrolide an ENT surgeon uses most: the usual alternative for penicillin-allergic patients with otitis media, sinusitis or tonsillitis, and, given at low dose for three months, an anti-inflammatory treatment for chronic rhinosinusitis without polyps. It is a potent inhibitor of the liver enzyme CYP3A4, so its interactions matter more than its spectrum.

# How it works | Mechanism and handling.
## Antibacterial and anti-inflammatory action
At usual doses it stops bacterial protein synthesis at the 50S subunit. At low doses given for months, macrolides reduce interleukin-8 and neutrophil recruitment, reduce goblet-cell mucus secretion and interfere with quorum sensing and biofilms; this immunomodulatory effect, not killing, is the rationale in chronic rhinosinusitis.
## Handling
Well absorbed, concentrates in tissues and macrophages; metabolised by CYP3A4 to an active metabolite and excreted partly in urine. Halve the dose when the eGFR is below 30 ml/min.

# Using it in ENT | Indications and doses.
## Acute infection in penicillin allergy
NICE lists clarithromycin (or erythromycin, and doxycycline in adults) as the alternative in penicillin allergy for acute otitis media, acute sinusitis and sore throat.
## Chronic rhinosinusitis
Long-term low-dose macrolides are considered for **chronic rhinosinusitis without polyps** with low IgE and neutrophilic inflammation, after maximal medical therapy, as in the EPOS and BRS guidance: 250 mg once daily for 12 weeks, continued only if it helps. Benefit is modest and less clear in eosinophilic polyposis. Check an **ECG for QT** and a statin and drug history before starting.
## Mycobacterial lymphadenitis
Non-tuberculous mycobacterial node infection in young children is best treated by **complete excision**; where excision would risk the facial nerve, clarithromycin with rifampicin or rifabutin for 3 to 6 months is an alternative.

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
- Nausea, diarrhoea, abdominal pain and a metallic taste (common).
- **QT prolongation** and torsades de pointes, especially with other QT-prolonging drugs or hypokalaemia.
- Reversible hearing loss and tinnitus at high doses.
- Hepatotoxicity; C. difficile; increased cardiovascular mortality was reported years after a course in patients with coronary heart disease, so use with caution in them.
## Interactions
A strong **CYP3A4 inhibitor**: contraindicated with simvastatin (rhabdomyolysis), and raises levels of atorvastatin, ciclosporin, tacrolimus, carbamazepine, theophylline, midazolam, DOACs, calcium-channel blockers and warfarin (raised INR). Additive QT effects with antipsychotics, quinolones, ondansetron and antiarrhythmics.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Starting long-term clarithromycin in a patient on simvastatin.
- No ECG before long-term therapy in a patient with cardiac disease or on QT drugs.
- Expecting long-term macrolides to shrink eosinophilic nasal polyps.
!!
