@stand The same small space behind the drum produces two very different patients: a feverish child in pain with a bulging drum, and a quiet child with fluid who simply does not hear well. The first needs pain relief and judgement about antibiotics; the second needs patience, a hearing test, and sometimes a grommet.

# Overview | Two diseases of one cleft.
## Definitions
### Acute otitis media
Rapid onset, within 48 hours, of middle ear inflammation with an effusion: pain, fever and a red, bulging drum.
### Recurrent acute otitis media
Three or more episodes in six months, or four or more in a year, with the child well in between.
### Otitis media with effusion
Fluid in the middle ear behind an intact drum, without signs of acute infection. Also called *secretory otitis media* or *glue ear*. It is *chronic* once it has lasted three months.
### Acute necrotising otitis media
A rare, destructive form, usually from beta-haemolytic streptococcus after measles or scarlet fever, which rapidly destroys the drum, annulus and ossicles.
## Epidemiology
Acute otitis media is one of the commonest reasons a young child sees a doctor, peaking between six months and two years. Effusion follows most acute attacks and is the commonest cause of hearing loss in childhood, with peaks around two and again around five years.
## Why it matters
Acute disease is about pain and the rare but serious complication. Effusion is about hearing, speech, behaviour and school, in a child who often does not complain at all.

# Pathogenesis | Why children, and why after a cold.
## The Eustachian tube in a child
The child's tube is shorter, wider and more horizontal than the adult's, and its muscles open it less efficiently. Secretions from the nasopharynx reach the middle ear easily, and the middle ear drains poorly.
!anat ns205_3 | The Eustachian tube with tensor and levator veli palatini, the muscles that open it.
## The sequence
A viral cold inflames the tube and the adenoid. The tube blocks, gas in the middle ear is absorbed, negative pressure develops and a transudate collects. Bacteria from the nasopharynx, where the adenoid acts as a reservoir, then infect the fluid.
## Organisms
### Bacteria
*Streptococcus pneumoniae* leads, followed by non-typeable *Haemophilus influenzae* and *Moraxella catarrhalis*. About half of the *H. influenzae* and almost all *Moraxella* produce beta-lactamase.
### Viruses
RSV, rhinovirus and adenovirus are found in many aspirates, alone or with bacteria.
## Risk factors
!tree Who gets otitis media
Environment | Daycare, siblings | smoke exposure, dummy use, bottle feeding lying flat, no breastfeeding
Anatomy | Cleft palate | Down syndrome, craniofacial syndromes
Host | Prematurity | immunodeficiency, low socioeconomic status
Adenoid | Bacterial reservoir | and tubal obstruction

# Acute otitis media | Pain, fever and a bulging drum.
## Stages
Acute suppurative otitis media runs through a sequence of stages, and the drum changes at each.
### Tubal occlusion
The drum is retracted with a foreshortened handle of malleus; there is deafness and fullness, and little fever.
### Presuppuration
Hyperaemia spreads from the handle of malleus and the periphery in a *cart-wheel* pattern. Pain is throbbing, and a child may have a high fever.
### Suppuration
Pus collects under pressure: the drum is red and bulging, landmarks vanish, and a yellow spot may mark where it will rupture. Pain is severe.
### Resolution
The drum ruptures, usually with a small anteroinferior perforation, discharge appears and pain and fever settle. Most perforations heal.
### Complication
If infection spreads beyond the cleft: mastoiditis ([[t:mastoiditis]]), facial palsy, labyrinthitis or intracranial disease ([[t:icc]]).
## Clinical picture
Otalgia, fever, irritability, ear pulling and poor feeding, often after a cold. These symptoms are non-specific on their own; the diagnosis rests on the drum.
## Diagnosis
*Pneumatic otoscopy* is the essential examination. A bulging drum with poor mobility makes the diagnosis. A red drum in a crying child is not enough, because crying flushes the drum. Tympanometry helps when the view is poor.
!flag Look for a complication first
- Facial weakness, vertigo, mastoid tenderness or swelling, neck stiffness, drowsiness.
- A child with a cochlear implant, immunodeficiency or a septic appearance is treated, not observed.
!!
## Management
### Pain relief for everyone
Paracetamol or ibuprofen, whatever is decided about antibiotics.
### Observation or antibiotics
Most acute otitis media settles without antibiotics in three days to a week, and antibiotics make only a small difference to pain (about 1 in 20 children benefits at two to three days, while about 1 in 14 gets diarrhoea, rash or vomiting). They do not meaningfully prevent mastoiditis: thousands of children would need treating to prevent one case. So the default is analgesia, safety-netting, and either no antibiotic or a back-up prescription to use if not better in three days or worse at any time.
!table When to give an immediate antibiotic (NICE)
Situation | Plan
Systemically very unwell, or signs of a complication | Immediate antibiotic; admit if a complication is suspected
Under 3 months with fever | Urgent paediatric assessment
High risk (immunocompromised, cochlear implant, significant heart, lung, kidney or neuromuscular disease) | Immediate antibiotic
Under 2 years with infection in both ears | Consider immediate antibiotic, or a back-up prescription
Any age with otorrhoea (a perforation) | Consider immediate antibiotic, or a back-up prescription
All others | No antibiotic or a back-up prescription; analgesia; review if worse
!!
!differ When to treat
NICE (2018): analgesia for all; immediate antibiotics only for the groups above; back-up prescriptions otherwise.
American Academy of Pediatrics (2013): antibiotics for severe disease (fever 39°C or more, or moderate to severe pain for over 48 hours) and for non-severe bilateral disease under two; observation is an option for non-severe unilateral disease from six months, and for non-severe disease of either kind from two years.
In practice: Follow the UK scheme; know both for examinations.
!!
### Choice of antibiotic
- **First line:** amoxicillin for 5 to 7 days (the UK uses standard doses; the American guideline uses high-dose 80 to 90 mg/kg/day to cover resistant pneumococci).
- **Penicillin allergy:** clarithromycin (or erythromycin).
- **Not improving after 2 to 3 days of amoxicillin:** co-amoxiclav.
- **Failure of second-line treatment** in a child who is unwell: specialist review, consider tympanocentesis or myringotomy for culture, and intravenous treatment (for example ceftriaxone).
!differ Dose and duration of amoxicillin
Dhingra: 40 mg/kg/day for at least 10 days.
Lee: 90 mg/kg/day (high dose, to overcome resistant pneumococci), with duration by age.
NICE: 5 to 7 days at standard doses.
In practice: Follow local guidance; in the UK, standard-dose amoxicillin for 5 to 7 days. Know all three for examinations.
!!
### Myringotomy
Indicated for a bulging drum with severe pain, incomplete resolution with persisting hearing loss, or effusion beyond 12 weeks, and in the child who fails second-line treatment, when the fluid is sent for culture.

## Recurrent acute otitis media
Defined above as three or more episodes in six months, or four or more in a year. Reduce risk factors (smoke exposure, dummies, day care where possible), encourage breastfeeding, and check vaccinations (pneumococcal and Hib); children who keep having infections despite vaccination may have poor antibody responses. The options are:
- **Watchful management,** treating each episode promptly; many children grow out of it.
- **Grommets,** which let infections be treated with antibiotic drops instead of oral antibiotics and may reduce episodes in the first six months; but in the largest randomised trial they did not reduce the number of episodes over two years compared with medical management (Hoberman et al., *NEJM* 2021). They are the usual choice when effusion persists between attacks.
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis** (for example amoxicillin at half dose, or azithromycin three times a week, for two to three months) roughly halves episodes while it is taken, but the effect stops with the drug and resistance is a concern; it is rarely used in the UK.
!differ Recurrent otitis media
Dhingra: Mentions amoxicillin prophylaxis, while noting its drawbacks and that many prefer grommets; grommets for 4 episodes in 6 months or 6 in a year.
Lee: Does not recommend prophylaxis; defines recurrence as 3 in 6 months or 4 in 12 months.
In practice: Prophylaxis is rarely used because of resistance; grommets are offered when effusion persists between attacks, though they do not reduce episodes over two years.
!!

# Otitis media with effusion | The quiet ear that cannot hear.
## Pathogenesis
Tubal dysfunction leaves negative pressure and fluid behind; the mucosa responds with more goblet cells and mucous glands, and the fluid becomes thick and tenacious, the classic *glue*. Persistent adenoid infection, allergy and unresolved acute otitis media all contribute.
## Clinical picture
### Symptoms
Often none: a parent or teacher notices inattention, poor speech, or a child turning up the television. Older children and adults describe fullness and muffled hearing.
### Signs
The drum is dull and opaque, sometimes amber, grey or bluish, with fine radial vessels rather than redness. It is often retracted, and may show an air-fluid level or bubbles. Mobility on pneumatic otoscopy is reduced.
!fig d10-2 | Otitis media with effusion: bubbles appear behind the drum on Valsalva.
## Investigations
### Tympanometry
A flat (type B) curve, or one shifted far to the negative side (type C).
### Audiometry
Usually a 20 to 40 dB conductive loss. An audiogram is needed when the effusion has lasted three months, or sooner if there is a speech or developmental concern.
### The nasopharynx in adults
!flag Unilateral effusion in an adult
Always examine the nasopharynx: nasopharyngeal carcinoma can first present this way, especially in smokers and people from endemic regions.
!!
!differ Scoping the nasopharynx
Dhingra: Always exclude a nasopharyngeal tumour in an adult with one-sided effusion.
Lee: The value of endoscopy is clearest in high-risk patients.
In practice: Examine the postnasal space in every adult with a persistent one-sided effusion, and biopsy if anything is abnormal; the threshold is lowest in smokers and people from endemic regions.
!!
!imaging | Adenoid enlargement, and otomastoiditis with its complications.
## Natural history
Most effusions clear on their own: screen-detected effusions clear in about 63% by three months and 88% by a year, and around 90% of effusions after treated acute otitis media resolve. But once a **bilateral** effusion has lasted three months, only about a quarter clear by six months. It affects 60 to 85% of children with Down syndrome or cleft palate.
## Management
NICE's 2023 guideline (NG233) recommends shared decisions at each step.
### Monitoring
Children with effusion but no hearing loss need reassurance only. With hearing loss (thresholds worse than 20 dB HL), reassess hearing after three months for bilateral disease, and consider it for unilateral disease. Antibiotics, steroids, antihistamines, decongestants and anti-reflux drugs have no lasting benefit and are not recommended.
!differ Medical treatment of effusion
Dhingra: Lists decongestants, antihistamines and antibiotics among the medical options.
Lee: Guidelines find no role for them.
In practice: Do not use them; follow current guidelines (NICE NG233).
!!
### Autoinflation
Consider a nasal balloon in children old enough to use it; it can speed clearance.
### Hearing aids and devices
Air-conduction hearing aids are an equal alternative to surgery when the loss is stable; **bone-conduction devices** (on a soft band) when the hearing fluctuates or air-conduction aids are unsuitable (narrow canals, otorrhoea). Preferred in many children with Down syndrome.
### Grommets
Consider grommets for persistent effusion with hearing loss, especially bilateral loss affecting speech, learning, behaviour or quality of life, or in children at risk (speech delay, sensory or learning difficulty, cleft palate, Down syndrome). Discuss the risks: otorrhoea, perforation, atrophy and tympanosclerosis (the operation is described in [[t:earsurg]]). Thick glue may need two incisions, one to let air in behind the fluid.
!fig d10-3 d10-4 | First, two incisions in the drum let thick mucus be aspirated. Second, a grommet in place.
!ops
### Adenoidectomy
NICE now advises **considering adenoidectomy alongside grommets** for children having them, unless there is a palatal abnormality (cleft or submucous cleft, which risks velopharyngeal insufficiency). It reduces the chance of needing grommets again. The American guideline offers tubes alone under four years, and tubes, adenoidectomy or both from four years.
### After grommets
Otorrhoea occurs in about 16% soon after surgery and 26% later; treat it with topical antibiotic or antibiotic-steroid drops rather than oral antibiotics. Blockage occurs in up to 7%, granulation in 5%, tympanosclerosis in about 32% and focal atrophy in 25%; persistent perforation in about 2%, rising to about 17% with long-stay tubes. Most children need no water precautions. Children with craniofacial anomalies or learning disability need a planned follow-up, because recurrent effusion is easily missed.
!pearl Pearls
- Almost every child with a cleft palate has effusion: offer earlier tubes, and keep watching after palate repair.
- Grommet otorrhoea is treated with topical drops, not oral antibiotics.
- Propping an infant up during feeds reduces reflux up the tube.
!!
!update Current evidence
A Cochrane review of 13 trials found that antibiotics did not reduce pain at 24 hours and reduced it at two to three days in only 1 child in 20, while causing side effects in about 1 in 14 (Venekamp et al., *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2015). In a randomised trial of 250 children with recurrent acute otitis media, grommets did not reduce the rate of episodes over two years compared with medical management (1.48 vs 1.56 a year), though the first episode came later (Hoberman et al., *N Engl J Med* 2021). For effusion, a Cochrane review found grommets improved hearing by about 10 dB in the first months, with the benefit fading by a year (Browning et al., 2010). NICE's 2023 guideline on effusion (NG233) added autoinflation, bone-conduction devices and consideration of adenoidectomy with grommets.
!!

# Complications and sequelae | Acute and long term.
## Of acute otitis media
- Complications now follow only 0.05 to 0.25% of episodes.
- Perforation in about 5%; around 90% heal.
- Acute mastoiditis ([[t:mastoiditis]]; fewer than 0.4% of episodes, mostly under two years) and subperiosteal abscess; intravenous antibiotics alone succeed in about three-quarters.
- Facial palsy and labyrinthitis.
- Meningitis and other intracranial complications ([[t:icc]]).
## Of chronic effusion
- Atrophic, retracted drum and atelectasis.
- Necrosis of the long process of the incus.
- Tympanosclerosis.
- Retraction pockets ([[t:tmperf]]) and cholesteatoma ([[t:chole]]).
- Cholesterol granuloma.
## Masked disease
Inadequate antibiotic courses can leave a smouldering ear that becomes a persistent effusion or masked mastoiditis.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## On call
!ward A child with a painful ear
1. Look for complications first: face, balance, mastoid, neck and conscious level.
2. Give analgesia to every child.
3. Perform pneumatic otoscopy; a red drum alone is not enough.
4. Decide antibiotics, a back-up prescription or no antibiotic by risk, age, both ears and otorrhoea.
5. Safety-net: return if worse at 48–72 hours, with neck swelling, weakness or drowsiness.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Diagnosing otitis media from a red drum in a crying child.
- Withholding analgesia while deciding about antibiotics.
- Treating effusion with decongestants and antihistamines.
- Missing nasopharyngeal carcinoma in an adult with one-sided glue.
