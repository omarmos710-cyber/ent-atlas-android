@stand The external nose sits in the middle of the face, so its problems are seen by everyone: infections of the skin and vestibule, deformities after injury, the bulbous rhinophyma of rosacea, and skin cancers that love the sun-exposed tip and ala. Two rules stand out: never squeeze a nasal furuncle, and biopsy any lesion that does not heal.

# Overview | Skin, cartilage and the vestibule.
## Applied anatomy
The upper third of the nose is bony (paired nasal bones and the frontal processes of the maxillae); the lower two-thirds is cartilaginous (upper lateral cartilages, lower lateral or alar cartilages, and the septal cartilage). The skin is thin and mobile over the dorsum and thick and sebaceous over the tip and alae. The *vestibule* is the skin-lined entrance, with hairs (vibrissae), up to the limen nasi.
!fig d23-1 d23-2 | First, the parts of the nose and related facial structures. Second, the osteocartilaginous framework.
!anat s94_11 s97_18 | The osteocartilaginous framework of the nose, and the change in skin thickness from nasion to tip.
## The danger area
Veins of the upper lip and nose drain through the facial and angular veins and the ophthalmic veins to the cavernous sinus, without valves. Infection here can spread to the cavernous sinus.
!anat s97_22 | The danger area of the face and its venous drainage to the cavernous sinus.

# Infections | Skin and vestibule.
## Furuncle of the vestibule
### Cause
Acute staphylococcal infection of a hair follicle, usually after nose picking or plucking hairs.
### Clinical picture
A small, exquisitely tender red swelling in the vestibule, with redness and swelling spreading to the tip and dorsum; it may point and rupture.
!fig d25-9 | Furuncle of the right nasal vestibule.
### Treatment
Warm compresses, analgesia, and topical and systemic antistaphylococcal antibiotics; incise only when fluctuant.
!flag Never squeeze a nasal furuncle
Squeezing or early incision can spread infection through the angular and ophthalmic veins to the cavernous sinus. Other complications: cellulitis of the upper lip and septal abscess. Recurrent furuncles mean checking for diabetes and nasal staphylococcal carriage.
!!
## Vestibulitis
### Cause
Diffuse dermatitis of the vestibular skin, usually *Staphylococcus aureus*, encouraged by nasal discharge from rhinitis or sinusitis and the trauma of wiping.
### Clinical picture
Acute: red, swollen, tender skin with crusts over erosions, sometimes involving the upper lip. Chronic: thickened skin with painful fissures and crusts.
!fig d25-10 | Acute vestibulitis on the left side.
### Treatment
Clean the crusts with hydrogen peroxide, apply an antibiotic-steroid ointment and continue it for several days after apparent cure; cauterise a chronic fissure with silver nitrate; treat the source of discharge.
## Cellulitis
Streptococcal or staphylococcal infection making the nose red, swollen and tender, sometimes spreading from the vestibule; systemic antibiotics, warmth and analgesia.
## Specific infections
Lupus vulgaris (apple-jelly nodules), leprosy and syphilis also affect the external nose (see the granulomatous disease topic).

# Deformities | Shape and function.
!fig d25-1 | Deformities of the nose.
## Saddle nose
A depressed dorsum, bony, cartilaginous or both.
### Causes
Depressed fractures (the commonest), excessive removal of septal cartilage at submucous resection, septal haematoma or abscess, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, cocaine, leprosy, tuberculosis, syphilis and relapsing polychondritis.
### Treatment
*Augmentation rhinoplasty*: septal, auricular or rib cartilage for a cartilaginous depression; bone (iliac crest or calvarial) for bony and cartilaginous loss. Autografts are preferred; synthetic implants tend to extrude.
## Hump nose
A dorsal hump of bone, cartilage or both, corrected by *reduction rhinoplasty*: removal of the hump and lateral osteotomies to close the open roof.
## Crooked and deviated nose
In a *crooked* nose, the dorsum from the root to the tip is C- or S-shaped; in a *deviated* nose, the dorsum is straight but lies to one side. Both usually follow injury, often in childhood. Correction by *septorhinoplasty* restores both appearance and airway.
!fig d25-2 | The nasal bridge is S-shaped in a crooked nose, and straight but displaced in a deviated nose.
## Nasal valve collapse
Weak or scarred lateral walls collapse on inspiration; Cottle's test is positive. Spreader grafts, alar batten grafts or functional rhinoplasty.

# Tumours of the external nose | Benign and malignant.
## Benign
Papilloma (wart), haemangioma, pigmented naevus, seborrhoeic keratosis, neurofibroma and sweat gland tumours. Congenital masses (dermoid, glioma, encephalocele) are covered in their own topic.
## Rhinophyma
The *potato nose*: a slow, lobulated, pink overgrowth of sebaceous glands and connective tissue over the tip and alae, with dilated vessels, in men past middle age with long-standing acne rosacea. It is cosmetic, but may block breathing or vision. Treatment is shaving it down with a scalpel, dermabrasion or carbon dioxide laser, letting the surface re-epithelialise from the remaining glands.
!fig d25-4b | Rhinophyma.
## Basal cell carcinoma
The commonest malignant tumour of the nasal skin (about 87%), at 40 to 60 years, on the tip and ala: a pearly nodule or an ulcer with rolled edges (*rodent ulcer*). Slow, locally destructive, almost never metastasises. Excision with 3 to 5 mm margins, Mohs surgery for high-risk sites and recurrences, or radiotherapy or cryotherapy for early lesions; reconstruction with local flaps.
!fig d25-6 | Basal cell carcinoma of the nose.
## Squamous cell carcinoma
The second commonest (about 11%), an infiltrating nodule or ulcer with everted edges on the side of the nose or columella; nodal metastases in about 20%. Radiotherapy for early lesions; wide excision with reconstruction, and neck dissection for nodes.
!fig d25-7a d25-7b | Squamous cell carcinoma of the nose, and of the nasal tip.
## Melanoma
The least common: superficial spreading or nodular; wide excision.
## Total rhinectomy
For advanced cancers, followed by a prosthesis or a forehead flap reconstruction.

# The vestibule | Narrowing and swellings.
## Stenosis and atresia of the nares
Trauma, burns, surgery, destructive inflammation (formerly smallpox) and Young's operation can narrow the nostrils; congenital atresia from failure of canalisation is rare. Corrected by excision of scar with flaps or grafts and stenting.
!fig d25-11 | Stenosis of the left naris after smallpox (A) and after trauma (B).
## Nasoalveolar (nasolabial) cyst
A smooth swelling in the floor and lateral wall of the vestibule, raising the ala and filling the nasolabial fold; excised through a sublabial incision.
!fig d25-12 | A nasoalveolar cyst at operation (arrow).
## Vestibular papilloma and carcinoma
Warts are excised. Squamous carcinoma of the vestibule may spread to the floor, columella and upper lip, and to the parotid and submandibular nodes.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Squeezing or incising an early furuncle.
- Treating a non-healing ulcer with creams without biopsy.
- Using synthetic implants that later extrude.
!!
