@stand Benzodiazepines such as diazepam enhance GABA-mediated inhibition, including in the vestibular nuclei, and reduce the anxiety that accompanies severe vertigo. They can help in the first day or two of a violent acute vestibular attack, but they impair compensation, cause sedation and falls, and lead to dependence. They have little place in chronic dizziness.

# At a glance | The drugs on one page.
## Summary
!table Benzodiazepines for vertigo
 | Summary
Agents | Diazepam (long-acting); lorazepam (shorter-acting); clonazepam (sometimes in persistent postural-perceptual dizziness or vestibular paroxysmia alternatives)
How they work | Positive allosteric modulators of the GABA-A receptor; vestibular suppression and anxiolysis
ENT uses | Severe acute vertigo for 1 to 3 days when other sedatives are insufficient; acute anxiety associated with vertigo
Adult dose | Diazepam 2 to 5 mg up to three times daily for a few days
Avoid | Respiratory depression, sleep apnoea, myasthenia, severe hepatic impairment; the elderly where possible
!!

# Using them in ENT | Indications.
## Acute vertigo
Reserve for the most distressing first days of an attack; combine with an antiemetic and stop as soon as possible. Persistent dizziness after an acute vestibular event (persistent postural-perceptual dizziness) is better treated with vestibular rehabilitation, cognitive behavioural therapy and SSRIs or SNRIs than with benzodiazepines.

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
Sedation, ataxia, falls, confusion; respiratory depression with opioids; tolerance and dependence after 2 to 4 weeks; withdrawal symptoms. In patients with obstructive sleep apnoea, they worsen nocturnal apnoea.
## Interactions
Opioids (fatal respiratory depression), alcohol, other sedatives; CYP3A4 inhibitors raise diazepam levels.
