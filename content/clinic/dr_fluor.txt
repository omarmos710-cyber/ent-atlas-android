@stand Intrathecal fluorescein stains cerebrospinal fluid bright yellow-green, so that a leak from the skull base or ear can be seen during endoscopic repair. It is widely used and helpful, but it is not licensed for intrathecal use, serious neurological complications have occurred at higher doses, and the patient must be consented specifically. Dilute, low-dose technique makes complications rare.

# At a glance | The agent on one page.
## Summary
!table Intrathecal fluorescein
 | Summary
Agent | Sodium fluorescein (preservative-free 10% or 5% solution)
How it works | A fluorescent dye that mixes with CSF and appears at the site of a leak; a blue-light filter on the endoscope increases sensitivity
ENT uses | Localising CSF leaks at endoscopic repair (spontaneous, traumatic, iatrogenic, encephaloceles); confirming a watertight repair
Dose | Commonly 0.1 ml of 10% fluorescein (10 mg) diluted in 10 ml of the patient's own CSF, injected slowly over about 10 minutes through a lumbar drain or needle, about 30 to 60 minutes before the endoscopic search
Consent | Off-label use; explain the rare risks
Avoid | Higher concentrations or doses; known fluorescein allergy
!!

# Using it in ENT | Practice.
## Technique
After anaesthesia, insert a lumbar drain (also useful post-operatively in some patients), withdraw CSF, dilute the fluorescein in it, and inject slowly. Head-down positioning and a Valsalva manoeuvre help the dye reach the leak. Fluorescein may take time to appear; a negative search does not exclude a leak. Other localisation tools are CT cisternography, high-resolution CT, MRI cisternography and beta-2 transferrin or beta-trace protein testing of nasal fluid.

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
Seizures, lower limb weakness and numbness, opisthotonus, cranial nerve deficits, and very rarely death, mostly with higher doses or concentrations; headache and nausea; transient yellow discoloration of skin and urine with systemic absorption.
