@stand Alginates react with gastric acid to form a viscous gel raft that floats on the stomach contents, reducing reflux of both acid and non-acid material, including pepsin, into the oesophagus and pharynx. Because pepsin and non-acid reflux are thought to matter in laryngopharyngeal reflux, an alginate after meals and at bedtime is a common first-line choice in UK ENT practice, alone or with a PPI.

# At a glance | The drugs on one page.
## Summary
!table Alginates
 | Summary
Agents | Sodium alginate with bicarbonate and calcium carbonate (for example Gaviscon Advance, Gaviscon Double Action)
How they work | Form a gel raft above the gastric contents that acts as a physical barrier; the raft is itself refluxed preferentially
ENT uses | Suspected laryngopharyngeal reflux; reflux symptoms in pregnancy; adjunct to PPIs
Adult dose | 10 to 20 ml (or 1 to 2 tablets chewed) after meals and at bedtime, up to four times daily
Child dose | Infant preparations are different products; do not use adult products in infants
Avoid | Severe renal impairment; caution with sodium restriction (heart failure, hypertension)
!!

# Using them in ENT | Practice.
## Laryngopharyngeal reflux
Lifestyle measures (avoid late meals, alcohol, smoking, carbonated drinks and trigger foods; lose weight; raise the head of the bed) and an alginate at bedtime are the first step; small trials suggest benefit, though the evidence base is limited. The bedtime dose protects during sleep, when laryngopharyngeal exposure is greatest.

# Safety | What goes wrong.
## Adverse effects
Generally minimal: bloating, constipation or diarrhoea; sodium and calcium loads.
## Interactions
Antacid components reduce absorption of some drugs (tetracyclines, quinolones, iron, levothyroxine, azoles): separate doses by about 2 hours.
