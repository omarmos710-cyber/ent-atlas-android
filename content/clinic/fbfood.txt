@stand A swallowed coin in a toddler, a fish bone in an adult's throat, a meat bolus in an elderly man, a denture after a seizure. Most swallowed objects pass harmlessly. The ENT surgeon's job is to find the ones that will not: those stuck in the pharynx or oesophagus, and those that are sharp, large, multiple magnets or button batteries. Remove them under direct vision before they ulcerate, perforate or block the airway. A button battery in the oesophagus is an emergency measured in hours.

# Where they lodge | Pharynx and oesophagus.
## The pharynx
- **Tonsil:** fish bones and needles, often in a crypt. Visible on examination and removed with forceps.
- **Tongue base and vallecula:** fish bones. Seen with a mirror or endoscope and removed under topical anaesthesia with curved forceps. A needle buried in the tongue base may need imaging and an open (pharyngotomy) approach, because it can migrate.
- **Posterior pharyngeal wall:** wires, pins and staples.
- **Pyriform fossa:** fish and chicken bones, needles and dentures. Small objects can be removed in the clinic. Large or impacted ones, and all in children, are removed under general anaesthesia.

## The oesophagus
- **Upper sphincter:** by far the commonest site is *at or just below the cricopharyngeus*, about 70%. Coins are held at the sphincter itself.
- **Other narrowings:** objects that pass may stop at the aortic and bronchial crossing (about 20%) or the cardia (about 10%).
- **Sharp objects** catch anywhere.
- **Lodgement below the cricopharyngeus in an adult** suggests an underlying stricture, ring, eosinophilic oesophagitis or cancer. Endoscope or image the oesophagus once the oedema settles.
## Who swallows things
- **Children:** about 80% are under five years old. Coins, toys, beads, *button batteries* and magnets.
- **Adults:**
  - Food boluses (meat), fish and chicken bones.
  - Dentures: an upper denture dulls palatal sensation.
  - Hasty eating, poor chewing, alcohol, sleep, seizures, unconsciousness.
  - Psychiatric illness, prisoners, and deliberate ingestion.
  - A *narrowed oesophagus*: a sudden bolus obstruction may be the first sign of a stricture or carcinoma.

# Assessment | History, signs, films.
## Symptoms
- **Choking or gagging** at the time.
- **Pain or a pricking feeling:** localised in the neck, often beside the trachea, and worse on swallowing. Pain localises well in the pharynx and cervical oesophagus but poorly lower down.
- **Dysphagia:** partial obstruction may become complete as oedema develops.
- **Drooling and inability to swallow saliva:** complete obstruction. There is a risk of aspiration.
- **Respiratory symptoms:**
  - Stridor or cough, especially in small children, from compression of the soft posterior tracheal wall or laryngeal oedema.
  - An object in the oesophagus of a child can present as wheeze or recurrent chest infection.
- **Warning symptoms:** chest, back or abdominal pain, fever and emphysema suggest perforation.
- **Silent cases:** some patients, especially children, have few symptoms. Normal eating and a normal X-ray do not exclude a foreign body.
## Signs
- Tenderness low in the neck beside the trachea (*Jackson sign*).
- Pain on rocking the larynx against the spine.
- *Pooling of saliva in the pyriform fossae* that does not clear with swallowing.
- The object itself, seen in the postcricoid region.
- Surgical emphysema, fever and tachycardia, which mean perforation.
## Imaging
- **Plain films:** posteroanterior and lateral of the neck, chest and abdomen (mouth to anus in children, to find all objects).
- **Coin orientation:**
  - A coin in the *oesophagus* lies *flat (en face) in the PA view* and edge-on in the lateral.
  - A coin in the *trachea* is the reverse, because the glottis is a sagittal slit. A tracheal coin lies edge-on in the PA view.
- **Button battery versus coin:** a battery shows a *double ring ("halo") on the PA view* and a *step-off* on the lateral. Look carefully at every "coin".
- **Radiolucent objects:** many fish bones, wood, plastic, aluminium and glass are invisible on X-ray. Indirect signs on the lateral neck film:
  - Air in the cervical oesophagus.
  - *Prevertebral soft-tissue thickening*.
  - Loss of the cervical lordosis.
  - Air in the tissues (perforation).
- **CT:** the most sensitive test for bones, suspected perforation and abscess. Use it freely when symptoms persist with a negative film.
- **Barium:** avoided. It coats the object and mucosa, can be aspirated, and makes endoscopy harder. A water-soluble study is used only if perforation is suspected and CT is not available.

# Removal | Under direct vision.
## Timing
- **Emergency, within hours:**
  - A *button battery in the oesophagus*.
  - Complete obstruction with drooling.
  - Airway compromise.
  - Sharp or pointed objects in the oesophagus.
  - Signs of perforation.
- **Urgent, within 24 hours:**
  - Other oesophageal foreign bodies, including coins and food boluses that do not pass.
  - Prolonged impaction increases ulceration, perforation and stricture.
- **Observation for a short period (12 to 24 hours with a repeat film):** a smooth coin in the lower oesophagus of a well child may pass into the stomach.
## Methods
- **Rigid oesophagoscopy under general anaesthesia:**
  - The traditional ENT approach, and the best for sharp or impacted objects and for objects at the cricopharyngeus.
  - Sharp points are drawn into the end of the scope and withdrawn together ("*ensheathing*"), and open safety pins are closed or turned so the point trails.
  - A hypopharyngoscope or a laryngoscope with a long blade suits objects at the upper sphincter.
- **Flexible endoscopy:** under sedation, for food boluses, coins and blunt objects. Usually done by gastroenterologists, with an overtube to protect the oesophagus from sharp objects. It also shows the underlying cause (eosinophilic oesophagitis, stricture, cancer), which should be biopsied.
- **Open surgery:**
  - *Cervical oesophagotomy* for impacted objects with hooks (partial dentures) above the thoracic inlet.
  - *Thoracotomy or thoracoscopy* for impacted lower objects and perforations.
!ops 45 | Oesophagoscopy and hypopharyngoscopy.
!flag Methods to avoid
- Blind removal with a Foley balloon catheter: the object can be aspirated as it is pulled into the pharynx.
- Pushing an object into the stomach blind.
- Meat tenderisers (papain): they can digest the oesophageal wall.
- Emetics.
- Glucagon, which relaxes the LES but does not help when there is a stricture or ring, and has little proven benefit.
!!
## After removal
- Re-examine the mucosa for ulceration, perforation and an underlying cause.
- Start oral fluids if the procedure was uneventful.
- Watch for pain, fever and emphysema, which mean perforation.
- Arrange follow-up endoscopy or a contrast study if a stricture or tumour is suspected.

# Special objects | Batteries, magnets, sharps.
## Button (disc) batteries
- **Mechanism:** an electrical current through the moist mucosa *hydrolyses tissue fluid and generates hydroxide at the negative pole*, causing alkaline (liquefactive) burns. Leakage of contents and pressure add to the damage.
- **Speed of injury:**
  - Mucosal injury within an hour.
  - Muscle injury within two to four hours.
  - Perforation within 8 to 12 hours.
  - Lithium cells of 20 mm or more are the most dangerous.
- **Late complications:**
  - Tracheo-oesophageal fistula.
  - Mediastinitis.
  - Stricture.
  - Vocal fold palsy.
  - *Aorto-oesophageal fistula with fatal haemorrhage*, sometimes days to weeks later.
- **Management:**
  - *In the oesophagus: immediate endoscopic removal*, at any hour.
  - Give honey (children over one year) or sucralfate on the way to theatre, if ingestion was within 12 hours, to coat and protect the mucosa. This should not delay removal.
  - Afterwards, CT or MRI for deep injury, and keep in hospital for observation if the injury is significant.
- **Once past the oesophagus:**
  - In the stomach, most pass.
  - Remove if symptomatic, if the battery is large in a young child, or if it is still in the stomach on a repeat film at about 48 hours (Dhingra: a child under six with a battery of 1.5 cm or more).
  - Batteries in the nose and ear are also emergencies.
!pearl The halo sign
Any round radio-opaque object in the oesophagus of a child is a button battery until a careful look excludes it. Look for the double contour (halo) on the frontal film and the step at the edge on the lateral. Remove it now, not on tomorrow's list.
!!
## Magnets
A single magnet usually passes. Two or more magnets, or a magnet with a metal object, attract each other across loops of bowel and cause pressure necrosis, fistulae and perforation. Remove them while they can be reached endoscopically, and seek early surgical advice if they have passed beyond.
## Sharp and long objects
- Pins, needles, bones, open safety pins and denture fragments with hooks carry the highest risk of perforation. They are removed from the oesophagus urgently.
- Beyond the stomach, sharp objects and long objects (over about 5 cm in a small child, 6 cm or more in adults) may not negotiate the duodenum. Remove them endoscopically if reachable, or follow them with daily films.
- Surgery is needed for pain, tenderness, failure to progress, or perforation.
## Objects beyond the stomach
- Most pass within a week on a normal diet. No purgatives.
- Check the stools and repeat films.
- Operate for:
  - Abdominal pain or tenderness.
  - An object that does not progress on serial films.
  - Sharp objects likely to perforate.
  - Long objects in small children.
  - Pyloric stenosis or other obstruction.
## Food bolus impaction
- **Approach:**
  - If the patient can swallow saliva and is comfortable, a short wait may allow it to pass.
  - If the patient cannot swallow saliva, or impaction lasts beyond 12 to 24 hours, remove it endoscopically.
  - At endoscopy, gently push it on if there is no stricture, or remove it piecemeal.
- **Afterwards:** almost all adults need investigation, usually *biopsy for eosinophilic oesophagitis* and a look for rings, strictures and cancer.

# Complications | Why we do not wait.
- **Airway obstruction:** laryngeal oedema, or tracheal compression in children.
- **Perforation:**
  - Leads to *retropharyngeal or parapharyngeal abscess* and *mediastinitis*.
  - Sharp objects can erode into the *aorta* (aorto-oesophageal fistula: a sentinel bleed, then exsanguination).
  - Can also cause pericarditis and empyema.
- **Periesophageal cellulitis and neck abscess.**
- **Tracheo-oesophageal fistula.**
- **Ulceration and stricture** from a neglected object.
- **Migration** of needles and bones into the neck, sometimes needing open exploration.
!table Pharyngeal and oesophageal foreign bodies at a glance
Situation | Action
Fish bone seen in tonsil or tongue base | Remove under topical anaesthesia with forceps
Persistent pricking with a normal examination | Flexible endoscopy; CT if still suspected; oesophagoscopy if seen or symptoms persist
Coin in the upper oesophagus | Rigid or flexible removal within 24 hours
Coin in the lower oesophagus, well child | Observe up to 24 hours; repeat film; remove if still there
Button battery in the oesophagus | Immediate removal; honey or sucralfate en route if it does not delay
Food bolus, cannot swallow saliva | Urgent endoscopy; biopsy for eosinophilic oesophagitis
Sharp object in the oesophagus | Urgent rigid oesophagoscopy, sheathing the point
Two or more magnets | Remove while reachable; surgical review

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Commonest site** (just below the cricopharyngeus) and the three narrowings.
- **Coin orientation:** oesophagus versus trachea.
- **Button battery:**
  - Halo sign.
  - Mechanism and timing of injury.
  - Immediate removal; honey or sucralfate.
- **Signs of an oesophageal foreign body** and of perforation.
- **Radiolucent objects;** why barium is avoided; the role of CT.
- **Rigid oesophagoscopy technique** for sharp objects.
- **What not to do:** Foley catheter, pushing down, papain.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Mistaking a button battery for a coin.
- Sending home a patient with a persistent foreign body sensation and a "normal X-ray".
- Using barium as the first study.
- Forgetting to look for an underlying stricture, eosinophilic oesophagitis or cancer in an adult with bolus impaction.
!!
