@stand Positioning is part of the operation. A good position brings the field to the surgeon, lowers venous pressure and blood loss, and keeps the airway safe; a bad one causes a stiff neck, a corneal abrasion, an ulnar palsy or a pressure sore that the patient remembers longer than the operation. The surgeon is responsible for the final position, and in ENT, where the head is turned, extended and covered, the team must check the tube, the eyes and the limbs again after every change.
@src Oxford Textbook of Otolaryngology; Oxford Handbook of ENT; K. J. Lee; Myers; NICE and AAGBI guidance; the literature

# Principles | What a good position does.
## The aims {day}
- **Access:** the field square to the surgeon's line of sight and within easy reach.
- **Less bleeding:** the head above the heart lowers venous pressure in the field.
- **A safe airway:** the tube secure and visible, with no kinking when the head turns.
- **No injury:** eyes protected, nerves and pressure points padded, joints within their normal range.
## Who does what {day}
The anaesthetic team controls the head and airway during moves; the surgeon decides the final position and checks it before draping; everyone checks pressure points. Move the patient with enough hands and a slide sheet, and say "ready, steady, move" so all move together.

# The positions | Operation by operation.
## Neck extended with a shoulder roll {day}
For thyroid, parathyroid, tracheostomy, laryngectomy and most neck operations. A sandbag or roll under the shoulders and a head ring let the neck extend and bring the trachea forward. Extend only as far as is comfortable; over-extension strains the cervical spine and can cause post-operative neck pain.
!danger The unstable neck
Rheumatoid arthritis, Down syndrome (atlantoaxial instability), ankylosing spondylitis, previous cervical fusion and trauma all limit how far the neck can be moved safely. Ask, examine the range of movement while the patient is awake, and position within it.
!!
## Head up (reverse Trendelenburg) {day}
For endoscopic sinus surgery, skull base surgery and thyroid surgery. Tilting the whole table 15 to 20 degrees head up reduces venous pressure in the field and improves the endoscopic view. The head is often turned slightly towards the surgeon. Watch blood pressure: tilting reduces venous return and can drop it.
## Head turned for ear and parotid surgery {day}
Turn the head away from the operated side on a head ring, ideally with the body slightly tilted or a wedge under the shoulder so that the neck itself is not twisted to its limit. Check the neck's comfortable range of rotation before induction. The ear should be horizontal and uppermost, with the surgeon seated and the microscope easily brought in.
## Tonsils, adenoids and the mouth {day}
Supine with a shoulder roll and the neck extended (the Rose position), and a mouth gag (Boyle-Davis) inserted with the tongue blade in the midline, opened gently and suspended on Draffin rods or a stand. Check that the gag has not kinked or dislodged the tube, that the lips and teeth are clear of the blade, and that the eyes are protected.
#!ph boyle_davis | A Boyle-Davis mouth gag. Check the tube under the tongue blade every time it is opened or moved.
## Microlaryngoscopy and rigid endoscopy {day}
Supine with the head on a ring, the neck flexed on the chest and the head extended at the atlanto-occipital joint (the "sniffing" position) to line up the mouth, pharynx and larynx. A tooth guard protects the upper teeth. Suspend the laryngoscope on a chest support or table bracket, never on the chest wall directly in a small child.
## Arms, eyes and pressure points {day}
- **Arms:** tucked at the sides with the palms facing the body, or on arm boards abducted less than 90 degrees to protect the brachial plexus; pad the elbows to protect the ulnar nerve.
- **Eyes:** taped closed, padded if out of the field, and never pressed on by the surgeon's hands or instruments.
- **Pressure points:** occiput, sacrum, heels and elbows; use gel pads for long cases.
- **Legs:** uncrossed; calf compression devices for VTE prophylaxis in adults.
!senior Senior's pearl
Before the drapes go on, look at the patient from the foot of the table: is the head straight, is the tube free, are the eyes covered, are the arms in a safe position? Once draped, nobody sees these again until the end.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Injuries and how to avoid them.
## Injuries from positioning {junior}
!table Positioning injuries
Injury | Cause | Prevention
Corneal abrasion | Open eye, drape or hand pressure, chlorhexidine | Tape, lubricate, pad; aqueous preparation near the eye
Ulnar neuropathy | Pressure at the elbow, pronated forearm | Pad the elbow, supinate or neutral forearm
Brachial plexus injury | Arm abducted over 90 degrees, head turned away | Limit abduction, avoid combined extremes
Neck pain, cervical injury | Over-extension or rotation | Stay within the awake range
Pressure sores | Long operations, thin patients | Gel pads, check heels and occiput
Dental injury | Gags, laryngoscopes | Tooth guard; note loose or crowned teeth before

# Evidence | What the studies show.
## Current evidence
!evidence
In a randomised trial of endoscopic sinus surgery, a 15 degree head-up position gave a better surgical field and less blood loss than the flat position (Hathorn et al., *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2013). Another trial compared 5, 10 and 20 degrees and found the best field and lowest blood loss at 20 degrees (Gan et al., *Int Forum Allergy Rhinol* 2014).

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Turning the head beyond its awake range.
- A gag that kinks the tube.
- Arms abducted beyond 90 degrees.
- Forgetting the eyes once the drapes are on.
!!
