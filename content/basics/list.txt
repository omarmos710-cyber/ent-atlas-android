@stand Running an operating list well is a senior skill that nobody formally teaches. A good list starts on time, finishes on time, does the right cases in the right order, has every piece of equipment and every bed ready, trains the junior, and sends patients home safely. A bad one cancels patients on the day, overruns, and pushes tired teams into mistakes. Learn it by watching good consultants plan, and by planning lists yourself under supervision before you are the one responsible.
@src Royal College of Surgeons Good Surgical Practice; Getting It Right First Time (ENT); Centre for Perioperative Care; the literature

# Planning | The week before.
## Building the list {senior}
- **Estimate time honestly** for each case, including anaesthesia and turnover, from your own data, not from hope.
- **Mix cases** so the list fits the session and so a long case does not push day cases past the time they can go home.
- **Book what each case needs:** microscope, laser and laser-trained staff, nerve monitor, navigation, drill, Coblation, frozen section, blood, implants, interpreters, high-dependency or intensive care beds, and the right assistant.
- **Confirm the patients are ready:** pre-assessment done, anticoagulants stopped, tests reviewed, consent discussed.
## Ordering the cases {senior}
!table Common principles for ordering a list
Put first | Why
Children (and youngest first) | Shorter fasting, less distress
Patients with diabetes | Shorter fasting, less disruption of insulin
Latex allergy | Latex-free room before any exposure
Major cases needing beds or critical care | Time to recover and to manage problems in working hours
Day cases that need time to recover before discharge | Enough time to go home the same day
Put last |
Known infection (MRSA, active infection) | Cleaning after the case
!!
Local practice varies; agree the order with the anaesthetist and the theatre team, and do not change it on the day without telling them.

# On the day | Running the list.
## Starting well {senior}
Arrive early, see every patient, confirm consent and marking, and lead the team brief with a realistic plan for the day. Make decisions about trainees early: which case each will do, and which parts.
## Keeping time {senior}
- Overlapping tasks: the next patient sent for while the current one is being closed, the junior closing while you consent the next.
- Avoid unnecessary delays: missing equipment, unchecked consent, unmarked sites, waiting for notes.
- If the list is going to overrun, decide early with the team which case to cancel and tell the patient yourself. Never start a major case that will clearly run into the night without a reason.
!senior Senior's pearl
A list that finishes on time is not the one where everyone hurries; it is the one where nothing is missing at the start of each case. Planning saves more minutes than speed.
!!
## Supervising trainees {senior}
Match the case to the trainee's level, agree the parts they will do, give them the time to do it safely (a list with trainees runs slower, so plan for it), take over when patient safety needs it, and give feedback at the end. Leave the theatre only when you trust the trainee with what remains and you are immediately available.

# Emergencies | Fitting them in.
## The emergency patient {senior}
Book emergency cases according to clinical urgency categories: immediate (life-threatening: airway obstruction, bleeding), urgent (within hours: abscess, foreign body), and expedited (within days). Communicate with the emergency theatre coordinator, and do not let elective work delay a patient who needs an airway or bleeding controlled.

# Evidence | What the studies show.
## Current evidence
!evidence
A meta-analysis of 33 studies (306,635 patients) found about 18% of operations worldwide are cancelled on the intended day, mostly for avoidable reasons such as lack of theatre time or facilities, patients not attending, and changes in medical condition (Abate et al., *Int J Surg Open* 2020). A scoping review of 129 studies in otolaryngology identified ways to improve theatre efficiency, including standardised equipment and teams, local anaesthesia and laryngeal masks where suitable, procedures outside the main theatre, and better scheduling (Hathi et al., *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2024).
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Optimistic timings.
- Equipment not booked.
- Starting a long case late in the day without a reason.
- Not planning trainee cases.
!!
