@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand Complete maxillectomy removes the whole maxilla, from the orbital floor to the palate and from the nasal cavity to the pterygoid plates, for tumours that arise within and fill the maxillary sinus. Squamous cell carcinoma is the commonest malignancy there, adenocarcinomas next. Surgery is the backbone of treatment, often followed by radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, except for lymphoma and plasmacytoma, which are treated without surgery. The operation is a sequence of osteotomies that free the maxilla on every side until only the pterygoid plates hold it, and the last cut, behind the plates, is where the internal maxillary artery bleeds and where a misplaced osteotome fractures through the pterygopalatine fossa and leaves tumour behind. Margins are everything and often difficult, beside the orbit, the optic nerve and the carotid. This chapter covers the family of maxillectomies, assessment with CT and MRI, Ohngren's line, airway and reconstructive planning, the operation step by step, and the obturator.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Complete maxillectomy
 | Summary
Types | **Infrastructure** (oral cavity tumours involving the sinus); **suprastructure** (facial skin tumours without palatal involvement, rarely lacrimal or orbital); **medial** (nasal tumours of the lateral wall, such as inverted papilloma); **complete** (tumours filling the sinus); complete with **orbital exenteration**; **extended** (with facial skin, anterior skull base or infratemporal fossa)
Indications | Malignant tumours; symptomatic or growing benign tumours (no wide margins needed); symptom relief
Contraindications | Unfit for anaesthesia or major blood loss; unresectable tumour (unless for palliation); diffuse distant metastasis; **lymphoma, plasmacytoma** and other highly radio- or chemosensitive tumours
Airway | Usually oral intubation and extubation; awake or elective tracheostomy for severe trismus, large pharyngeal or soft palate resections, or free flap palate reconstruction
Anaesthesia | General; paralysis acceptable unless a parotidectomy or neck dissection is added; normal blood pressure, no planned hypotension; careful fluids; tube taped to the opposite side
Position | Table turned 180 degrees (or just past 90 toward the operated side); tarsorrhaphy; neck and thigh prepared; **povidone-iodine** around the eyes, washed out with balanced salt solution at the end
Antibiotics | First- or second-generation cephalosporin or a penicillin 30 minutes before incision and for 24 hours; clindamycin in allergy
Tray | Soft tissue and neck dissection sets; small bone saws; 1 mm side-cutting pencil drill; chisels; dental extraction set; oral retractors; monopolar and bipolar diathermy; 0 and 30 degree endoscopes and tower

## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to remove the cancer together with the whole upper jaw on that side.
- **The palate opening** is closed with an obturator (a plate like a denture) made beforehand, or rebuilt with tissue from elsewhere.
- **The eye:** it may sink (hypoglobus) or see double; vision loss is rare. If the cancer involves the eye more than the scans show, its removal may be needed: tell us your wishes in advance.
- **Numbness of the cheek** (the infraorbital nerve is usually taken), changes in speech and swallowing, lip competence.
- **Bleeding**, wound problems, and failure of a free flap if one is used.
- **A neck operation** if nodes are found when the neck is opened for a free flap.
- **Radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy** usually follows for cancer.
- **Jaw stiffness** (trismus): exercises start early.
!!
## Before you start
Patients present with nasal congestion, a palatal or alveolar lesion, epistaxis, facial numbness, tearing, diplopia or visual loss. Nodal metastasis is under 10% at presentation overall, but up to **35%** for squamous cancer of the alveolus and hard palate (usually levels I and II and the facial nodes). Trismus, airway compromise, blindness, ophthalmoplegia and lower cranial nerve palsies mean advanced disease.
!senior What the examination tells you
- **Loss of palatal sensation:** perineural spread into the palatine canals or the pterygopalatine fossa.
- **Trismus:** infratemporal fossa invasion.
- **Hyperglobus** may be expansion, not invasion; **severe muscle restriction and visual loss** almost always mean intraconal invasion.
- **Tearing:** lacrimal invasion, or pterygopalatine invasion disrupting the vidian supply.
- **Skin change:** breach of the anterior wall, advanced disease with reconstructive consequences.
!!
Examine the teeth, palate, buccal mucosa and soft palate movement; cranial nerves III to VI, eye movements and infraorbital sensation; the neck. Endoscope the nasal floor, the lateral wall and the middle meatus for ethmoid involvement, and biopsy intranasal tumour.
## Imaging
CT and MRI are complementary and both essential. **CT** shows the bony boundaries and invasion of the palate, posterior wall and orbital floor; **MRI** shows the soft tissue, perineural spread, orbit, skull base, intracranial extension and facial skin. Stage the neck and chest with contrast CT or PET-CT. Angiography only for a suspected vascular malformation or carotid invasion.
!mf 1 | (A) Coronal CT: destruction of the medial maxillary wall and ethmoids, with little information on orbital or intracranial spread. (B) Coronal MRI of the same patient showing the full orbital and intracranial extent.
!mf 2 | Ohngren's line, from the medial canthus to the angle of the mandible: tumours below and in front of it do better than those above and behind.
## Preparation
Stage fully and present at a multidisciplinary meeting. Plan reconstruction: a **prosthodontist** for the obturator, a **microvascular surgeon** if a free flap is considered. Discuss the "what ifs" and record the patient's wishes (more orbital involvement than imaging shows, facial skin involvement, positive nodes found in the neck) so decisions in theatre can be made quickly. Check blood count, coagulation and biochemistry, and group and screen. Plan the airway with the anaesthetist. Embolisation is rarely needed, only for vascular tumours such as AVMs or angiofibromas: the internal maxillary artery is the main supply, with the inferolateral trunk of the carotid also feeding the infratemporal fossa. Carotid sacrifice has virtually no place in high-grade epithelial malignancy.

# Anatomy you must see | Freeing the maxilla.
## The attachments to cut
The maxilla is attached at the **nasal cavity** (piriform aperture and medial wall), the **orbit** (rim and floor), the **zygoma** (malar process or arch, and the lateral buttress), the **palate**, and finally the **pterygoid plates**. Behind the plates lie the **pterygoid muscles**, the **internal maxillary artery** and, at the skull base, **V3**; V2 leaves through the infraorbital foramen and is followed for a neural margin.
## Margins
En bloc resection with clear margins is the principle, and bony margins should be **at least 1 to 1.5 cm** from tumour. It is hard to achieve along the periorbita, or when tumour reaches the orbital apex or the infratemporal fossa.

# The operation, stage by stage | Osteotomy by osteotomy.
## Choose the incision
Take the incisions down to bone with diathermy if the anterior soft tissue is not invaded: a sublabial incision alone; sublabial with partial or full degloving; a **lateral rhinotomy**, which the author places at the junction of the nasal dorsum and lateral wall rather than in the nasofacial crease (better appearance and less contact with the airway); or an extended lateral rhinotomy with a **lip split** (with a dart against notching) or a **Weber-Ferguson** periocular extension at the infraorbital rim in the elderly, or subciliary or transconjunctival in the young.
!mf 3 | Incisions: (A, C) the lateral rhinotomy at the dorsum-sidewall junction rather than the nasolabial crease; (B) with a lip split and dart; (D) the Weber-Ferguson extension.
!see What you should see
- An incision that gives the whole anterior maxilla, with the lip marks aligned if it is split.
!!
## Expose and take V2
Expose the whole anterior face of the midface, the inferior and lateral orbit and the buccal space. Dissect out **V2** and take neural margins. Incise along the piriform aperture into the nose and retract the nose to the other side.
!see What you should see
- The anterior maxilla, the infraorbital rim and nerve, and the buccal fat laterally.
!!
## The palate
Make the palatal incisions and take intraoral margins. If the soft palate is kept, free it from the hard palate to open into the nasopharynx; control the palatine vessels with bipolar.
!see What you should see
- Palatal cuts through mucosa to bone with a margin, and the soft palate freed if it is staying.
!!
## The orbit
Elevate the periorbita from the rim and floor, and examine them closely for invasion.
!see What you should see
- Clean periorbita that lifts easily off the floor: no tumour through it.
!!
## Anterior and orbital osteotomies
With a **1 mm side-cutting pencil drill** and copious irrigation, cut from the upper piriform aperture through the orbital rim, protecting the eye with a metal retractor. Lift the globe and join the medial and lateral orbital rim cuts with a thin sharp osteotome, running back into the orbit to free the orbital floor.
!see What you should see
- The cut through the rim and floor with the globe held up and protected.
!!
## The lateral osteotomy
With the same drill, cut from the lateral orbit through the rim and across the malar process (occasionally the arch), back toward the pterygoid plates and through the **lateral buttress**.
!see What you should see
- The cut across the malar buttress heading for the pterygomaxillary junction.
!!
## The palatal osteotomy
With a thin reciprocating saw, cut the palate from **back to front** and continue into the front of the nose.
!see What you should see
- The palate divided from back to front, between tooth sockets where possible.
!!
## The pterygoid plates: from the side
Now only the plates hold the maxilla. The traditional curved osteotome behind the plates, angled up into the infratemporal fossa, gives no proximal control of the internal maxillary artery and can fracture through the **pterygopalatine fossa** instead of behind the plates. So work **from the lateral side**: retract the mandible down and back, find the plates and muscles by blunt dissection, and feel (and if needed clamp) the **internal maxillary artery**. Feel the fused plates at the base of the middle cranial fossa, fracture them from the sphenoid with a sharp straight osteotome, and rock the maxilla medially and down.
!see What you should see
- The plates divided under vision with the internal maxillary artery clipped, and the maxilla rocking free.
!!
!danger The last cut
Incomplete removal of the posterior wall and pterygoid plates is a common error; a fracture through the pterygopalatine fossa leaves tumour behind and opens a bleeding artery you cannot reach. Control the artery from the side.
!!
## Free and deliver
With the infratemporal fossa open, control the internal maxillary artery fully and strip the pterygoid muscles from bone with bipolar. Find **V3** and take skull base neural margins if needed. Cut the rest of the medial maxillary wall with large curved scissors, back from the medial orbital osteotomy, across the duct and down toward the torus tubarius, and remove the specimen. Send soft tissue margins for frozen section and secure haemostasis.
!see What you should see
- A clean cavity from orbital floor to palate, the pterygoid stumps behind, the infratemporal fossa dry, and the orbit supported by its periorbita.
!!
## Graft, pack and obturate
Lay a **medium-thickness split skin graft** from the thigh over the exposed buccal tissue and drape it back into the infratemporal defect. Pack with gauze and fix the **temporary obturator** with wires and screws. Close the face in layers, carefully realigning orbicularis oris and oculi. If a free flap with a bony palate is used, **remove the coronoid process** routinely so the mandible cannot fuse to the new palate.
!mf 4 | Healed defect and obturator: (A) five years after a Weber-Ferguson incision; (B) the defect with the obturator out; (C) the obturator; (D) in place.
!see What you should see
- The graft lining the cavity, the obturator seated firmly, and the lip and lids realigned.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Loss of vision or diplopia after surgery
Act | emergency ophthalmology review: pressures, acuity, fields, eye movements
Raised pressure (over 30) with falling acuity | emergency lateral canthotomy and cantholysis
Then | imaging for retrobulbar haematoma ; plan decompression
!!
!path Bleeding behind the plates
Prevent | identify and control the internal maxillary artery from the lateral approach before the final fracture
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Bony margins that are too small or too superficial (less than 1 to 1.5 cm).
- Incomplete removal of the posterior wall and pterygoid plates.
- Underestimating the difficulty of clearing periorbital or ethmoid disease.
- Leaving the coronoid when a bony free flap rebuilds the palate.
!!

# Aftercare | Eye, wound, mouth opening.
## After the operation
Fastidious wound and eye care. Clear fluids, or nil by mouth with a nasogastric tube after a free flap. Early mobilisation and routine **DVT prophylaxis**. Start **trismus exercises early**, because the muscles of mastication scar as they heal.

Complication | Notes

Corneal ulceration | Tarsorrhaphy and eye care
Bleeding or haematoma | Internal maxillary artery
Vision loss or diplopia | Emergency ophthalmology; canthotomy for raised pressure with falling acuity
Facial numbness | Beyond V2, which is expected
Carotid injury | Rare, in the parapharyngeal region

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Do margins matter?
Complete removal is a central principle, but near the carotid and optic nerve it may not be possible: microscopic residual disease has been reported in **30%** of open and **24%** of endoscopic resections. Residual gross disease clearly causes recurrence and death; the effect of microscopic margins was long equivocal, but the International Collaborative Study Group found margin status a critical predictor of overall, recurrence-free and disease-specific survival, and Manjunath and colleagues found more regional and distant recurrence with positive margins. Margins must be taken in three dimensions, from bone, nerve and soft tissue, and discussed frankly with the patient.
