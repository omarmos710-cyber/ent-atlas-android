@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand Medialisation laryngoplasty, Isshiki's type I thyroplasty, moves a paralysed or deficient vocal fold to the midline from outside the larynx, through a small window in the thyroid cartilage, so that the mobile fold can meet it. The patient is awake under light sedation and talks to you throughout, so you can tune the result by ear and by flexible endoscope in real time. Medialisation fills out the membranous fold, but neither it nor injection can close a large gap at the back of the glottis; for that you add an arytenoid adduction, a suture through the muscular process that does the work of the lateral cricoarytenoid, rotating the arytenoid, closing the posterior gap, lengthening the fold and correcting a height mismatch. This chapter covers selection, the window and why the muscular tubercle decides where it goes, building the implant pocket while listening to the voice, the posterior approach to the arytenoid past the pyriform sinus, and the errors (too anterior, too superior, too little) that bring patients back.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Medialisation laryngoplasty and arytenoid adduction
 | Summary
Indications | Symptomatic **glottic insufficiency** (dysphonia, breathlessness on speaking, aspiration) from paralysis, paresis, atrophy (presbylarynx) or scar, especially when movement has little or no chance of returning; soft tissue loss of the fold after surgery or trauma
Add arytenoid adduction | A **large posterior glottic gap**, a foreshortened (slack) fold, or a significant **vertical height mismatch**; it is the only operation that consistently closes the posterior glottis
Contraindications | Poor abduction of the other fold (airway); malignancy involving the laryngotracheal complex; lesions of the vocal fold; coagulopathy (relative); intact movement of the fold (for arytenoid adduction)
Anaesthesia | **Monitored anaesthesia care**: local anaesthetic with light sedation and analgesia, so the patient can phonate; nebulised topical anaesthetic beforehand; glycopyrrolate to dry secretions; dexamethasone 10 mg
Position | Supine with a modest shoulder roll to extend the neck; make the patient comfortable
Antibiotics | One prophylactic intravenous dose 30 to 45 minutes before incision
Tray | Head and neck soft tissue set; Cottle or Freer elevator; caliper; drill with a 1 to 2 mm cutting bur; flexible laryngoscope with C-mount camera and monitor; the implant. Gore-Tex: middle ear instruments (alligator, annulus elevator, curette), Woodson elevator, 1 to 3 mm Kerrison rongeurs. Silastic: Netterville medialisation set and Silastic block. Arytenoid adduction: 4-0 permanent monofilament suture and a Kitner (peanut) dissector
Implant | Silastic, hydroxylapatite or Gore-Tex: any can work; the choice and the shaping are personal to the surgeon
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to move the weak vocal cord to the middle so the cords meet, for a stronger voice and safer swallowing.
- **You will be awake** and asked to speak during the operation: this is how the result is fine-tuned.
- **The other side or an extra procedure:** both sides, or an arytenoid adduction, may be needed depending on what is found on the day.
- **The voice will sound slightly strained at the end,** because of swelling; it settles over 2 to 3 weeks and is judged at about 4 weeks.
- **Revision** may be needed for a persisting gap at the back, too little medialisation, an implant in the wrong place or a height mismatch.
- **Breathing difficulty** from swelling, bleeding or a haematoma.
- **Implant problems:** movement, or extrusion into the airway (an emergency) or through the skin.
- **Infection**, and with arytenoid adduction a **leak from the throat into the neck** (fistula).
- A scar in a skin crease of the neck.
!!
## Before you start
Ask how the hoarseness began, suddenly or slowly, and what came before it: surgery, intubation, an upper respiratory infection. The voice is typically weak, breathy and effortful; ask about breathlessness on speaking, coughing and choking, especially with liquids, and aspiration pneumonia. In the past history look for neck surgery (thyroid, parathyroid, carotid), cervical spine or thoracic and mediastinal surgery, malignancy (head and neck, thyroid, oesophagus, lung), stroke, and lung disease.
!senior Little reserve
A patient with COPD or other lung disease has little pulmonary reserve: aspiration from a paralysed fold hurts them sooner, and they need earlier intervention.
!!
Examine the head and neck and the cranial nerves. **Transnasal flexible laryngoscopy** shows the movement of both folds, above all the abduction of the other one, and the airway; **stroboscopy** shows the closure pattern and whether the folds are level. When paralysis has no clear cause, image with contrast-enhanced CT or MRI from the **skull base to the aortic arch**, along the whole course of the recurrent laryngeal nerve.

Stop anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs (NSAIDs, warfarin, clopidogrel, factor Xa inhibitors) in agreement with the cardiologist or haematologist. Prepare the patient psychologically for an operation on the neck while awake.

# Anatomy you must see | The tubercle and the grain of rice.
## For medialisation
- **The inferior border of the thyroid ala** sets the plane of the vocal fold, and the window is placed in relation to it.
- **The whole muscular tubercle** on the inferior border of the ala: its angulation can tilt your idea of the true lower border and misorient the window, so you must expose about 5 mm behind it to see it all.
- **The window site:** about 5 to 7 mm behind the midline of the thyroid cartilage and 3 mm above its inferior border, immediately lateral to the vocal fold.
!mf 1 | The site of the medialisation laryngoplasty window, 3 mm above the inferior border of the thyroid ala.
## For arytenoid adduction
- **The posterior border of the thyroid ala** and its inferior cornu.
- **The cricopharyngeus,** attached to the inferior cornu.
- **The pyriform sinus,** lying directly over the posterior lamina of the cricoid.
- **The posterior cricoarytenoid muscle** on the posterior cricoid lamina, its fibres running from medial and inferior to lateral and superior, converging on
- **the muscular process of the arytenoid,** a small ridge that feels and looks like **a grain of rice**.

# The operation, stage by stage | Medialisation laryngoplasty.
## Anaesthetise and sedate
Before the patient comes into theatre, nebulise a 50:50 mixture of **4% lidocaine (2 mg/kg) and 1% tetracaine (0.5 mg/kg)** to anaesthetise the upper aerodigestive tract so that the patient tolerates the flexible scope. Give glycopyrrolate unless contraindicated, and one dose of dexamethasone. Pack the more open side of the nose with pledgets of 4% lidocaine and oxymetazoline. Once sedated, inject 1% lidocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline superficially and deep over the thyroid ala on the side or sides of surgery. If you plan an arytenoid adduction, add a **superior laryngeal nerve block** in the thyrohyoid space.
!see What you should see
- A calm, breathing patient who can still speak, and a clear view of the folds on the flexible scope.
!!
## Incise and expose the cartilage
Make a **4 to 5 cm curved incision in a skin crease near the inferior border of the thyroid ala** on the operative side, crossing the midline by about 1 cm. Divide the platysma and raise subplatysmal flaps up to the hyoid and down to the cricoid. Divide the midline raphe between the strap muscles and hold the skin and straps apart with two self-retaining retractors or fishhooks.
!see What you should see
- The thyroid cartilage from the notch to its inferior border, both alae exposed in the midline.
!!
## Raise the outer perichondrium and find the tubercle
Incise the outer perichondrium along the midline and elevate it widely, subperichondrially, back to about **5 mm behind the muscular tubercle**. Only when the posterior part of the tubercle is seen can you identify the true inferior border of the cartilage.
!see What you should see
- The bare thyroid ala from the midline back to behind the muscular tubercle, with its true lower border.
!!
!danger A misplaced window
If the tubercle is not fully exposed, its angulation misleads you about the true inferior border, the window is set at the wrong angle or height, and the implant medialises the wrong part of the fold.
!!
## Mark and confirm the window
With the caliper, mark the site 5 to 7 mm behind the midline and 3 mm above the inferior border. Pass a **25 G needle** through the cartilage there and watch it on the flexible scope: it should enter immediately lateral to the vocal fold, not too far back, forward or up. If the ala is too ossified for the needle, drill a small hole with a 1 to 2 mm cutting bur and pass a blunt instrument such as an annulus elevator through it instead.
!see What you should see
- On the scope, the needle tenting the tissue just lateral to the fold, at the level of its free edge.
!!
## Cut the window
For **Gore-Tex**, a **3 × 3 mm** window, with the drill in ossified cartilage or a No. 15 or Beaver blade. For **Silastic**, typically **6 × 13 mm**, with drill or scalpel. Remove the cartilage from the window.
!see What you should see
- A clean window with the inner perichondrium intact beneath it.
!!
## Make the pocket while the patient speaks
Incise the inner perichondrium above, behind and below to leave an **anteriorly based flap**, and elevate the paraglottic space backward and downward for the implant. Watch the flexible scope and listen to the voice as you go: they tell you the size, position and kind of implant the fold needs, and this step decides the success of the operation.
!see What you should see
- The fold moving to the midline along its whole length on the scope as you press, with the voice clearing.
!!
!danger The two commonest mistakes
**Overmedialising anteriorly** gives a pressed, strained voice; **placing the implant too superiorly** pushes the ventricle and the false fold medially and impairs phonation. Keep the bulk where the fold needs it.
!!
!senior Keep within the perichondrium
With a Gore-Tex strip, keep the dissection inside the inner perichondrium where the implant will lie. Wide undermining lets it migrate to a less favourable position.
!!
## Insert and tune the implant
Insert the implant through the window while watching the scope and listening to the voice. Adjust it until the fold is medialised and the glottis closes. **Slightly overmedialise**: the fold swells during the operation and the swelling will go down.
!mf 2 | Gore-Tex placed into the paraglottic space through the window, medialising the vocal fold.
!see What you should see
- **A slightly strained voice, a medialised fold and complete glottic closure** on the screen: the intended end point.
!!
!danger Entering the airway
Unrecognised breach of the mucosa, usually of the ventricle, while placing the implant is thought to be the cause of later extrusion into the airway, a dangerous complication. Work in the paraglottic space and look for any breach.
!!
## Secure and close
Fix the implant to the adjacent cartilage with a permanent **4-0 Prolene** suture. Secure haemostasis; a small drain in the dependent part of the wound is optional. Lay the perichondrium back over the window, bring the strap muscles together in the midline, and close the skin in two layers, dermal and epidermal, for the best scar.
!see What you should see
- The fold straight in the midline on the scope after the implant is fixed, with no bulge above it.
!!

# The operation: arytenoid adduction | Rotating the arytenoid from behind.
## Extend the exposure
Use the same approach, then divide about **1 cm of the medial sternohyoid** just below its insertion on the hyoid to open the posterior view. Continue the subperichondrial elevation from the front two thirds of the ala to its posterior border and skeletonise the inferior cornu.
!see What you should see
- The posterior border of the ala and the inferior cornu.
!!
## Free the back of the larynx
Dissect around the posterior border of the ala from the inferior cornu up toward the greater cornu, **keeping on the cartilage** so as not to enter the pyriform sinus. Rotate the larynx medially with a hook on the greater cornu to bring its back into view. Release the upper part of the **cricopharyngeus** from the inferior cornu with bipolar diathermy to expose the pyriform sinus.
!see What you should see
- The back of the cricoid coming into view as the larynx rotates, with the pyriform mucosa intact and bulging when the patient puffs.
!!
!senior Puff out your cheeks
Ask the awake patient to puff out the cheeks from time to time: the pyriform sinus inflates and shows you exactly where its walls are.
!!
## Lift the pyriform sinus and find the muscle
The pyriform sinus lies directly over the posterior cricoid lamina and the posterior cricoarytenoid muscle, so it is your guide. Dissect it off the cricoid from **inferolateral to superomedial** until the posterior cricoarytenoid appears, recognised by fibres running from medial-inferior to lateral-superior. Retract the lower part of the sinus and follow the muscle up to the ridge where its fibres end: the **muscular process**.
!mf 3 | Identifying the muscular process of the arytenoid.
!see What you should see
- Muscle fibres of the posterior cricoarytenoid converging on a small bony knob, the muscular process.
!!
!danger The pyriform sinus
A breach of the pyriform sinus leads to a pharyngocutaneous fistula. If you see one, oversew it carefully at once.
!!
## Open a posterior window if needed
If the muscular process is hard to reach, remove a small piece of the posterior border of the thyroid cartilage with a rongeur, a posterior thyroplasty window, to help you find it and pass the suture.
!mf 4 | Removing a small part of the posterior border of the thyroid cartilage with a rongeur to help find and suture the muscular process.
!see What you should see
- The muscular process through the gap in the posterior border.
!!
## Place the adduction suture
Pass a **figure-of-eight 4-0 non-absorbable suture** (such as Mersilene) through the muscular process. Pull gently and confirm on the flexible scope that you control the arytenoid. If the medialisation window is not yet cut, clamp the two arms, let the larynx return to its position, and make the window as above.
!see What you should see
- The arytenoid rotating inward and the posterior fold closing when the suture is tensioned.
!!
## Bring the suture forward
Through the window, develop a track with an elevator in the paraglottic space from the muscular process to the window. Pass a middle ear alligator back along it and draw both arms forward through the window. Pass one arm through the cricothyroid membrane in front of and below the window, and the other through the thyroid cartilage just in front of the window.
!see What you should see
- Both suture arms emerging at the front, running freely when pulled.
!!
!danger A blocked track
Without a clear track from the muscular process to the window, the arytenoid will not rotate properly however you tension the suture.
!!
## Tension gently, then medialise
With gentle tension on the sutures and a simulated medialisation through the window, judge the voice and the fold on the scope to set the tension and the amount of medialisation. **Little tension is needed**: the suture only guides the arytenoid into a good position and holds it there; excessive tension over-rotates it and spoils the voice. When combined with adduction, the fold usually needs less medialisation. Tie the suture, complete the medialisation laryngoplasty, place a small dependent drain and close as before.
!see What you should see
- On the scope, the vocal process swinging medially and slightly down, with the fold lengthening.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Voice only marginally better after the implant
Look | is there a posterior gap, a height mismatch or a short, slack fold on the scope?
Posterior gap or mismatch | add an arytenoid adduction now (consented beforehand)
Implant too anterior or superior | reposition: the bulk should lie at the level of the fold, not the false fold
Other side | consider augmenting the opposite fold if it too is deficient
!!
!path Pyriform sinus opened
Confirm | look for the mucosal breach, puffing the cheeks
Repair | oversew carefully at once
Afterwards | watch the wound for a pharyngocutaneous fistula
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Operating when the airway is already compromised by poor abduction of the other fold.
- Placing the implant too superiorly or too anteriorly.
- Breaching the airway with the implant, or the pyriform sinus during the posterior dissection.
- No clear track for the adduction suture.
- Too much tension on the adduction suture.
!!

# Aftercare | Judge it at four weeks.
## After the operation
Medialisation is usually a day case; arytenoid adduction often means a 23-hour stay, depending on comorbidity. Give analgesia; if observed overnight, intravenous dexamethasone 6 to 8 mg every 8 hours for 24 hours; after arytenoid adduction, a 6-day tapering course of methylprednisolone. Sit the patient up. **No voice rest** and no routine antibiotics. Review in clinic at about **4 weeks**: oedema of the fold and surrounding tissues takes 3 to 4 weeks to settle, and an earlier examination is not a valid judge of the result.
## Complications
!table Complications
Complication | Notes
Implant malposition | Commonest: **too much medialisation** and **too superior a position**. Superior placement prolapses the ventricle and medialises the false fold; anterior overmedialisation gives a pressed, strained voice
Undermedialisation | Failure to allow for intraoperative oedema, or to recognise that the other side also needed augmentation; hence the slightly strained voice at the end
Implant extrusion | Medially into the airway (severe, can be an airway emergency; from an unrecognised mucosal breach, usually of the ventricle) or laterally through the skin
Pharyngocutaneous fistula | From a breach of the pyriform sinus during arytenoid adduction
Over-rotation of the arytenoid | From excessive suture tension; usually seen and corrected on the table
Bleeding, haematoma, airway obstruction, infection | General risks of the neck wound
Persistent dysphonia | Posterior gap, undermedialisation, implant too anterior or superior, height mismatch; may need revision
!!
## Alternatives
- **Observation** if the patient has no voice complaint and no dysphagia.
- **Injection laryngoplasty** with a long-lasting material such as calcium hydroxylapatite, or with autologous fat.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Evidence and history
- **Previous radiotherapy** does not significantly change subjective voice results or complications after medialisation laryngoplasty.
- **Isshiki** reintroduced medialisation (type I thyroplasty) in 1974; the principle is unchanged: move the paralysed fold to the midline so that the mobile fold can meet it.
- **Zeitels' adduction arytenopexy** (1998) is an alternative way to reposition the arytenoid, moving it posteriorly, superiorly and medially on the cricoid.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Why the patient is **awake**, and the end point: a slightly strained voice, a medialised fold, complete closure.
- The **window**: 5 to 7 mm behind the midline, 3 mm above the inferior border; why the **muscular tubercle** must be fully seen.
- The **commonest implant errors**: too anterior and too superior.
- **Why add arytenoid adduction**: posterior gap, short fold, height mismatch; neither injection nor medialisation closes the posterior glottis.
- How arytenoid adduction works: it mimics the **lateral cricoarytenoid**.
- The landmarks behind: pyriform sinus, cricopharyngeus, posterior cricoarytenoid, the "grain of rice".
- Why you review at 4 weeks, not sooner.
- Thyroplasty medialises the fold; it does not move it anteriorly, posteriorly or superiorly.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Not exposing the posterior end of the muscular tubercle.
- Forgetting to check the other fold's abduction before surgery.
- Stopping when the voice sounds perfect on the table, and undermedialising.
- Wide undermining around a Gore-Tex strip.
- Pulling hard on the adduction suture.
- Judging the result before the swelling has gone.
!!
