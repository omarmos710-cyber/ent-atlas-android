@stand Myringotomy with insertion of a ventilation tube (grommet) is the commonest operation in children's ENT and often the first ear operation a resident performs alone. It takes minutes, but it is done through a speculum a few millimetres wide, on a membrane a tenth of a millimetre thick, beside the ossicles and the chorda tympani. This chapter covers the decision, the drum you must map before you cut, each stage of the procedure, tympanocentesis for culture, the newer balloon dilation of the Eustachian tube, and the aftercare and complications you will see in clinic.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Myringotomy and ventilation tube
 | Summary
Indications | Bilateral otitis media with effusion for 3 months or more with documented hearing loss (children 6 months to 12 years); recurrent acute otitis media with effusion present; acute otitis media failing antibiotics; acute otitis media with a complication (mastoiditis, meningitis, intracranial abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis, facial palsy); otitis media with effusion in children at risk (sensory, physical, cognitive or behavioural problems); retraction and non-adherent atelectasis; eosinophilic otitis media; barotrauma and hyperbaric oxygen therapy
Contraindications | Clear fluid at myringotomy (possible CSF: do not insert a tube); a suspected high jugular bulb or aberrant carotid artery (stop and image); unfitness for anaesthesia
Anaesthesia | General by mask or laryngeal mask in children and anxious adults; in adults topical phenol or trichloroacetic acid on the drum, local injection of the canal (1% lidocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline) or sedation
Position | Supine, head turned away from the operated ear; semi-recumbent or sitting in the office
Antibiotics | Not recommended; clean-contaminated
Tray | Operating microscope or 0-degree endoscope (4 mm or 2.7 mm), specula of several sizes, wax loops, curettes and cup forceps, myringotomy knife, serrated crocodile (alligator) forceps, Rosen needle or curved pick and right-angled hook, 3, 5 and 7 French suction, the chosen tubes, cotton pledgets with 1:1000 adrenaline, antibiotic-steroid drops; Alden-Senturia trap or tuberculin syringe with an 18-gauge needle for culture
Time | 5 to 10 minutes for both ears
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the parents
- **Why:** to let air into the middle ear and drain the fluid, so that hearing returns and infections are fewer. Tubes fall out on their own, usually within 6 to 12 months for short-term tubes.
- **Discharge (otorrhoea):** in more than 1 in 5 children at some point; treated with drops.
- **Blockage** of the tube (about 7%), **granulation tissue** (about 4%) and **early extrusion** (about 4%).
- **A hole left in the drum** after the tube falls out (1% to 6%); most close, a few need repair.
- **The tube falling inwards** into the middle ear (about 0.5%), rarely needing an operation to retrieve it.
- **Scarring of the drum** (myringosclerosis) and thinning, usually harmless.
- **Need for further tubes** if the fluid returns once they extrude.
- The risks of a general anaesthetic.
!!
## Before you start
Take a focused history: fevers, otalgia, discharge, ear tugging and sleepless nights in a small child; hearing, speech, school performance and balance; how long effusion has been documented and how many acute episodes there have been. Note previous treatment (antibiotics, nasal steroids, antihistamines, decongestants) and conditions that make otitis media more likely or more serious: adenoid hypertrophy, rhinosinusitis, reflux, cleft palate and craniofacial syndromes, Down syndrome, ciliary dysfunction, smoke exposure, a family history of tubes, and previous tubes or ear surgery.
Examine both drums with pneumatic otoscopy or the microscope, the gold standard: opacity, bulging, fluid levels, retraction pockets and their depth, atelectasis, cholesteatoma and mobility. A flexible or rigid nasendoscopy shows the adenoid and the Eustachian tube orifice in older children and adults. A tympanogram (type B or C) and an age-appropriate hearing test document the indication. CT is needed only when cholesteatoma, granulation, acute mastoiditis, a tumour, a tegmen defect or encephalocele, or a vascular anomaly is suspected.
!pearl Definitions the guideline uses
Recurrent acute otitis media: three episodes in 6 months, or four in 12 months with one in the last 6. Persistent acute otitis media: failure to resolve on appropriate antibiotics, or two episodes within a month. Chronic otitis media with effusion: fluid for more than 3 months.
!!
## Choosing the tube
Short-term tubes are grommet-shaped, either an hourglass (Shepard type) or with bevelled inner flanges (Armstrong type), which stays longer; they are retained 6 to 12 months. Long-term tubes are T-tubes with long inner flanges, retained more than 12 months, for children with early extrusion or a long need for ventilation, such as cleft palate; the flanges can be trimmed. Plastic and silicone are standard; metal and hydroxyapatite tubes are still used.

# Anatomy you must see | Map the drum before you cut it.
## The drum through the microscope
Find the **handle of the malleus**, running down and back from the lateral process to the **umbo** at the centre of the drum. Draw an imaginary line along the handle and a second line at right angles through the umbo: these divide the **pars tensa** into four quadrants. The **pars flaccida** lies above the lateral process. The **light reflex** fans anteroinferiorly from the umbo. The **annulus** is the thickened rim of the pars tensa, seated in the tympanic sulcus.
!mf 4 | A right tympanic membrane through the endoscope. The posterosuperior quadrant (X), behind which lie the incus, stapes and chorda tympani, is avoided.
## What lies behind each quadrant
- **Posterosuperior:** the long process of the incus, the stapes and its footplate in the oval window, and the chorda tympani crossing medial to the drum. This quadrant is never incised.
- **Posteroinferior:** the round window niche and, in some, a high **jugular bulb**.
- **Anteroinferior:** the hypotympanum and the Eustachian tube orifice anteriorly; the usual site for a tube, and the least prone to early extrusion in many hands.
- **Anterosuperior:** the tensor tympani and the protympanum; a safe site but more awkward through a narrow canal.
- **Anterior wall** of the middle ear: in rare cases a dehiscent or aberrant **internal carotid artery**; a pulsatile red mass behind the drum is a reason to stop.
## The Eustachian tube
The tube runs from the protympanum down, forward and medially to the **torus tubarius** in the nasopharynx, posterior and slightly below the back of the inferior turbinate. Its lateral third is bony, its medial two thirds cartilaginous; the narrowest point, the **isthmus**, is the bony-cartilaginous junction. The middle 8 to 12 mm of the cartilaginous part acts as a valve, closed at rest and opened by the tensor veli palatini on swallowing and yawning; in infants it is shorter and more horizontal, which is why they get otitis media.

# The operation, stage by stage | Five stages, the same in the office and in theatre.
## See the drum and clean the canal
Choose the largest speculum the canal will take, and the microscope or a 0-degree endoscope. Remove wax and squamous debris with a loop, curette or fine suction, touching the canal wall as little as possible: the thin medial canal skin bleeds at a touch, and blood obscures the drum and blocks the tube. If you plan a culture, sterilise the canal first with alcohol or povidone-iodine irrigation.
!see What you should see
- The whole pars tensa with the annulus, the handle of the malleus and the umbo.
- The colour of the drum and of the fluid behind it, and any retraction pocket.
!!
!danger The canal wall
A haematoma raised by a speculum pushed too deep or a loop scraping the canal ruins the view and starts the bleeding that blocks tubes.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Look at the pars flaccida and the posterosuperior quadrant before you cut. A retraction pocket with keratin there is cholesteatoma, which changes the operation.
!!
## Make the myringotomy
With the myringotomy knife make an incision of about **2 mm** in the **anteroinferior quadrant** (or anterosuperior), radial or circumferential according to preference, through the full thickness of the drum. Keep away from the posterosuperior quadrant, do not touch the handle of the malleus and do not extend to the annulus. An incision that is too small makes insertion difficult; it can be widened with the crocodile forceps.
!see What you should see
- A clean slit with fluid (serous, mucoid or pus) welling through it, or the mucosa of the promontory if the ear is dry.
!!
!danger Clear fluid and pulsation
Clear fluid that keeps coming may be CSF: do not insert a tube; seek senior advice and image. A blue or red mass behind the drum, or pulsatile bleeding, means a jugular bulb or carotid artery; stop.
!!
## Clear the middle ear
Aspirate the effusion through the myringotomy with a **5 French** suction, or a 3 French if the drum is thin and atrophic, and a 7 French with saline irrigation if the fluid is thick glue. Send fluid for culture when the patient is unwell, immunocompromised or has a complication.
!see What you should see
- The middle ear emptied, with the drum falling back from the promontory.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Do not chase every drop of glue. Suction long and hard at the promontory traumatises the mucosa, and the tube will do the rest of the draining.
!!
## Insert the tube
Load the tube on serrated crocodile forceps at the edge of its outer flange, angled so the leading inner flange will enter the incision first. Pass it down the speculum without touching the canal, engage the inner flange through the incision and push or tease it home with a curved pick or Rosen needle until both flanges sit either side of the drum. A T-tube is inserted by folding the long flanges together.
!see What you should see
- The tube seated perpendicular to the drum with its lumen open, and middle ear mucosa visible through it.
!!
!danger The tube that falls in
Pushing too hard through a large incision can drop the tube into the middle ear. Retrieve it through the incision with a hook; if you cannot, place a new tube and deal with the medialised tube as a planned procedure.
!!
## Finish
Stop bleeding with a pledget soaked in 1:1000 adrenaline; a dry tube is a tube that stays open. Instil antibiotic-steroid drops if there was effusion, pus or bleeding. Record the side, the site, the fluid (serous, mucoid, purulent or dry) and the tube type.
!see What you should see
- A dry canal and a patent tube.
!!

# Variations and difficulties | When the standard steps do not work.
## The atelectatic drum
When the drum lies on the promontory there is nowhere to insert a tube. Inject **0.1 to 0.2 ml of air** through the drum with a 27-gauge needle to lift it off the promontory and create a space. In adherent atelectasis a tube may be impossible and tympanoplasty with a tube is the alternative; in some, observation is right.
!mf 2 | Severe atelectasis, with the drum draped over the stapes and promontory as a natural type II or III tympanoplasty.
## The narrow canal
Dilate a stenotic meatus with serially larger specula. When only a small speculum will pass, carry the tube into the medial canal beside the speculum and then manoeuvre it into the incision.
## Myringotomy for culture and tympanocentesis
For a culture, follow the first two stages, then swab the middle ear or aspirate with a 3 or 5 French suction attached to a small syringe. For **tympanocentesis**, follow the first stage and aspirate through the drum with an 18-gauge Rosen needle on a tuberculin syringe or an **Alden-Senturia trap**, again avoiding the posterosuperior quadrant. Both are used in acute otitis media with high fever, sepsis or a complication; myringotomy alone for pain is now uncommon, and a tube is usually inserted in a complicated case.

# Balloon Eustachian tuboplasty | For adult Eustachian tube dysfunction.
## Who and how
Balloon dilation of the cartilaginous Eustachian tube is offered to adults, and to children from about seven years when the tube approaches adult length, with dilatory Eustachian tube dysfunction: aural fullness, popping and pain with pressure change, type B or C tympanograms, persistent effusion or non-adherent atelectasis, and those who previously improved with tubes. It is contraindicated by a **dehiscent carotid canal** in the bony tube, so obtain a temporal bone CT beforehand and look at the sinuses too. It is done under general anaesthesia via a laryngeal mask, supine, the table turned 90 degrees and the head turned slightly toward the surgeon, with cardiac monitoring.
### The steps
- Decongest the nose with pledgets of oxymetazoline or 1:1000 adrenaline, then inspect with a 30- or 45-degree endoscope.
- Bend the catheter tip to about 55 to 70 degrees and guide it to the back of the nose; some systems use a 70-degree guide catheter. Out-fracture the inferior turbinate with a Freer elevator, or dilate between septum and turbinate, if the passage is tight; if the scope and catheter do not fit one side, view from the other.
- Identify the **torus tubarius**, rotate the catheter laterally into the orifice, and use it to roll the posterior cushion medially to see the lumen.
- Advance gently; **stop at resistance**, which is the isthmus. A balloon no longer than about 18 mm treats the cartilaginous valve without entering the bony tube.
- Inflate for **2 minutes**, deflate and withdraw, then inspect the lumen for tears or a false passage. A pledget soaked in dexamethasone can be placed in the orifice if the mucosa is swollen.
!mf 1 5 | Left: the nasopharyngeal opening of a left Eustachian tube with inflamed, hypertrophic posterior cushion (X) and a collapsed lumen with secretions (arrow). Right: the lumen of the cartilaginous tube after balloon dilation.
!danger Carotid plexus
Ventricular ectopics, bradycardia, tachycardia or a surge in systolic pressure during inflation are attributed to stimulation of the carotid sympathetic plexus: deflate at once and abandon the procedure. Never dilate beyond the isthmus.
!!
### After balloon dilation
No nose blowing for a week (surgical emphysema). Nasal steroid spray for several weeks, longer with sinonasal disease. A modified Valsalva (Valsalva with a swallow) from the second week, or a Politzer manoeuvre if the patient cannot; avoid Valsalva for 3 to 4 weeks after combined tympanoplasty or ossicular surgery. Review at 2 to 4 weeks with an audiogram and tympanogram, and keep long-term follow-up, as outcomes are still being defined. Complications include a false passage (along the anteromedial wall), epistaxis, surgical emphysema, a patulous tube, drum perforation from the pressure, tinnitus, dysrhythmias and, very rarely, carotid injury. Case series report success in about 70% to 80%.

# When it goes wrong | Drills for the ear.
## Crisis drills
!path Bleeding during myringotomy
Minor bleeding | pledget with 1:1000 adrenaline ; avoid further canal trauma
Brisk bleeding | fill the canal with thrombin-soaked gelatin sponge ; raise the head ; treat hypertension
Profuse or pulsatile bleeding | pack the canal tightly ; consider a jugular bulb or carotid injury ; senior help and transfer to a tertiary centre ; imaging
!!
!path Clear fluid through the myringotomy
Stop | do not insert a tube
Think | CSF leak through a tegmen defect or inner ear malformation
Act | senior review, CT of the temporal bones, manage as a CSF leak
!!
!path Arrhythmia during balloon dilation
Deflate | at once
Abort | withdraw the catheter
Monitor | treat hypertension, observe the rhythm
!!

# Aftercare | Drops, follow-up and water.
## After a tube
No drops are needed for a dry ear. For serous fluid or bleeding, steroid-fluoroquinolone (or fluoroquinolone) drops for 3 days; for mucoid or purulent fluid, up to 10 days, with review at 1 to 2 weeks. Otherwise review at about 4 weeks with an audiogram and tympanogram, then every 3 to 6 months until the tubes extrude and the drums heal. Routine water precautions are not supported by the evidence; advise them for lake, river or sea water, shallow diving, and children who get pain or discharge after swimming.
## Complications
!table Complications of ventilation tubes
Complication | Frequency | Management
Otorrhoea | More than 20% | Antibiotic or antibiotic-steroid drops and aural toilet; culture-directed treatment if persistent; look for cholesteatoma or mastoid disease if it will not settle
Tube blockage | About 7% | Drops or hydrogen peroxide; replace if needed
Granulation tissue | About 4% | Antibiotic-steroid drops; remove the tube if persistent (foreign-body reaction)
Early extrusion | About 4% | Seen in some on CPAP; lower the pressure; replace if still indicated
Perforation after extrusion | 1% to 6% | Observe a small one (it works as a ventilation tube); myringoplasty or tympanoplasty if persistent
Medialised tube | About 0.5% | Retrieve through the incision; exploratory tympanotomy if there is inflammation and it cannot be retrieved
Retention beyond 2 to 2.5 years | Uncommon with short-term tubes | Remove, with or without myringoplasty, or replace
Myringosclerosis, atrophy, atelectasis | Common | Generally irreversible and usually harmless
!!
## Alternatives
Culture-directed antibiotics (with infectious disease advice) for recurrent or persistent acute otitis media; treatment of reflux, rhinosinusitis and Eustachian tube inflammation with nasal or oral steroids; **adenoidectomy** with or instead of tubes when the adenoid is large and in children over about four with recurrent effusion; tubes and balloon dilation as alternatives to each other in adults; microdebrider reduction of a hypertrophic posterior cushion.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: for acute suppurative otitis media, a circumferential incision in the posteroinferior quadrant midway between the malleus handle and the annulus, avoiding the incudostapedial joint; for effusion, a small radial incision in the posteroinferior or anteroinferior quadrant; a grommet preferably in the anterosuperior quadrant for longer retention. It warns of an incision that stays in the superficial layers of a thick drum, of cutting the inflamed posterior canal wall, and of a high jugular bulb, aberrant carotid or glomus tympanicum; it adds intratympanic steroid injections and aero-otitis as indications.
Lee: uses the AAO-HNS guideline indications, emphasises adenoidectomy with a second set of tubes, and gives the otorrhoea, blockage and perforation rates.
Myers adds balloon Eustachian tuboplasty, practical fixes for the atelectatic drum and the narrow canal, and the warning about clear fluid and vascular anomalies.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Guideline indications: 3 months of bilateral effusion with hearing loss; recurrent acute otitis media with effusion; the at-risk child.
- Which quadrant and **why the posterosuperior quadrant is avoided** (incus, stapes, chorda tympani).
- Short-term versus long-term tubes and who gets a T-tube.
- Complication rates: otorrhoea more than 20%, perforation 1% to 6%.
- Clear fluid and the vascular anomaly: what you do.
- Balloon dilation: the isthmus, the carotid canal and the arrhythmia.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Incising the posterosuperior quadrant or touching the malleus handle.
- Raising a canal-wall haematoma with an oversized speculum or rough cleaning.
- A myringotomy too small for the tube, or so large that the tube falls in.
- Missing an attic retraction or cholesteatoma before inserting a tube.
- Leaving blood in the tube at the end.
- Advancing a balloon past resistance at the isthmus.
!!
