@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand Rigid tracheoscopy and bronchoscopy, a Hopkins rod telescope alone or inside a ventilating bronchoscope, is how the otolaryngologist sees and treats a child's larynx, trachea and bronchi: stenoses, webs, granulation, tracheitis, bronchial casts, and above all inhaled foreign bodies. Airway foreign bodies are the fourth commonest cause of accidental death in toddlers, about 3000 a year in the United States, mostly under the age of 5. Half are never witnessed, a third of children have no signs, and four in ten have a normal chest radiograph, so suspicion, not certainty, is the indication: any one of the history, examination or imaging pointing to a foreign body is reason enough for a rigid bronchoscopy, which is both the test and the treatment. Nuts and seeds are the commonest, then plastic toy parts, then metal, and the right main bronchus is the usual home. The skill is in patience: spontaneous ventilation, gentle positive pressure, a duplicate object to rehearse with, the right forceps, the object turned into the widest plane of the glottis, and everything withdrawn together. This chapter covers the indications, the technique of rigid and flexible endoscopy, removal of foreign bodies and the crises along the way.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Paediatric airway endoscopy and foreign bodies
 | Summary
Rigid endoscopy | Suspected foreign body; **biphasic or expiratory stridor** (almost always); inspiratory stridor without a cause on flexible laryngoscopy; stenosis, webs and granulation for treatment (laser, balloon, forceps, steroid injection); surveillance in tracheostomy-dependent children (suprastomal granulation, erosion at the tube tip); bacterial tracheitis and plastic bronchitis for debridement; securing a difficult airway with a ventilating bronchoscope
Flexible scope | The distal airway: chronic cough, recurrent pneumonia, persistent wheeze, haemoptysis, lavage; not usually for removing foreign bodies, but an adjunct for distal ones
Difficult if | Medically fragile patients (relative); cervical spine instability or fusion, severe Chiari, temporomandibular ankylosis or oral abnormalities that block laryngoscopy (flexible bronchoscopy may be an alternative)
Anaesthesia | **No sedative premedication** with stridor or a suspected foreign body; inhalational induction then intravenous access; **spontaneous ventilation** preferred for foreign bodies, avoiding forceful positive pressure; the **endoscopist performs the first laryngoscopy**; intravenous steroid at the start
Position | Supine, head extended on a shoulder roll in a gel ring; neutral and SSEP-monitored if the cervical spine is unstable
Antibiotics | Not routinely, unless infection is suspected
Tray | Age-appropriate laryngoscope (Phillips, Miller or Parsons); 0 degree Hopkins rod; rigid ventilating bronchoscopes with telescope and camera; rigid and flexible suctions; optical forceps (alligator, peanut, cup); non-optical forceps (forward-grasping, rotation, globular, hollow object); Fogarty catheters and ureteral baskets; flexible bronchoscope
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the parents
- **Why:** to look into the windpipe and lungs under anaesthetic and remove anything that has been breathed in.
- **Damage to teeth**, the throat, voice box or airways; bleeding into the airway.
- **A collapsed lung or air leak** in the chest.
- **The object may not come out** at once: a second operation after a few days, or rarely an operation on the chest.
- **An overnight stay** is usual; a chest radiograph afterwards.
!!
## Before you start
Ask about previous laryngoscopy, bronchoscopy, airway surgery or tracheostomy; intubation or intensive care; anaesthetic problems in the family; spine or brain abnormalities; stridor (inspiratory, expiratory or biphasic, its duration, severity and triggers); a **witnessed choking episode** (present in only about half); cough, choking, gagging, breathlessness, wheeze, hoarseness or dysphagia.
## Examination
Check neck extension, mouth opening and Mallampati grade. A **laryngeal** foreign body causes hoarseness, aphonia, distress or stridor. A **tracheal** one may give the classic triad: an audible **slap**, a palpable **thud** and an **asthmatoid wheeze**. A **bronchial** one causes wheeze and reduced air entry on that side. **5 to 40%** have no signs at all.
## Imaging
Never send a child in significant respiratory distress to radiology: go to theatre. About **80%** of airway foreign bodies are radiolucent. Inspiratory and expiratory chest films show **air trapping** on the affected side (accentuated on expiration); in children who cannot cooperate, **lateral decubitus** films show trapping when the affected side is down. Neck films may show laryngeal or subglottic objects. The commonest findings are air trapping or hyperinflation and atelectasis, but **about 40% are normal**. Flexion-extension cervical films in Down syndrome or other instability (their accuracy is debated).

# Anatomy you must see | Landmarks.
## Landmarks
- **At laryngoscopy:** tongue base, vallecula, epiglottis, arytenoids and vocal folds.
- **At bronchoscopy:** subglottis, trachea, carina, main and lobar bronchi.
- **Transnasal flexible:** nasal cavity, nasopharynx, soft palate, epiglottis, arytenoids, cords.
- The **right main bronchus** is the commonest site; the **sagittal plane** is the widest diameter of the larynx.

# The operation, stage by stage | Laryngoscopy, bronchoscopy, extraction.
## Expose the larynx
Protect the upper teeth with a guard, or moist gauze if there are none. Insert the laryngoscope along the **right side of the tongue**, sweep it left, place the tip in the **vallecula**, and identify the epiglottis, arytenoids and cords. Apply anti-fog solution. Introduce the telescope or bronchoscope through the mouth and examine the oropharynx, hypopharynx and larynx.
!mf 3 | Inserting the rigid bronchoscope after direct laryngoscopy, the blade along the right of the tongue and its tip in the vallecula.
!see What you should see
- The epiglottis, both arytenoids and the cords, with the scope tip in the vallecula.
!!
## Pass the glottis
Turn the bronchoscope **90 degrees anticlockwise** so the leading edge of its bevel lies in the plane of the cords, pass the tip through, and once in the subglottis turn it **90 degrees clockwise**. This protects the cords and vocal processes.
!mf 4 | Passing the rigid bronchoscope: rotated 90 degrees anticlockwise to align the bevel with the cords (A, B), passed beyond them (C), and rotated back 90 degrees clockwise in the subglottis (D).
!see What you should see
- The bevel slipping between the cords without touching them, then tracheal rings.
!!
## Steady the scope
With the tip in the trachea, remove the laryngoscope and support the bronchoscope between **thumb and forefinger** to protect the upper gum and teeth. (With a telescope alone, leave the laryngoscope in place.) The side port connects to the anaesthetic circuit, so the child can be ventilated through it, which also makes it an emergency airway.
!mf 5 | The bronchoscope supported by thumb and forefinger once in the trachea, protecting the upper gum and teeth.
!see What you should see
- The carina in view and the chest moving with ventilation through the side port.
!!
## Examine the bronchi
For the **right** bronchi, turn the head **left** and rotate the tip clockwise; for the **left**, turn the head **right** and rotate anticlockwise. Clear secretions with a rigid suction through the main channel or a flexible catheter through the side port. With a large leak, change to intravenous anaesthesia to avoid polluting theatre with anaesthetic gas.
!see What you should see
- Each lobar opening in turn: on the right the upper lobe, then middle and lower; on the left the upper with the lingula, then the lower.
!!
## Flexible bronchoscopy
Usually under general anaesthesia, transnasally or through an LMA, tube or tracheostomy (check the size fits; lubricate with silicone; use a right-angled adaptor with a perforated diaphragm to ventilate around it). Apply topical lidocaine to the cords and carina through the side port. Lavage, diagnosis of the lower airway and some laser treatment are possible.
!see What you should see
- The cords, the trachea and the lobar and segmental bronchi through the flexible scope with the child oxygenating well.
!!
## Prepare for the foreign body
Have an experienced endoscopist, anaesthetist and team, and **check every instrument beforehand**. Ask the family for a **duplicate** of the object to plan the grip. Suction secretions and study the object's shape and position before touching it.
!see What you should see
- The object's shape, its presenting part and the swollen mucosa round it.
!!
## Grasp it properly
Choose the instrument for the object: **optical peanut forceps** for nuts, **alligator forceps** for sharp objects. Grasp under direct telescopic view, avoiding pushing it further down. Keep sharp points **inside the bronchoscope** or trailing. Rotate the object into the **sagittal plane** before it reaches the glottis. If it fits inside the sheath, **withdraw object, forceps and bronchoscope together**, so it does not catch at the glottis, break off or tear the mucosa. Oxymetazoline helps with bleeding or granulation around it.
!mf 6 | A dry corn kernel in the distal trachea (A), grasped with optical peanut forceps (B), and removed (C).
!mf 7 | A radiopaque pen clip in the right main bronchus (A, B), grasped with alligator forceps (C), and removed (D).
!see What you should see
- The forceps blades beyond the object's widest part before they close, then the object, forceps and scope moving out together.
!!
!danger The object lost at the glottis
If it is dropped in the subglottis or trachea and obstructs the airway, **push it back into the right main bronchus** so the child can be ventilated, then try again.
!!
## Look again
After removal, **re-bronchoscope** to check for injury, residual fragments or a second object. If bleeding, oedema or granulation prevents complete removal, take what you safely can, treat with antibiotics and steroids for a few days in hospital, and return when the inflammation has settled.
!see What you should see
- A clear bronchus to its segments on both sides.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Obstruction during extraction
Act | push the object back into the right main bronchus ; ventilate ; regroup and retry
!!
!path A child too unwell for imaging
Act | straight to theatre for rigid endoscopy
!!
!path Cannot remove it
Options | staged removal after antibiotics and steroids ; Fogarty catheter or basket for distal objects ; flexible bronchoscope as an adjunct ; rarely thoracoscopy or thoracotomy
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Not checking the equipment before starting.
- Grasping hastily, breaking the object or pushing it distally.
- Letting it dislodge at the glottis or subglottis.
- Missing a residual fragment or a second object.
!!

# Aftercare | Observe and image.
## After the operation
Diagnostic bronchoscopy may be a day case, but observe most infants **under 6 months** overnight with pulse oximetry, and children with significant airway disease on the ward or in intensive care. Most children stay overnight after foreign body removal (older children with normal airways may go home the same day). Obtain a **chest radiograph**, especially if pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum or atelectasis is possible. Salbutamol for bronchospasm; several doses of steroid for significant oedema; antibiotics if the object was long-standing or infection suspected.
## Complications
!table Complications
Timing | Complications
During | Dental injury; injury to the pharynx, larynx, trachea or bronchi; bleeding into the airway; pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum; failure to remove
Early | Atelectasis; wheeze; stridor; laryngeal oedema; traumatic laryngitis; pneumonia; post-obstructive pulmonary oedema; lung abscess
Late | Bronchopleural fistula; bronchial stricture; laryngotracheal stenosis
!!
## Alternatives
Rigid bronchoscopy is the gold standard; radiographs alone are neither sensitive nor specific enough; CT (with or without virtual bronchoscopy) can help with unusual cases; thoracoscopy or thoracotomy rarely.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Which children need rigid endoscopy?
All the following are indications: suspected foreign body, subglottic web or stricture, a tracheostomy with an obstructing suprastomal granuloma, unexplained biphasic stridor and plastic bronchitis. **Laryngomalacia** is the exception: it is diagnosed by flexible laryngoscopy and needs rigid endoscopy only if the symptoms are out of proportion to what is seen. The editor stresses a high index of suspicion and a low threshold for rigid endoscopy in suspected foreign bodies.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- The epidemiology: under 5s, nuts and seeds, right main bronchus, half unwitnessed.
- Signs: slap, thud, asthmatoid wheeze; reduced air entry; often none.
- Radiographs: radiolucent objects, expiratory and decubitus films, 40% normal.
- Anaesthesia: no sedation, spontaneous breathing, endoscopist's first look.
- Passing the glottis with the 90 degree rotations.
- Forceps choice, the sagittal plane, withdrawing en bloc.
- The rescue: push it into the right main bronchus.
- Postoperative observation and the chest film.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Dismissing a wheezy toddler with a normal radiograph.
- Bagging hard and driving the object deeper.
- Stopping after the first object.
!!
