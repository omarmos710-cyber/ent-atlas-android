@stand A foreign body in the ear canal is usually a bead or an earring in a small child, a cotton bud tip in an adult, a hearing-aid dome or a disc battery in an older person, or a live insect at night. Removing it is a test of judgement more than of skill: the first attempt has the best chance, and each failed attempt makes the next harder by making the canal swell and the patient frightened. This chapter tells you how to choose the right method for the object, how to remove it under the microscope without hurting the canal or the drum, when to stop and arrange a general anaesthetic, and how to deal with the three emergencies: batteries, insects and swelling vegetable matter.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Removal of a foreign body from the ear canal
 | Summary
Indications | Any foreign body in the canal, presenting with pain, discharge, hearing loss or found on examination; a foreign object should not be left in the canal
Urgency | Disc batteries: remove at once. Live insects: urgent. Vegetable matter: soon, before it swells. Inert objects: elective, so the right instruments, light and anaesthesia can be arranged
Anaesthesia | None for most in the clinic; local canal infiltration for a painful canal; sedation or general anaesthesia for young children, anxious or uncooperative adults, a severely inflamed canal, an object wedged medially, or after failed attempts
Position | Seated or supine with the head stabilised on a headrest; insect cases supine with the affected ear up; avoid a papoose if possible
Antibiotics | Not needed for an atraumatic removal; topical drops for a traumatised or infected canal; systemic antibiotics for cellulitis or a diabetic patient
Tray | Binocular microscope, specula 3 to 6 mm, suction 3 to 12 French (a 10 or 12 French nasal sucker for round objects), small crocodile and Hartmann forceps, right-angled hook, wax curette and loop, cotton-tipped applicators, mineral oil, alcohol and lidocaine for insects, a strong magnet for batteries, lidocaine with adrenaline, silver nitrate, ear wick, irrigation kit (rarely used)
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient or parent
- Most objects come out in the clinic in a few minutes; some need a short general anaesthetic, especially in young children.
- **Bleeding and a graze of the canal skin**, which can become infected.
- **A hole in the eardrum** or, rarely, injury to the hearing bones, with hearing loss or dizziness, if the object is deep or the patient moves.
- **Narrowing of the canal** later, after a severe injury.
- Rarely, a small cut at the entrance of the ear or behind the ear to reach an object that is wedged deep.
!!
## Before you start
Ask when it happened and whether anyone saw it; what was within reach (beads, toys, food, batteries in the house, hearing aids); how long it has been there; pain, discharge, smell, bleeding and hearing loss; and, crucially, **how many attempts have already been made and by whom**. A history of previous foreign bodies, infection or discharge suggests a canal that may be stenosed, friable and hypervascular, or an object that has been there for weeks. Ask about diabetes, bleeding tendency and anticoagulants, and previous ear surgery: in a canal-wall-down cavity or a congenital anomaly the facial nerve, the lateral semicircular canal and the windows may be exposed.
Examine both ears with the microscope, and the nose (a child who puts something in one orifice may have put something in another). Listen to the breathing and the chest: if there is any suggestion of an inhaled object, flexible laryngoscopy and a chest radiograph come first. Imaging is not routine: CT if the object's medial extent is in doubt or the ear has had extensive surgery or disease, MRI if an encephalocele or CSF leak is suspected. Obtain an audiogram before any removal under sedation or general anaesthesia, and an audiogram and CT if vertigo or hearing loss suggests the drum or inner ear has been breached.
!pearl Who should go straight to the ENT clinic
Spherical or sharp objects, disc batteries, vegetable matter, objects next to the drum, objects present for more than 24 hours, children under four, a hidden object or an agitated child, and any ear where removal has already been tried.
!!

# Anatomy you must see | The canal narrows, bends and becomes exquisitely sensitive.
## The canal
The adult canal is about 24 mm long. Its **lateral third is cartilaginous**, hair-bearing and mobile, lined by thick skin; its **medial two thirds are bony**, lined by thin skin directly on periosteum, which bleeds and hurts at the lightest touch. The canal narrows at the **bony-cartilaginous junction** and again at the **isthmus** in the bony part; a round object pushed past the isthmus is trapped. Medially the floor dips into the **anteroinferior recess** between the drum and the anterior canal wall, where small objects hide. In a child the bony canal is short and the drum lies more horizontally.
## The nerves you will provoke
The canal and concha are supplied by the auricular branch of the vagus (Arnold's nerve), which can trigger coughing, vomiting or a vasovagal faint, and by the auriculotemporal nerve and the cervical plexus. Facial grimacing moves the cartilaginous canal, so a calm patient matters.
## The drum and beyond
Keep the drum, the handle of the malleus and the posterosuperior quadrant in view. In an operated ear, know where the facial nerve and lateral canal lie before you put an instrument in the cavity.

# The operation, stage by stage | Match the method to the object.
## Prepare the patient and the room
Plan before you touch: the right speculum sizes, the suction, a hook and forceps on the tray, the microscope set up, and an assistant to steady a child's head. Explain each step and the noise of the suction. A calm, still patient is the single most important factor; a child who has already been hurt may need a general anaesthetic, and it is better to decide that now than after another failed attempt.
!see What you should see
- The object itself, its shape, consistency and how far in it lies, and whether any canal wall or drum is visible beyond it.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
You may have only one chance with a child. If you cannot see past the object, or the child will not keep still, stop and book a short general anaesthetic.
!!
## Try suction first for smooth, round objects
Beads and other hard, smooth objects cannot be grasped: forceps simply push them further in. Choose a **large suction (10 or 12 French)**, bring the tip into contact with the object to make a seal, and draw it out along the canal. Soft, wet material behaves like wax and can be aspirated with a 5 French sucker.
!mf 1 | A large-bore sucker sealed against the object draws most objects out; take care not to push the object further in.
!see What you should see
- The object coming out on the sucker tip without the canal wall bleeding.
!!
!danger Pushing it deeper
Approaching a round object directly from the front pushes it past the isthmus onto the drum. If suction will not seal, change method rather than pushing harder.
!!
## Get behind it with a right-angled hook
Pass a **right-angled hook** flat along the canal wall, parallel to the object and through the gap between object and wall, past its medial side. Rotate the tip 90 degrees so it lies behind the object and draw it laterally. Pass along the superior or anterior wall where there is room; instruments slid medially along the posterior wall can strike the drum.
!mf 2 | A right-angled hook passed beyond the object and turned behind it, then drawn laterally.
!see What you should see
- The tip of the hook beyond the object before you turn it, and the object rolling outward.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Irregular, soft objects (paper, tissue, foam, a cotton bud tip) are the ones for the crocodile forceps. Hard, smooth ones are for the hook and the sucker.
!!
## Clear and inspect
After removal, look again at the whole canal and the drum. Remove fragments and wax, check the canal skin for lacerations and replace any flap of lacerated skin so that epithelium is not trapped to form a canal cholesteatoma. Check the drum for perforation. Remove any second object.
!see What you should see
- An intact drum with a visible handle of malleus and a canal without trapped skin flaps.
!!
!danger Missed injury
Failing to reposition a lacerated canal skin flap can trap epithelium and produce a canal cholesteatoma months later.
!!

# Special objects | Batteries, insects and vegetables.
## Disc batteries
Remove a battery **at once**. Moisture lets current flow and causes liquefactive necrosis of the canal and drum within hours. **Do not irrigate**, and avoid lacerating the canal: water and blood both let current flow. Combine forceps and a right-angled hook; most disc batteries are magnetic, so a strong magnet may lift it out atraumatically. Inspect carefully afterwards for burns and arrange early review.
## Live insects
A live insect causes terrible noise and pain, and trying to grasp it makes both worse and risks the drum. Lay the patient supine with the ear up and **drown or paralyse it first** by filling the canal with mineral oil, alcohol (ether in older practice), or topical lidocaine or tetracaine; expect a brief flurry of activity as it drowns. Infiltrate the canal with local anaesthetic if the pain is severe. Then remove the insect and the fluid with suction, pick out wings and legs with forceps or a fine cotton applicator, and inspect the canal and drum. Irrigation is acceptable once the insect is dead and the drum is known to be intact.
## Vegetable matter
Peas, beans, nuts, seeds and corn swell when they absorb water or canal secretions, becoming tightly impacted and painful. Remove them dry: suction if intact, or a right-angled hook passed medial to the object to roll it out. **Avoid irrigation**, which makes them swell and disintegrate. If the material is soft and crumbling and cannot be suctioned, gentle irrigation may be needed after all.
## Irrigation, when it is right
Irrigation rarely flushes a foreign body out and often macerates the skin; reserve it for soft, friable material and dead insects in an ear with an intact drum. Use warm water (water at body temperature avoids caloric vertigo), half-strength hydrogen peroxide, a proper irrigator or a wide cannula on a syringe, and direct the jet along the canal wall, not at the drum. Never irrigate an ear with a perforation, an atrophic or retracted drum, a battery or vegetable matter.

# When it is wedged | Surgical access.
## Widening the meatus
A wedged object that completely fills the canal leaves no room for an instrument. After local infiltration, often best done in theatre, a **relaxing incision at the incisura** (the 12 o'clock position between the tragus and the helix) widens the meatus enough to pass a hook medial to the object.
!mf 3 | An incision at the incisura (12 o'clock) widens the meatus so that an instrument can pass medial to the object.
## The postauricular approach
Rarely, an object impacted at or beyond the bony isthmus, or pushed into the middle ear, needs a postauricular incision under sedation or general anaesthesia. Elevate the canal skin, reflect the ear forwards, and, if needed, drill away a prominent spine of Henle or the tympanomastoid suture to recontour the canal and deliver the object from behind.
!mf 4 | The ear reflected forwards through a postauricular incision to expose an object lodged in the medial canal.

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path The object goes deeper
Stop | do not push again
Reassess | can you see beyond it? is the drum intact? is the patient still?
Escalate | microscope and general anaesthetic ; meatal or postauricular access if wedged at the isthmus
!!
!path Vertigo, hearing loss or blood from behind the drum
Suspect | perforation, ossicular dislocation, stapes fracture or perilymph fistula
Act | stop instrumentation ; audiogram and CT ; early senior review ; avoid irrigation
Plan | exploratory tympanotomy for a suspected fistula or ossicular injury
!!
!path Faint during removal
Recognise | vagal stimulation from Arnold's nerve
Act | stop, lay flat, raise the legs ; recover before trying again ; consider infiltration or a general anaesthetic
!!

# Aftercare | Most need nothing; some need a wick and drops.
## After removal
An atraumatic removal from a healthy canal needs no treatment. If the canal was macerated, infected or lacerated, give antibiotic-steroid drops twice daily; if it is oedematous, place an **ear wick** and remove it after about three days (a week for a severe laceration), continuing the drops for a few more days. Keep the ear dry for one to two weeks (cotton wool with petroleum jelly in the shower). Review within a week any patient with infection, a difficult removal or a complication, and early for any drum or middle ear injury.
## Complications
!table Complications
Complication | Recognition | Management
Canal laceration and bleeding | Blood in the canal, a raised skin flap | Replace the flap, topical adrenaline or silver nitrate, drops
Otitis externa and cellulitis | Pain, swelling, discharge, spread to the concha | Drops and wick; oral antibiotics (ciprofloxacin covers Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas) if cellulitis or diabetes
Drum perforation | Seen on inspection; conductive loss | Keep dry; most heal; myringoplasty if persistent
Ossicular injury, perilymph fistula | Conductive or sensorineural loss, vertigo | Audiogram, CT, exploration
Canal stenosis | Late narrowing after severe trauma or infection | Stenting early; canalplasty if established
Canal cholesteatoma | Late keratin collection from trapped skin | Removal; prevent by replacing flaps
Battery burn | Necrosis of canal or drum | Early review, debridement, repair later
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: lists forceps, syringing, suction, microscopic instruments and the postaural approach; recommends syringing for most seed grains and smooth objects, warns that smooth hard objects such as ball bearings should not be grasped with forceps, and advises general anaesthesia and the microscope for impacted objects or after earlier attempts. For live insects it advises killing the insect with oil before removal.
Lee: emphasises that foreign bodies in children are often multiple and that batteries are an emergency.
Myers argues against irrigation for most objects (it rarely flushes them out and macerates the canal, and makes vegetable matter swell), favouring large-bore suction and the right-angled hook, and adds the magnet for batteries and the incisural incision.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Choose by the object: **soft and irregular, forceps; hard and round, suction or a hook behind it**.
- The **disc battery**: remove now, no irrigation, no bleeding, try a magnet.
- The **insect**: drown it first.
- **Vegetable matter**: no water.
- Ease of removal falls with every failed attempt; know when to stop and book a general anaesthetic.
- Anatomy: the isthmus and the anteroinferior recess; Arnold's nerve.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Grasping a bead with forceps and pushing it onto the drum.
- Irrigating a battery or a pea.
- Grabbing a live insect.
- Instruments slid along the posterior canal wall onto the drum.
- Leaving a lacerated skin flap displaced.
- Repeated attempts on a frightened child.
!!
