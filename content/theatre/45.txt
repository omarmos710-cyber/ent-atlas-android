@stand Rigid oesophagoscopy and hypopharyngoscopy are part of every panendoscopy for head and neck cancer, of every impacted food bolus and coin, and of pouch and stricture surgery. They are also the commonest way a junior resident perforates an oesophagus. This chapter sets out the anatomy that tells you where the scope is, the choice between rigid, flexible and transnasal endoscopy, each stage of passing a rigid scope safely to the cardia, how to examine and biopsy, and how to recognise and manage a perforation.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Rigid oesophagoscopy and hypopharyngoscopy
 | Summary
Indications: diagnostic | Dysphagia, odynophagia or a mass; staging and synchronous primaries in head and neck cancer (panendoscopy); the unknown primary; suspected trauma, stricture or inflammation; biopsy
Indications: therapeutic | Removal of a foreign body; dilatation of strictures; stent insertion; endoscopic pouch surgery (cricopharyngeal myotomy); laser procedures; excision of benign lesions
Contraindications | Haemodynamic instability; poor general health; known perforation; inability to obtain informed consent; for rigid endoscopy, an immobile or unstable cervical spine, large osteophytes, trismus, a receding mandible or aortic aneurysm (use flexible endoscopy)
Anaesthesia | General with an oral endotracheal tube (often a small tube placed to the left corner of the mouth); full relaxation
Position | Supine; head raised and neck flexed on the chest with the head extended at the atlanto-occipital joint, so mouth, pharynx and oesophagus fall in one line; no shoulder roll
Antibiotics | Not routine (except gastrostomy)
Tray | Rigid oesophagoscopes in several lengths and diameters (adult and paediatric), light carriers, 0-degree long telescope and camera, long suction cannulas, cup biopsy forceps, serrated and crocodile (alligator) grasping forceps, optical forceps, bougies and a guidewire or lumen finder, upper tooth guard or wet gauze, lubricant; laryngoscopes (Weerda, Dedo) for the hypopharynx
Time | 10 to 20 minutes
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Sore throat** for a day or two.
- **Damage to teeth, crowns or lips**; a broken tooth needs a dentist.
- **Perforation of the oesophagus** in about 1%, higher (up to about 6%) when a stricture is dilated or a foreign body removed, and higher with rigid than flexible scopes. It may need a nasogastric tube, antibiotics and fasting, or an operation, and can rarely be life-threatening.
- **Bleeding** from biopsy sites.
- **Heart rhythm changes** from vagal stimulation, usually brief.
!!
## Before you start
Take the dysphagia history: solids, liquids or both, the level the patient points to, onset, progression and duration, regurgitation, weight loss, pain, hoarseness, cough, haemoptysis, heartburn, globus, postnasal drip, night sweats and a neck lump. Note cancer history and radiotherapy to the neck, smoking and alcohol, cardiac and respiratory disease, rheumatological disease (scleroderma), and anything that limits neck movement: arthritis, osteophytes, spinal fusion. Ask about previous chest, abdominal or mediastinal surgery and drugs that dry the mouth.
Examine the head and neck: masses, nodes, tenderness, and how far the neck flexes and extends (fibrosis after radiotherapy limits both). Perform **flexible nasendoscopy** in every patient: pooling of secretions, cord movement, the airway, masses and the dynamic movement of the larynx and pharynx.
A **contrast swallow** shows a pouch, web, stricture, dysmotility, extrinsic compression or a radiolucent foreign body before you instrument. Use **barium** unless perforation is suspected; then use a **water-soluble agent** (gastrografin), which does not cause mediastinitis if it leaks (but is harmful if aspirated). A speech and language therapy assessment or modified barium swallow shows the swallow dynamics. CT characterises a mass. Medical clearance for the anaesthetic is usually all the preparation needed.

## Rigid, flexible or transnasal?
The patient's general condition, the purpose and the patient's cooperation decide. **Rigid** endoscopy is better for the hypopharynx, cricopharyngeus and cervical oesophagus, where mucosal folds hide lesions from a flexible scope, and takes large suction cannulas and grasping forceps for foreign bodies; it needs a general anaesthetic and a mobile neck. **Flexible** oesophagoscopy (transoral under sedation or **transnasal** under topical anaesthesia) reaches the gastro-oesophageal junction and stomach, works in a stiff neck or past osteophytes, is safer, and is preferred for surveillance and for placing a gastrostomy; transnasal endoscopy is done in the clinic and the patient goes straight home.

# Anatomy you must see | Three constrictions and a wall without serosa.
## The oesophagus
The oesophagus runs 22 to 25 cm from the **pharyngo-oesophageal junction at C6** (the lower border of the cricoid) to the cardia at about T11. It curves gently to the left in the neck, returns to the midline around T5, and swings left again in front of the aorta before passing through the diaphragmatic hiatus. It has **cervical** (cricopharyngeus to the sternal notch), **thoracic** and **abdominal** parts, and its wall has mucosa, submucosa, muscularis and adventitia but **no serosa**, which is why leaks spread freely into the mediastinum.
!table Distances from the upper incisors in an adult
Landmark | Distance | What it means
Cricopharyngeus (upper sphincter) | About 15 to 16 cm | The narrowest point; most perforations and impacted foreign bodies
Aortic arch and left main bronchus | About 23 to 27 cm | Pulsation visible; second constriction
Lower sphincter and cardia | About 38 to 40 cm | Third constriction; red, velvety gastric mucosa beyond
!!
## The hypopharynx
The **piriform sinuses** lie either side of the larynx, the **postcricoid region** behind it, and the **posterior pharyngeal wall** behind that. **Killian's dehiscence**, the weak area between the thyropharyngeus and the cricopharyngeus fibres of the inferior constrictor on the posterior wall, is where a pouch arises and where a scope pushed against a closed sphincter perforates. The cervical spine lies directly behind; anterior osteophytes indent the posterior wall.

# The operation, stage by stage | Rigid oesophagoscopy.
## Position and protect
After intubation, position the head: raised on a pillow or headrest with the neck flexed and the head extended at the atlanto-occipital joint (the "sniffing" position), so that the axes of mouth, pharynx and oesophagus align; an assistant may hold the head to adjust it during the passage. Protect the upper teeth with a guard or folded wet gauze. Choose the scope (a long adult scope for the oesophagus, a shorter wider one or a laryngoscope for the hypopharynx) and lubricate it.
!see What you should see
- A guard over the upper teeth and a head the assistant can move.
!!
!danger The teeth
The upper incisors are the most common thing injured. The scope must never lever on them.
!!
## Enter the mouth and find the arytenoids
Open the mouth with a scissor movement of thumb and index finger. Hold the scope like a pen in your right hand and introduce it from the **right side of the tongue**, moving to the midline over its dorsum. Rest the scope on the **thumb of the left hand**, which acts as the fulcrum and protects the teeth, and use the left thumb and index finger to advance it; the right hand only changes the angle. Pass down the posterior pharyngeal wall: you see the epiglottis, then the endotracheal tube, then the **arytenoids**.
!mf 4 | The left thumb as the fulcrum and the advancing hand, keeping pressure off the incisors; the right hand only steers.
!see What you should see
- The epiglottis, the tube passing through the glottis and the two arytenoids behind it.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Your left hand never leaves the scope. If you need both hands for an instrument, an assistant holds it, or you withdraw.
!!
## Pass the cricopharyngeus
Keeping the tip **strictly in the midline behind the larynx**, lift the larynx forward with the tip (a gentle lever with the left thumb) to open the postcricoid space. The closed sphincter appears as a horizontal slit or rosette. Apply slow, sustained, gentle pressure and wait: the sphincter relaxes and the lumen opens ahead; follow it in. **Never force it.** If it will not open, ask for more muscle relaxant, ask the anaesthetist to deflate the tube cuff, use a smaller scope, or find the lumen with a fine bougie or guidewire.
!mf 5 | The scope advanced over the posterior pharyngeal wall; the tongue base and larynx are lifted forwards until the scope lies in the axis of the lumen.
!see What you should see
- The lumen of the oesophagus opening ahead of the tip, with mucosa on all sides.
!!
!danger The commonest site of perforation
Pushing against a closed cricopharyngeus drives the tip through Killian's dehiscence or into a pouch. If you cannot see the lumen, you are not in it.
!!
## Advance to the cardia
Once in the oesophagus, advance only with the lumen constantly in view, suctioning secretions with a long cannula. At about **25 cm**, pulsation of the aortic arch marks the second constriction; lower the head slightly to keep the lumen in line. Toward the cardia, the head and shoulders are lowered and the head moved slightly to the right, so the scope points toward the left anterior superior iliac spine. The cardia is recognised by redder, velvety, rugose gastric mucosa.
!see What you should see
- A continuous lumen, aortic pulsation at the mid-oesophagus, and the change to gastric mucosa at about 38 to 40 cm.
!!
!danger Resistance
Never advance against resistance. A stricture, tumour or angulation is a reason to stop, image or switch to a flexible scope, not to push.
!!
## Examine on the way out and biopsy last
Withdraw slowly, inspecting all four walls; the mucosa is best examined coming out. Take biopsies **at the end**, so that bleeding does not hide the rest of the examination, and take several from the edge of a lesion with cup forceps. Examine the hypopharynx with a laryngoscope or the scope's tip as you leave: each piriform sinus to its apex, the postcricoid region and the posterior wall. Suction air from the stomach before removing the scope, and check the teeth.
!mf 3 | Oesophagoscopes and long suction cannulas; below, serrated, cup and crocodile forceps: cups for biopsy, the others for foreign bodies.
!see What you should see
- All four walls inspected, the depth of any lesion recorded in centimetres from the incisors, and intact teeth.
!!

# Flexible transnasal oesophagoscopy | In the clinic chair.
## Technique
Sit the patient upright or slightly reclined. Spray the nose with 4% lidocaine and oxymetazoline, then place pledgets of tetracaine, and give 2% lidocaine gel to gargle and swallow. Pass the scope along the widest nasal passage under vision, avoiding septum and turbinates, to the nasopharynx. Survey the larynx and pharynx, then each piriform sinus. Place the tip in the **left piriform sinus** and ask the patient to swallow: the scope slips through the cricopharyngeus. Insufflate to open the lumen, inspect the mucosa to the lower sphincter and gastro-oesophageal junction, enter the stomach and retroflex to see the cardia, then examine the oesophagus again on withdrawal. A flexible biopsy or grasping forceps passes down the channel. No cardiac monitoring is needed without sedation, and the patient leaves at once.
!pearl Where transnasal endoscopy changes management
It is most useful in men, obese patients and those with dysphagia after head and neck cancer treatment, and avoids a general anaesthetic. Common errors are poor topical anaesthesia, forcing the scope through the narrow side of the nose, and failing to look on the way out.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Perforation and the heart.
## Crisis drills
!path Suspected perforation during or after the procedure
Recognise | in theatre: surgical emphysema, high airway pressures, reduced breath sounds ; afterwards: pain in the neck, chest or back, fever, tachycardia, odynophagia, crepitus
Act now | stop ; chest radiograph ; nil by mouth ; nasogastric tube placed with care ; intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics ; H2 blocker or proton pump inhibitor
Confirm | water-soluble contrast swallow when awake and stable ; CT if unclear
Treat | small contained cervical leak: conservative ; large or thoracic leak, sepsis or pneumothorax: early surgical exploration and repair, drainage, chest drain ; involve thoracic surgeons
!!
!path Bradycardia or hypotension during endoscopy
Recognise | vagal reflex from oesophageal stretch
Act | withdraw the scope ; it usually settles
If it persists | atropine (or glycopyrrolate) ; reassess before continuing
!!
!path Broken or avulsed tooth
Retrieve | find the fragment ; check it has not been aspirated (chest radiograph if missing)
Act | replant an avulsed adult tooth at once ; urgent dental or maxillofacial opinion ; wire fixation if needed ; document and tell the patient
!!

# Aftercare | Watch for mediastinitis.
## After the procedure
Most patients go home the same day after several hours' observation, starting with clear fluids. Before discharge, look for fever, tachycardia, odynophagia, chest pain and crepitus in the neck; tell the patient to return at once if any develop. Antibiotics and strong analgesia are not normally needed. **Unexpected pain or fever after oesophagoscopy is a perforation until proven otherwise.**
## Complications
!table Complications
Complication | Notes
Dental injury | Chipped, loosened or avulsed teeth; dentist; wire fixation
Perforation | About 1% overall; up to 6% with dilatation or foreign body removal; more with rigid scopes and inexperienced operators; usually at the cricopharyngeus
Bleeding | From biopsies; usually minor
Arrhythmia, hypotension | Vagal; withdraw the scope, atropine
Pneumothorax, mediastinitis | Consequences of perforation
Epistaxis | Transnasal endoscopy
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: lists rigid, flexible and transnasal oesophagoscopy, with contraindications including trismus, cervical spine disease, a receding mandible and aortic aneurysm; positions the head raised with the neck flexed and the head extended at the atlanto-occipital joint, passes the scope in four steps (arytenoids, cricopharyngeal sphincter, aortic arch and left bronchus at 25 cm, cardia, with the scope pointing to the left anterior superior iliac spine), and recognises the cardia by redder, velvety mucosa.
Lee: emphasises panendoscopy in head and neck cancer and the 15, 25 and 40 cm constrictions.
Myers compares rigid and flexible approaches, describes transnasal oesophagoscopy step by step, and quantifies perforation risk (about 1%, up to 6% with dilatation or foreign body removal).
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- A shoulder roll that extends the neck and kinks the passage.
- Levering on the upper incisors.
- Pushing against a closed cricopharyngeus.
- Advancing without seeing the lumen, or against resistance.
- Biopsying first and examining through blood.
- Sending home a patient with unexplained pain or fever.
!!
