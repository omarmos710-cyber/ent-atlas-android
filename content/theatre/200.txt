@stand The thyroid begins at the foramen caecum on the tongue and descends through the neck, passing in front of the hyoid, to settle over the trachea. The track it leaves behind normally disappears; when part of it persists and fills with secretion, it forms a thyroglossal duct cyst, the commonest congenital midline neck lump in children. Removing only the cyst is the classic mistake: it recurs in roughly half of cases, because the track runs through the hyoid and up into the tongue. Sistrunk's operation removes the cyst, the middle of the hyoid and a core of tongue up toward the foramen caecum in one piece, and brings recurrence down to about 5%. This chapter covers the diagnosis, the Sistrunk procedure, the traction-suture technique for recurrent and high-risk cysts, and the rare cancer found in the specimen.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Sistrunk procedure for thyroglossal duct cyst
 | Summary
Indications | A midline neck mass (for diagnosis and treatment); recurrent infection; failed previous excision or incision and drainage; cosmetic concern; possible malignancy
Contraindications | Comorbidities that make anaesthesia and surgery unsafe; inadequate support for recovery
Anaesthesia | General; a nasal RAE tube helps if you need to work through the mouth; ask that the patient is not paralysed so the hypoglossal nerve can be watched
Position | Supine, shoulder roll, neck extended; lower face and neck prepared
Antibiotics | Clean-contaminated (the pharynx may be entered): at induction and for 24 hours (clindamycin, ampicillin-sulbactam, or cefazolin with metronidazole)
Monitoring | Standard
Imaging | **Ultrasound** first: a thin-walled, anechoic midline cyst, and a normal thyroid in its usual place; CT or MRI for extensive or unclear lesions (MRI can trace the track on fat-saturated T2); nuclear scans now rarely needed
Tray | Basic soft tissue set; bone cutters or hyoid scissors; headlight; for recurrent or high-risk cysts, a Jennings mouth gag, perforating towel clip, 3.5 inch 18 gauge spinal needle, heavy (No. 2) braided polyester suture, tonsil clamp, and a sterile button or short piece of 14 French catheter; quarter-inch Penrose drain
Before theatre | Stop anti-inflammatories and other drugs that increase bleeding; medical clearance if needed; treat infection first
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient (or parents)
- **What it is:** removing the cyst together with the middle part of the small bone above it (the hyoid) and a core of tissue from the base of the tongue, because the root of the cyst runs through them.
- **Why not just the cyst:** removing only the cyst lets it come back in about half of cases; this operation lowers that to about 1 in 20.
- **Recurrence** is still possible, usually within months, and needs another operation.
- **Bleeding**, rarely at the tongue base, where a large clot can block the airway; a drain is often used and the patient may stay overnight.
- **Infection**, treated with antibiotics or by opening the wound.
- **A small leak of saliva** into the neck if the throat is opened, which usually closes within days.
- **The scar** in a neck crease, and a dimple if the muscles do not fill the gap.
- **Rarely, a thyroid cancer** is found in the cyst; this operation is usually enough treatment for it.
!!
## Before you start
### The history
- A midline lump, often enlarging quickly after a cold or sore throat; painful, red and warm if infected.
- **Previous infections**, antibiotics, and any incision and drainage (often from a misdiagnosed abscess) or previous excision.
- Swallowing difficulty, painful swallowing or stridor if large.
- Vitamin E, anti-inflammatories, herbal products; antibiotic allergies.
### The examination
- A smooth midline lump, usually 1 to 2 cm (up to 10 cm), most often **just below the hyoid** at the thyrohyoid membrane (about 65%), sometimes above the hyoid or at its level.
- It **moves up on swallowing and on poking out the tongue**, because it is tethered to the hyoid and the tongue.
- Non-tender unless infected; look for **previous scars and draining sinuses**.
- Look in the mouth at the base of the tongue.
!table Midline neck lumps in children
Lesion | Clue
Thyroglossal duct cyst | Moves with tongue protrusion and swallowing; cystic; most common (about 55% to 70%)
Dermoid cyst | Does not move with the tongue; contains cheesy, whitish material
Ectopic thyroid | Solid; may be the only thyroid tissue
Lymph node | Firm, often with infection elsewhere
Plunging ranula, lipoma, haemangioma | Each with its own imaging pattern
!!
!flag The only thyroid
Before removing a solid midline mass, make sure the patient has a normal thyroid elsewhere on ultrasound. Removing ectopic thyroid that is the only thyroid tissue leaves the patient permanently hypothyroid.
!!
### Imaging clues to cancer
Carcinoma (almost always papillary) occurs in about 1%, more often in adults. Worry about **solid or enhancing nodules in the wall** and **calcification** (psammoma bodies, best seen on CT). In practice it is usually found unexpectedly in the specimen.

# Anatomy you must see | The track and its landmarks.
## The track
- The thyroid diverticulum starts at the **foramen caecum**, where the front two-thirds of the tongue meet the back third in the midline, and descends in front of the hyoid and the laryngeal cartilages to sit over the trachea.
- The track loops close to, and often through, the **body of the hyoid**, then runs up through the tongue muscle to the foramen caecum; its remnants and secretory tissue are scattered and **cannot be seen** at surgery, which is why the operation removes a block of tissue.
- The cyst is lined mainly by ciliated columnar epithelium and contains colloid-like fluid.
## Landmarks
- **Foramen caecum** at the tongue base, the upper limit.
- **Body of the hyoid**, of which the central 1 to 2 cm is removed (less in small children).
- **Thyroid notch**, marking the midline.
- **Cricoid cartilage**, which in a small child can sit high and be mistaken for the hyoid.
- The **lingual arteries** lie either side of the midline in the tongue base; straying sideways reaches them.
- The **vallecula** lies just behind the hyoid, covered by only a thin layer of tissue.

# The operation, stage by stage | Cyst, hyoid, tongue core.
## Incision
Infiltrate a natural upper-neck skin crease with bupivacaine and adrenaline. Make a transverse incision across the midline, usually just below the body of the hyoid; if the cyst is stuck to the skin or the skin is inflamed, include an ellipse of skin. Raise the flaps and go down to the front of the cyst, retracting the strap muscles.
!mf 1 | A, the incision in a midline skin crease. B, the cyst exposed with the straps retracted, still attached to the hyoid. C, the hyoid held and cleared. D, the hyoid divided with a bone cutter.
!see What you should see
- The cyst in the midline, with the straps on either side and a stalk running up toward the hyoid.
!!
## Cyst or dermoid?
Look at the contents if the cyst opens or you aspirate it. **Mucoid or purulent** material means a thyroglossal cyst: proceed with Sistrunk. **Thick, whitish, cheesy** material in a cyst that was never infected suggests a **dermoid**, for which simple excision is enough.
## Free the cyst but not its stalk
Free the cyst on its front, lower, side and back surfaces, but **leave its attachment to the hyoid intact**: the tissue between cyst and hyoid can hold microscopic remnants of the track. Lift the cyst and open a plane behind the body of the hyoid.
!danger The vallecula
Only a thin layer separates the back of the hyoid from the mucosa of the vallecula. Stay close to the bone, or you will enter the pharynx.
!!
## Divide the hyoid
Dissect just over the front of the hyoid in the midline, then cut the bone on each side of the midline to free a central segment of about **1 to 1.5 cm** (needle diathermy in small children; bone cutters or hyoid scissors in older patients). **Leave the soft tissue attached to the top of the hyoid**, which carries the track upward.
!see What you should see
- A free central segment of hyoid with the cyst hanging below it and soft tissue still attached above it.
!!
## Core out the tongue base
Grasp the free piece of hyoid with an Allis or Leahy clamp and pull it **forward and down**. With insulated needle diathermy, cut a **cylinder of tongue tissue about 1 cm wide** upward toward the foramen caecum, stopping just short of the mucosa or including a small disc of it. Take a more generous core if the cyst has been infected before (higher risk of recurrence). Keep checking the midline: a double-gloved finger in the mouth pressing on the foramen caecum helps.
!danger The lingual artery
Wandering off the midline in the tongue base brings you to a lingual artery and serious bleeding. Check the midline repeatedly.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
The cyst is the visible part; recurrence comes from what you cannot see. Keep everything between the cyst, the hyoid and the foramen caecum in one block, and do not trim it.
!!
## Close
If you enter the pharynx, close the mucosa with simple interrupted sutures, using the first as a traction stitch to help place the rest. Bring the strap muscles together in the midline to fill the gap left by the hyoid and avoid a dimple. Close the central part of the wound loosely over a **quarter-inch Penrose drain**, which comes out the next morning, then close the skin in layers.
!see What you should see
- The specimen in one piece (cyst, central hyoid and tongue core), a closed pharynx if entered, and straps meeting in the midline.
!!

# The operation: traction suture and transhyoid pharyngotomy | Use a guiding traction suture when the risk of recurrence is high (previously operated cysts, or a first operation on a cyst that has been infected) and in large patients with a short, thick neck.
## Place the suture before preparing the neck
With the mouth held open by a Jennings gag and the tongue pulled forward with a perforating towel clip, pass a long 18 gauge spinal needle through the skin over the body of the hyoid and out through the tongue at the **foramen caecum**. Remove the stylet, thread a No. 2 braided polyester suture through the needle, catch its end in the mouth with a tonsil clamp, and withdraw the needle. Tie a **stopper** (a sterile button or a short length of 14 French catheter) to the end in the mouth, leaving a long tail brought out of the mouth and clamped. Then prepare and drape the neck.
!mf 2 | A traction suture passed with a spinal needle from the skin over the hyoid to the foramen caecum, to guide the tongue base resection.
## Use the suture
During the operation, pull the suture into the wound and pull it forward and down while you core out the tongue around it. It keeps the core in the midline and delivers the upper tongue base into the wound, greatly improving the view of the top of the resection. For a recurrent cyst, include the tongue mucosa at the foramen caecum, then cut the suture and have the anaesthetist remove the part in the mouth. Close the pharyngotomy carefully in layers with interrupted absorbable sutures over a Penrose drain. Alternatively, a curved retractor placed through the mouth into the vallecula pushes the tongue base into the wound.
!mf 3 | The traction suture delivering the upper tongue base into the wound for the top of the resection.
!mf 4 | For a recurrent cyst, the core includes a cuff of tongue with its mucosa; the pharyngotomy is closed in layers.
!see What you should see
- A core of tongue centred on the suture, reaching the foramen caecum, and a secure layered closure.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Swelling under the chin or difficulty breathing after surgery
Suspect | a haematoma at the tongue base, which can obstruct the airway catastrophically
Act | open the wound and release the clot ; call for anaesthetic help ; return to theatre to control the bleeding
!!
!path Saliva leaking from the wound
Cause | breakdown of the tongue base closure (pharyngocutaneous fistula)
Act | oral antibiotics and dressings ; most close within days in a healthy patient
!!
!path The lump comes back
Cause | secretory tissue left in the tongue base (usually, if the hyoid was already removed), forming a pseudocyst anywhere in the front of the neck, mostly within months
Act | re-excision with a suture-guided transhyoid pharyngotomy or another approach to the tongue base
!!
!path Papillary carcinoma in the specimen
Act | for a cancer confined to the cyst, the Sistrunk procedure is usually adequate ; assess the thyroid with ultrasound and discuss in a multidisciplinary meeting for further treatment if needed
!!

# Aftercare | Often a day case.
## After the operation
- Paracetamol, ibuprofen, and an opioid if needed.
- Soft diet for a few days if preferred.
- Drain out the next morning; some patients can go home the same day.
## Complications
!table Complications of the Sistrunk procedure
Complication | Notes
Recurrence | About 5% after Sistrunk, much higher after cyst excision alone; higher after infection
Haematoma | Rare, but tongue base bleeding can obstruct the airway; loose central closure over a drain
Lingual artery bleeding | Dissection off the midline
Infection | Oral antibiotics; drainage if severe
Pharyngocutaneous fistula | Usually closes within days
Skin buttonholing, dimpled scar | Adequate incision; straps brought together
!!
## Alternatives
Percutaneous ethanol sclerotherapy has had some success in small series and may suit poor surgical candidates, but it can cause inflammation, haematoma, fever and allergy, and leaked sclerosant scars the field if surgery is later needed. Endoscopic transoral excision avoids a scar but has no long-term results yet.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: shows the sites of thyroglossal cysts along the track (base of tongue, suprahyoid, subhyoid as the commonest, over the thyroid cartilage, and in front of the cricoid), with the classic sign of movement on protruding the tongue.
Lee: explains the embryology: the thyroid begins at the foramen caecum, descends in front of the hyoid, and any part of the track can form a cyst closely related to the hyoid; the lateral thyroid primordia come from the fourth and fifth pouches, the C cells from the ultimobranchial bodies, and a pyramidal lobe is present in up to 40%.
Myers gives the full Sistrunk technique: deciding cyst versus dermoid from the contents, keeping the cyst attached to the hyoid, removing 1 to 1.5 cm of central hyoid with the tissue above it, a 1 cm core of tongue to the foramen caecum, the suture-guided transhyoid pharyngotomy for recurrent and high-risk cases, and the airway risk of a tongue base haematoma.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Excising the cyst alone.
- Mistaking the cricoid for the hyoid in a small child.
- Separating the cyst from the hyoid, or the hyoid from the tissue above it.
- Entering the vallecula behind the hyoid.
- Straying from the midline into the lingual artery.
- Too small a tongue core, especially after infection.
- Leaving a midline dimple by not closing the straps.
- Removing the only thyroid tissue.
!!
