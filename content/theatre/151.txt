@stand In thyroid eye disease the orbital fat and muscles swell inside a fixed bony box, pushing the eye forwards and, at worst, squeezing the optic nerve at the apex. Orbital decompression removes walls of that box so the contents expand into the sinuses or the temporal fossa. The ENT surgeon's part is the endoscopic medial wall and floor; the lateral wall is often added by the oculoplastic surgeon for a "balanced" decompression that reduces proptosis with less double vision.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Endoscopic orbital decompression
 | Summary
Urgent indications (active disease) | Compressive optic neuropathy not responding to intravenous steroids; corneal exposure and ulceration
Elective indications (inactive disease, stable for about 6 months) | Disfiguring proptosis, pressure or pain, dry exposed eyes, globe subluxation
Approaches | Endoscopic medial wall and medial floor; lateral wall through a lateral canthal or lid incision; fat decompression; combinations ("balanced" medial and lateral)
Sequence of rehabilitation | Decompression first, then squint surgery, then eyelid surgery
Anaesthesia | General, hypotensive
Tray | Endoscopes, navigation, sinus set, sickle knife or beaver blade for the periorbita, Kerrison rongeurs, drill
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Double vision** may appear or worsen (especially after medial decompression), sometimes needing squint surgery.
- **Numbness** of the cheek or teeth (infraorbital nerve) if the floor is taken.
- **Sinusitis** from obstructed drainage.
- **CSF leak, bleeding, orbital haematoma,** and rarely **loss of vision.**
- The eye may still look different; eyelid surgery may follow.
!!
## Before you start
Work with the ophthalmologist: activity score, visual acuity, colour vision, fields, pupils, Hertel exophthalmometry and eye movements. Review CT for the sinuses, skull base, the ethmoidal arteries and the bulk of the apex. Treat active disease medically first unless sight is threatened.

# The operation, stage by stage | Medial wall and floor.
## Open the sinuses widely
Decongest; do a septoplasty first if needed. Trim the lower part of the middle turbinate for room, remove the uncinate and enlarge the maxillary antrostomy backwards and upwards to show the junction of the medial wall and floor. Complete the ethmoidectomy front to back and open the sphenoid widely; expose, but do not dissect, the frontal recess.
!see What you should see
- The whole lamina papyracea from the frontal recess to the sphenoid face, and the orbital floor from the antrum.
!!
## Remove the lamina papyracea
Fracture the lamina anteriorly with an elevator and lift the fragments off the periorbita, removing bone back to the sphenoid face, up to the skull base and down to the junction with the floor.
!see What you should see
- Intact periorbita bulging into the ethmoid along the whole medial wall.
!!
!danger Preserve a strut if you can
Removing the bone at the junction of the medial wall and floor (the inferomedial strut) lets the globe drop and increases diplopia. Many surgeons keep it unless maximum decompression is needed for optic neuropathy.
!!
## Take the medial floor
With angled rongeurs remove the floor medial to the infraorbital nerve; drill the thick bone at the junction if it is being removed. Going lateral to the nerve adds little and risks numbness and a sunken eye.
!see What you should see
- The orbital floor removed up to, but not beyond, the infraorbital canal.
!!
## Open the periorbita
Incise the periorbita in parallel strips from back to front, starting superiorly so herniating fat does not obscure the view, with a sickle knife, keeping the cuts shallow over the medial rectus. Massage the globe gently to help fat prolapse into the ethmoid.
!mf 3 4 5 6 | Optic neuropathy before decompression, and the medial wall and floor removed on CT afterwards.
!see What you should see
- Orbital fat prolapsing into the ethmoid, and a softer, less proptotic eye.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
For compressive optic neuropathy the apex matters most: take bone right back to the sphenoid face and open the periorbita posteriorly. For cosmetic proptosis, a balanced medial and lateral decompression gives the reduction with less risk of new double vision.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Bleeding obscures the field
Act | identify and coagulate the anterior ethmoidal or sphenopalatine artery ; head up, controlled hypotension
!!
!path Vision falls after surgery
Act | remove any packing ; assess for haematoma ; canthotomy if tight ; urgent ophthalmology
!!

# Aftercare | Expect swelling, watch the eye.
## After surgery
No nose blowing for 2 weeks; saline douching; antibiotics if indicated; check vision and eye movements. Squint and lid surgery wait until the decompression has settled, usually months.
## Complications
New or worse diplopia, infraorbital numbness, sinusitis, CSF leak, orbital haematoma, vision loss, hypoglobus.
!update Current evidence
A systematic review found only limited randomised evidence comparing decompression techniques, with outcomes and complications varying by approach (Boboridis et al., *Adv Ther* 2015). In 247 patients, new diplopia in primary gaze followed decompression in 22%; preoperative diplopia in side gaze was the main risk factor, and medial-plus-lateral decompression gave more proptosis reduction than lateral alone (Rocchi et al., *Thyroid* 2012). Balanced endoscopic medial and transcutaneous lateral decompression improved compressive optic neuropathy in all cases in one series (Hernández-García et al., *Acta Otolaryngol* 2017).
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Myers: endoscopic medial wall and medial floor decompression with periorbital incisions, combined with lateral decompression as needed, urgently for optic neuropathy and electively for proptosis.
K. J. Lee: the walls that can be removed and the balanced approach to reduce diplopia.
The literature: diplopia after decompression is common and predicted by pre-existing restriction.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Indications**: urgent versus elective.
- **Which walls** and why balance matters.
- The **steps** and the periorbital incisions.
- **Complications**, especially diplopia.
- The **order** of rehabilitative surgery.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Decompressing in active disease without a sight-threatening reason.
- Opening the periorbita inferiorly first, so fat hides the field.
- Cutting deep over the medial rectus.
- Obstructing the frontal recess.
!!
