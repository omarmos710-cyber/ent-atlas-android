@stand Myringoplasty repairs the eardrum; tympanoplasty repairs the drum and also deals with the middle ear behind it, whether that is chronic infection, cholesteatoma or a broken ossicular chain, which usually means lifting a tympanomeatal flap. Both aim at the same three things: a dry ear, an intact drum (so the patient can get it wet again), and hearing kept or improved. Every technique, inlay, underlay or overlay, succeeds in more than nine out of ten ears in skilled hands; what decides the result is the surgeon's precision and the choice of patient. This chapter takes you from the assessment of a perforation, through paper patching, fat and cartilage butterfly grafts, to the lateral (overlay) and medial (underlay) techniques, and the complications each one invites.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Myringoplasty and tympanoplasty
 | Summary
Indications | A perforation that restricts water activities; recurrent discharge or recurrent ear infections; to improve hearing
Contraindications | An only hearing ear (prefer a hearing aid unless active disease persists despite maximal treatment); active vestibular disease; active discharge not yet settled (dry the ear first)
Anaesthesia | General for most; intravenous sedation with local anaesthetic for a limited, transcanal repair in a cooperative patient
Position | Supine, bed turned 180 degrees with the feet toward the anaesthetist, head turned away
Antibiotics | Not routinely needed; a single dose of cefazolin (clindamycin if allergic) for an infected ear or when a prosthesis is placed
Monitoring | Facial nerve monitoring optional; not the standard of care for this operation
Imaging | CT only if cholesteatoma or mastoid disease is suspected; MRI for imbalance, vertigo or asymmetric sensorineural loss
Tray | Middle ear instrument set; round knives, picks, cup forceps, Freer elevator; Lempert speculum; high-speed drill with cutting and diamond burs for canaloplasty or mastoidectomy; ossicular prostheses if reconstruction is planned; Gelfoam, Silastic sheet
Before theatre | Dry the ear with drops or systemic antibiotics; read every previous operation note (local graft tissue may be used up); confirm the patient will come back for follow-up
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **What it does:** closes the hole in the eardrum, and checks and repairs the middle ear behind it, aiming for a dry ear that can get wet and, if possible, better hearing.
- **The graft may not take**, leaving or creating a hole that needs another operation; more likely after previous surgery or with a poorly working Eustachian tube.
- **Hearing may not improve**, may get worse, and very rarely is lost completely.
- **Taste change** on the side of the tongue if the taste nerve is stretched or cut; usually temporary.
- **Dizziness**, usually short-lived; **tinnitus**.
- **Facial weakness:** very rare.
- **Numbness of the top of the ear** after an incision behind it.
- **Late problems:** the drum healing away from the bone (lateralisation), scarring in the front angle (blunting), and small skin cysts.
- **Water precautions** for several weeks to months.
!!
## Before you start
### The history
- **Hearing loss:** sudden or gradual.
- **Discharge:** chronic or recurrent.
- **Trauma**, such as a slap, a cotton bud, barotrauma, diving or forceful syringing.
- **Pain** is not typical of a simple perforation: look for another cause in the head and neck.
- **Vertigo** must be investigated before surgery; do not operate on an ear with an active vestibular problem.
- Previous ear surgery and grommets; **cleft palate** repair (tube dysfunction); severe seasonal allergy; anticoagulants; smoking.
### The examination
Use the microscope.
- **The perforation:** size (small, subtotal, total); site (central or **marginal**); a "trail" of keratin running from a marginal edge means squamous epithelium in the middle ear.
- Wet or dry; the middle ear mucosa; cholesteatoma, hypertrophic mucosa or granulation suggests a mastoidectomy may also be needed.
- **The other ear:** retraction, atelectasis, effusion and tympanosclerosis point to poor tube function, which predicts a poorer result, especially in children.
### The audiogram
A small perforation can have normal hearing, and a near-total one causes about **35 to 40 dB** of conductive loss. A bigger gap than the hole explains points to an **ossicular** break or fixation, which you must plan for. In mixed loss, closing the gap may still leave the patient needing a hearing aid; but if a severe mixed loss is too much for a comfortable aid, correcting the conductive part can help rehabilitation.
!flag Who is less likely to succeed
Multiple failed previous repairs, persistent Eustachian tube dysfunction, severe disease in the other ear, and palatal problems. Tell these patients beforehand, and consider cartilage.
!!

# Anatomy you must see | The drum, the canal and what lies behind.
## The tympanic membrane
- **Pars tensa** and **pars flaccida**; three layers: outer squamous epithelium, middle fibrous layer, inner mucosa.
- The **fibrous annulus** sits in the bony **sulcus** of the tympanic ring.
- **Central** perforations have a rim of drum all round; **marginal** ones reach the annulus.
## The canal
- The **bony-cartilaginous junction** is where the canal block is injected.
- The **tympanomastoid** and **tympanosquamous suture lines** guide the vascular strip incisions.
- The **anterior canal bulge** often hides the anterior annulus; it must be drilled for lateral grafting.
- The **linea temporalis** marks the upper limit of the periosteal flap; the **temporomandibular joint** lies in front of the anterior canal wall.
## The middle ear
- **Ossicles:** malleus (head, neck, handle, lateral and anterior processes), incus (body, short and long processes), stapes (head, crura, footplate).
- The **chorda tympani** crosses medial to the drum, near the posterior annulus, under the tympanomeatal flap.
- The **facial nerve:** its tympanic segment above the oval window and its mastoid segment behind the posterior canal wall.
- **Tympanosclerosis:** chalky plaques in the drum, on the ossicles and the medial wall; remove plaque that stiffens the drum.

# The operation: myringoplasty | Small and traumatic perforations.
## Anaesthesia of the canal
Inject 1% to 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline in four quadrants, just lateral to the bony-cartilaginous junction, through a Lempert speculum, until the skin blanches. Inject behind the ear too if you will take fascia or use a postauricular approach.
## The fresh traumatic tear
Fold the edges out from the medial surface so no squamous epithelium is left inside, then lay them back together. Support them with Gelfoam soaked in ciprofloxacin (with or without dexamethasone) medially, and a cigarette paper, Steri-Strip or silk patch on the outer surface.
## The small chronic perforation
Touch the edges with phenol or trichloroacetic acid, then **freshen the margin all round with a sharp pick**, removing epithelium that has grown under the medial surface and making the edge bleed; then patch the outer surface.
!danger Skin left inside
Squamous epithelium left on the medial surface of the edge, or trapped under any graft, grows into a cholesteatoma. Rimming the perforation properly is the single most important step of every technique.
!!
## Fat or areolar tissue graft
If patching fails, prepare the edges the same way, fill the middle ear behind the hole with Gelfoam, and pass a dumbbell of fat from the ear lobe through the perforation, or place compressed areolar tissue or fascia medial to it, held by Gelfoam on both sides.

# The operation: cartilage butterfly (inlay) | Eavey's butterfly graft suits small to medium perforations whose **whole edge can be seen through the canal**. With no canal incisions and no packing, there is less pain, the hearing improves at once, and the operation is short. (Because no tympanomeatal flap is raised, some call it a myringoplasty.)
## Rim and measure
Inject the canal and the tragus. Rim the perforation with a curved pick, without enlarging it beyond what you can see through the canal. Measure it with a right-angled hook or round knife.
!mf 6 | Measuring the perforation with a right-angled hook; the exposed malleus and tympanosclerosis help lock the graft.
## Harvest and shape the cartilage
Incise the back (posteromedial) surface of the tragus through skin and cartilage in one stroke with a size 15 blade, keeping the dome of the tragus for cosmesis; free the cartilage, keeping perichondrium on one or both sides, and close with one or two plain gut stitches. Cut the cartilage to the **exact shape** of the perforation but **1 to 2 mm larger**. With a blade, cut a groove about **2 mm deep** all round its edge, keeping the two flanges equal, so it opens like butterfly wings.
!mf 7 | The tragal cartilage shaped 1 to 2 mm larger than the perforation, with a groove cut all round its edge so it opens like butterfly wings.
## Snap it in
Pass the graft down the canal and seat it so one flange lies **medial** to the edge of the perforation and the other, with perichondrium, **lateral**. Check every point of the rim, then press gently on the centre: the whole drum should move with it.
!mf 8 | The butterfly graft in place, one edge lifted to show the two flanges.
!see What you should see
- A graft that sits flush in the hole all round, with the medial flange under the drum remnant at every point.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Sizing makes or breaks this graft: too large and it will not snap in, too small and it falls into the middle ear. Use the same instrument to measure the hole and to mark the cartilage.
!!

# The operation: lateral (overlay) graft | The lateral graft suits **total and near-total perforations**, revisions, and drums thick with plaque or scarred remnants that need removing.
## Vascular strip and postauricular approach
Under the microscope, rinse the canal and make **vascular strip incisions** along the tympanomastoid and tympanosquamous suture lines (a semilunar incision joining them just lateral to the annulus is optional; leaving it out lets the strip lift the outer layer of the drum remnant with it). Make a postauricular incision a few millimetres behind the crease, ending just below the lobule and **never beyond the mastoid tip**, where the facial nerve becomes superficial in children. Dissect forward in the loose areolar plane over the temporalis fascia until the posterior canal skin is a few millimetres thick, and place a self-retaining retractor.
!mf 9 | The vascular strip incisions along the tympanomastoid and tympanosquamous suture lines.
## Harvest the fascia
Incise the true temporalis fascia (not the muscle) for 2 cm at its lower end, lift it off the muscle with a Freer, and cut a 2 by 2 cm piece; the loose areolar layer over it can be used if it is thick enough. Clean off muscle and fat on a block, press it flat and let it dry under a lamp. Make a 7- or T-shaped periosteal incision along the linea temporalis and down the skin incision, and lift the periosteal flap into the retractor with the ear.
## Lift the canal skin
Complete the elevation of the vascular strip and fold it out of the canal into the retractor. With a large round knife, lift the rest of the canal skin and periosteum from the annulus **outward** to the bony-cartilaginous junction, leaving it attached there and folding it out. If the outer layer of the drum remnant stays behind, remove it separately.
!mf 10 | The periosteum and canal skin lifted from the annulus outward, toward the cartilaginous canal.
## Drill the canal
Widen the bony canal with a size 3 cutting bur then a size 2 diamond with continuous suction irrigation, removing the **anterior and inferior bulges** until you can see the **whole annulus** at once and the anterior angle is opened. Keep away from the facial nerve posteriorly and the temporomandibular joint anteriorly.
!mf 11 | Drilling away the anterior and inferior canal bulges with suction irrigation.
!danger Blunting
A canal left narrow, or a graft that rides up onto the anterior or inferior canal wall instead of lying on the annulus, scars across the anterior angle, which blunts and thickens the drum and costs hearing. Drill until the whole annulus is in view, and keep the graft off the bone.
!!
## De-epithelialise the remnant
Starting in front with a round knife along the bony annulus, scrape the squamous layer off the drum remnant all round, leaving the annulus and fibrous layer intact, and clear the rim of the perforation with cup forceps. For a total perforation or a thin graft, fill the middle ear with small Gelfoam pledgets.
!danger Pearls and cholesteatoma
Any squamous epithelium left under a lateral graft becomes an intratympanic cyst or cholesteatoma. Take your time over this step.
!!
## Place the graft
Trim the dried fascia to an oval about 1.3 by 1.5 cm and cut a slit at 12 o'clock. Wet it, and lay it **lateral** to the annulus and the cleaned remnant, with the slit tucked **medial to the malleus handle** (in contact with the tensor tendon, in Sheehy's description). Overlap the two edges of the slit, or fold the anterior flap back over the handle. Smooth the graft onto the annulus in front and below, **keeping it off the bony canal**, and bring any excess onto the posterior wall. If the malleus is missing, cut two slits to make a tongue that tucks under the scutum into the attic, then overlap the side flaps over it.
!mf 12 | The dried fascia trimmed to an oval with a slit at 12 o'clock for the malleus handle.
!mf 13 | The graft lateral to the annulus with its slit medial to the handle.
!mf 14 | The anterior flap folded back over the exposed handle, or both flaps overlapped leaving only the umbo exposed.
!mf 15 | With no malleus, two cuts create a tongue to tuck into the attic.
!mf 16 | The tongue tucked into the attic and the flaps overlapped to seal the top of the graft.
!danger Lateralisation
A graft not placed medial to the malleus handle drifts outward from the ossicles over 6 to 12 months, and the hearing falls. The handle anchors the graft.
!!
## Replace the skin and pack
Lay the canal skin back on the bone, overlapping the graft by about 1 mm, without folding its edges under (folded edges form canal cysts). Roll a dry cigar of Gelfoam into the anterior sulcus, or place a piece of thick Silastic over the graft, then Gelfoam on top. Release the retractors, return the vascular strip, close the periosteal flap with one stitch to steady the ear, check through the speculum that the strip is in place with its edges everted, fill the canal with Gelfoam and place a cotton ball. Close the periosteum and skin in layers with absorbable sutures and glue, and apply a mastoid dressing for 24 hours.
!mf 17 | The canal skin returned over the bone, overlapping the fascia by about 1 mm.
!see What you should see
- The graft flat on the annulus all round, medial to the handle, clear of the anterior canal wall, and the canal skin lying flat with no edges turned under.
!!

# The operation: medial (underlay) graft | The medial graft suits most central perforations with an adequate rim, infrequent discharge and little middle ear disease. If the whole perforation is visible through the canal, the operation can be transcanal, with a small postauricular incision just for the fascia.
## Flap and middle ear
Inject, rim the perforation, make the vascular strip incisions and join them with a **semilunar incision about 4 mm lateral to the annulus**. Harvest fascia as above. Lift the **tympanomeatal flap** with the annulus out of its sulcus with a round knife until you enter the middle ear, finding and preserving the **chorda tympani**. Inspect and feel the ossicles: if the chain is stiff, separate the incudostapedial joint to tell whether the stapes or the malleus and incus are fixed. Clear granulation, hyperplastic mucosa, cholesteatoma or plaque that limits movement.
## Place the graft
Fill the middle ear with Gelfoam as a bed, wet the graft, and place it against the **medial** surface of the perforation, tucked under the remnant all round. Lay the flap back and hold everything with Gelfoam lateral to the drum.
!mf 18 | The medial graft: after freshening the edges and raising the tympanomeatal flap, the graft is placed medial to the perforation on a bed of Gelfoam.
## The over-under graft
When the perforation reaches the malleus handle, scrape the periosteum and any drum remnant off the handle (incise its periosteum with a sickle knife and peel from the umbo up to the lateral process), then place the graft **lateral to ("over") the handle** and **medial to ("under") the annulus and remnant**. Put Gelfoam over the handle to press the graft onto the bone.
!see What you should see
- A graft lying under the remnant all round, supported by Gelfoam, with the flap returned exactly and the chorda intact.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Underlay grafts fail anteriorly, where the remnant is thin and the canal bulge hides the edge. The over-under technique, a little more Gelfoam anteriorly, or a cartilage graft for a large or high-risk anterior perforation, all reduce this.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path The face is weak in recovery
First | wait for the local anaesthetic to wear off before acting
If still weak | remove the packing ; examine the nerve's course ; steroids ; imaging and exploration if no improvement or the nerve was not seen
!!
!path The canal is narrowing after lateral grafting
Recognise | soft-tissue stenosis across the mid canal, sometimes looking like lateralisation
Act | early debridement and stenting in clinic ; silver nitrate to granulation ; keep or replace the Silastic stent
!!
!path A small white cyst appears on the graft or canal
Recognise | an epithelial pearl from trapped or folded-in skin
Act | open and marsupialise it in clinic while small
!!
!path The hearing drops months after a lateral graft
Suspect | lateralisation (graft lifted off the malleus) or anterior blunting
Act | microscopy and audiogram ; revision surgery if hearing is significantly affected
!!

# Aftercare | Weeks of patience.
## After the operation
- **Paper patch:** keep the ear dry and review until the patch migrates onto the canal wall, usually 6 to 12 weeks.
- **Packed myringoplasty:** remove the packing at 7 to 10 days.
- **Cartilage butterfly:** no drops needed; review at about 3 weeks; keep dry until healed, usually 3 to 4 weeks.
- **Lateral and medial grafts:** remove the mastoid dressing the next day; the wound can get wet after 24 hours with glue, but keep the canal dry with a Vaseline-coated cotton ball. Start drops twice a day a week before the review at about 3 weeks; they soften the Gelfoam so it can be suctioned out.
- **Silastic after lateral grafting** can be removed at the first review, or left as a stent for 6 to 8 weeks if there is concern about stenosis, blunting or lateralisation.
- The drum should be intact by **3 weeks**; check mobility with pneumatic otoscopy and do tuning forks; **audiogram at 2 to 3 months**.
- **Water restrictions lift** at about 2 months after medial grafting and 3 to 4 months after lateral grafting.
## Complications
!table Complications of myringoplasty and tympanoplasty
Complication | Cause or prevention
Graft failure, persistent perforation | Poor rimming; tube dysfunction; previous surgery; mucosal disease
Lateralisation | Lateral graft not placed medial to the malleus; 6 to 12 months later
Anterior blunting | Narrow canal or graft on the anterior canal wall
Epithelial pearls and cholesteatoma | Skin left medial to the graft or folded under
Canal stenosis | Healing of the canal after lateral grafting
Taste disturbance | Chorda tympani stretched or cut
Sensorineural loss, vertigo, tinnitus | Manipulation of the ossicles, drilling on the chain
Facial weakness | Local anaesthetic (wait), or injury
Wound infection | Uncommon
!!
## Choosing a technique and material
!table Techniques compared
Technique | Best for | Watch for
Paper patch | Small fresh traumatic or small chronic perforation | Repeated application may be needed
Fat plug | Small central perforation after a failed patch | Small holes only
Cartilage butterfly | Small to medium perforations visible through the canal | Exact sizing
Medial (underlay) | Most central perforations with a rim | Anterior failure, narrowed middle ear space
Lateral (overlay) | Total and subtotal, revisions, diseased remnants | Blunting, lateralisation, pearls; harder to learn
!!
Success exceeds 90% with any technique in skilled hands, and some studies give 97% or more for button, overlay and underlay grafts alike; one large series slightly favoured lateral grafts. Graft materials give similar hearing, but cartilage gives a better-looking, more durable drum and is preferred for **high-risk ears** (atelectasis, cholesteatoma, revisions). Routine mastoidectomy with tympanoplasty has not clearly improved closure, though it may reduce revisions; add it for severe mucosal disease or cholesteatoma.
## Alternatives
Observation, or a hearing aid with water precautions, for any patient. A conductive loss over 30 to 35 dB usually limits function enough that observation alone is not reasonable, though an aid avoids surgical risk at the cost of batteries, breakdowns, background noise and feedback. A staged operation with Silastic in the middle ear may be needed to rebuild an aerated space first.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: separates **myringoplasty** (closing the drum without inspecting the middle ear) from **tympanoplasty** (clearing disease and rebuilding the hearing mechanism) and gives the Wullstein types; lists contraindications to myringoplasty (discharge, uncontrolled nasal allergy, otitis externa, squamous ingrowth, an only hearing ear, and young age); compares underlay grafting (narrower middle ear, anterior reperforation) with overlay (blunting, pearls, lateralisation); and uses a paper patch with cautery of the rim, or a fat graft, for small holes.
Lee: covers tympanoplasty with ossicular reconstruction, noting that more patients reach a gap under 20 dB with a partial than a total prosthesis, and that graft material matters less than technique.
Myers describes three techniques in operative detail: the cartilage butterfly with exact sizing, Sheehy's lateral graft with vascular strip, canaloplasty and de-epithelialisation, and the medial graft with the over-under modification; it stresses rimming the perforation as the essential step in all of them, and the specific timetable for drops, reviews and lifting water restrictions.
!!

!exam What the examiner is looking for

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Inadequate rimming.
- Not checking the other ear and tube function.
- Missing an ossicular problem the audiogram predicted.
- A narrow canal or a graft on the anterior canal wall.
- Leaving skin under the graft or folding canal skin edges under.
- A lateral graft not medial to the malleus handle.
- Extending the postauricular incision below the mastoid tip in a child.
- Wrongly sized cartilage.
!!
