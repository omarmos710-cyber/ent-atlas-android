@stand Total ear reconstruction for microtia carves a new ear from the child's own rib cartilage and slips it under the thin skin of the side of the head, then over two or three further stages turns the lobule, deepens the concha and lifts the ear off the scalp. It is as much sculpture as surgery. The ENT surgeon's part is to time it with hearing rehabilitation, never to burn the skin that will cover the framework, and to place any canal or bone conduction implant where it will not ruin the reconstruction.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Microtia reconstruction with rib cartilage
 | Summary
Indications | Microtia in a child or adult who wants an ear, with realistic expectations
Timing | When the chest has enough cartilage and the child is aware of the difference, commonly from about 6 to 10 years; before school teasing if possible
Alternatives | Watchful waiting; a silicone prosthesis (glued or on osseointegrated anchors); a porous polyethylene framework covered with a temporoparietal fascia flap; fewer-stage autologous techniques (Nagata)
Stages | Three-stage (framework; lobule transposition and conchal deepening; elevation with a skin graft) or four-stage
Anaesthesia | General, with an epidural or paravertebral block for the chest
Antibiotics | Perioperative (cefazolin); dexamethasone
Tray | Rib harvest set, cartilage carving instruments, sterilised template from the opposite ear, 4-0 clear nylon, drains
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the family
- **Several operations,** about two months apart, and the result is an ear that looks natural at conversation distance, not an exact copy.
- **Chest:** pain, a scar, a chest wall depression, and a small risk of **pneumothorax.**
- **Skin loss** over the framework, exposing cartilage; it may need a flap to cover it.
- **Infection, haematoma or seroma.**
- **Asymmetry,** scarring, and a framework that loses definition over time.
- Hearing is not changed by the reconstruction itself.
!!
## Before you start
Assess hearing in both ears and fit a bone conduction aid on a softband early if needed. Plan with the otologist: a bone-anchored implant or atresiaplasty must not be sited where the framework will go, and atresiaplasty is usually done after the framework. Make a template of the opposite ear from a flipped photograph or a tracing on sterilisable film; in bilateral cases use age- and sex-appropriate dimensions. Mark the ear and the side of rib harvest before induction.

# The operation, stage by stage | Three-stage autologous reconstruction.
## Harvest the rib cartilage
Through a 3 to 4 cm incision over the seventh rib, reach ribs 6 to 8. Handle the cartilage with skin hooks to preserve perichondrium. Take the floating (eighth) rib cartilage for the helix, leaving a little cartilage over the bony junction, and the synchondrosis of ribs 6 and 7, marked from the template, for the base. Stay close to the cartilage and away from the pleura. Check for air leak, place a drain and close in layers.
!mf 1 | The synchondrosis of the sixth and seventh ribs harvested from the template outline.
!see What you should see
- Clean cartilage blocks and an intact pleura when the cavity is filled with saline and the lungs inflated.
!!
!danger Pneumothorax
Fill the wound with saline and ask for a sustained inflation: bubbles mean a pleural tear. Repair it over a catheter evacuated under positive pressure, or place a chest drain, and get a chest X-ray.
!!
## Carve the framework
Clean the cartilage of soft tissue. Cut the base from the synchondrosis using the template, thinning it if needed. Thin and curve the floating rib to form the helix and helical crus, then fix it to the edge of the base with 4-0 clear nylon, knots underneath. Sculpt the triangular fossa and scapha with a scalpel, gouge or punch, and fix a separate tragal piece.
!mf 2 | A carved framework with its helix attached.
!see What you should see
- A framework whose helix, antihelix, fossae and tragus stand out clearly; definition must be exaggerated because skin softens it.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Do not over-thin the helix. Cartilage that is too thin buckles and warps as it heals, and the ear loses its shape over the following years.
!!
## Make the pocket and insert the framework
Make a short incision in front of the vestige and undermine a skin pocket immediately beneath the subdermal plexus, 3 mm beyond the planned outline. Remove the vestigial cartilage without buttonholing the skin. Insert the framework, place a fine suction drain behind it so the skin is sucked down onto every contour, and close.
!mf 3 | Immediately after stage one, the skin draped over the framework by suction.
!see What you should see
- The skin shrink-wrapped onto the framework with good colour and capillary return.
!!
!danger The subdermal plexus
Too thick a pocket gives an ear without definition; too thin, or cauterised skin, dies and exposes the cartilage. Bipolar only, away from the skin surface.
!!
## Stage two: lobule and concha
About two months later, rotate the lobule vestige back to its correct position, tucking the lower framework into a pocket in the lobule to keep the ear's height, and deepen the conchal bowl by excising soft tissue.
!mf 4 | Lobule transposition at the second stage.
!see What you should see
- A lobule in line with the helix and a defined conchal hollow.
!!
## Stage three: elevation
Two months later, incise around the framework 2 mm outside its edge, lift it off the scalp, place a cartilage wedge behind it to set the projection, cover the back of the ear with a turnover flap or fascia, and graft the raw sulcus with full-thickness skin (often from the groin).
!mf 5 | The third stage creates the postauricular sulcus and projection.
!mf 6 | Before and after reconstruction of a grade III microtia.
!see What you should see
- An ear standing out from the head at about the same angle as the other side, with a grafted sulcus.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Skin over the framework darkens or breaks down
Small exposure | dressings and antibiotic ointment ; it may granulate
Larger | cover early with a local flap or temporoparietal fascia flap and skin graft before the cartilage is lost
!!
!path Haematoma or seroma under the skin
Act | needle aspiration or drainage ; check the drain
Infection | culture, antibiotics ; drain pus early to save the framework
!!

# Aftercare | Protect the new ear.
## After each stage
Light, non-compressive dressings; drain suction for a few days; antibiotics; no pressure on the ear while sleeping. Chest pain is the main early discomfort. Protect the ear from trauma during sport.
## Complications
Pneumothorax, chest wall deformity, infection, haematoma, skin necrosis and framework exposure, resorption or distortion of the framework, scarring and asymmetry.
!update Current evidence
**Complications by technique.** In 470 rib-cartilage reconstructions at one centre, complications occurred after 3% of Brent and 12% of Nagata first stages, with a learning curve for both (Fu et al., *Int J Pediatr Otorhinolaryngol* 2019).
**Rib or polyethylene.** In a comparison of the two frameworks, porous polyethylene needed fewer operations (about 3.4 against 4.9) and gave better definition and size match, but two of 17 implants became infected and extruded, while cartilage gave better colour match and no extrusion (Constantine et al., *JAMA Facial Plast Surg* 2014).
**Psychosocial benefit.** Prospective assessment after rib-cartilage reconstruction showed improvements in self-confidence and social life (Steffen et al., *J Plast Reconstr Aesthet Surg* 2010).
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Myers: describes the three-stage autologous technique in detail, with the four-stage Brent technique, the Nagata method, polyethylene frameworks and prostheses as alternatives.
K. J. Lee and the Oxford books: classify microtia by grade, emphasise early bone conduction hearing and coordinating any atresiaplasty after the framework.
The literature: polyethylene gives definition in fewer stages but risks extrusion; autologous reconstruction is durable with a learning curve.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Grades** of microtia and associated anomalies (atresia, first and second arch syndromes).
- **Timing** and the reasons for it.
- The **stages** and what each achieves.
- **Rib harvest** and pneumothorax.
- **Options:** rib, polyethylene, prosthesis; pros and cons.
- **Coordination** with hearing rehabilitation.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Placing a bone conduction implant where the ear will go.
- Cauterising the pocket skin.
- Building a framework without enough definition.
- Missing a pleural tear.
!!
