@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand The sphenoid is the deepest of the sinuses, and its walls are the internal carotid artery, the optic nerve, the pituitary and the skull base. The operation itself is simple: find the natural ostium, which always lies medial to the superior turbinate, and widen it. Every serious complication comes from entering the sinus somewhere else, or from working in the sinus without knowing what its walls hide. This chapter covers the two routes to the ostium (transnasal, through the sphenoethmoidal recess, and transethmoidal, through the ethmoid cavity), the landmarks that find the ostium when it is hidden, how to widen it safely, and why the inferior part of the superior turbinate can usually be taken without costing the patient their sense of smell.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Endoscopic sphenoidotomy
 | Summary
Indications | Chronic symptomatic sphenoid sinusitis after failure of maximal medical therapy; a neoplasm in the sphenoid, for biopsy or definitive treatment; CSF leak, with or without meningoencephalocele, for skull base repair; access for expanded endonasal approaches to the skull base or orbit
Contraindications | Not medically fit for surgery; a largely dehiscent carotid artery (relative)
Routes | **Transnasal (transseptal)**: through the sphenoethmoidal recess between septum and middle turbinate, for isolated sphenoid disease and sagittal-plane skull base access. **Transethmoidal**: lateral to the middle turbinate after ethmoidectomy, as part of functional sinus surgery for chronic sinusitis. Either may add a **posterior septectomy** for both sinuses. A **transpterygoid** extension reaches a pneumatised lateral recess
Anaesthesia | General; total intravenous anaesthesia may reduce bleeding; keep blood pressure and heart rate low, but keep the mean arterial pressure normal if the optic nerve is already compromised. Topical anaesthesia in the office for revision of a scarred ostium
Position | Supine, reverse Trendelenburg about 20 degrees, head turned slightly toward the surgeon
Antibiotics | First- or second-generation cephalosporin; clindamycin in penicillin allergy
Tray | Endoscopic sinus set (Cottle elevator, J curette, mushroom and Kerrison punches, straight through-cutting forceps); 0 degree 4 mm endoscope; microdebrider; image guidance with its probe; pledgets of 1:1000 adrenaline
Imaging | Thin-cut CT (under 1 to 1.25 mm) in all three planes, loaded on the navigation system
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to open the deepest sinus and let it drain, or to treat or sample a growth, or to repair a leak.
- **Bleeding,** sometimes from an artery in the back of the nose needing cautery.
- **Leak of brain fluid** and meningitis: rare.
- **Damage to the eye or the nerve of sight**, which can be permanent: rare but devastating.
- **Injury to the main artery to the brain**: very rare, life-threatening.
- **Scarring** that narrows the opening again, sometimes needing a further procedure.
- **Reduced sense of smell:** a small risk if part of the superior turbinate is removed.
- Nasal cleaning in clinic and saline rinses afterwards.
!!
## Before you start
Ask about the cardinal symptoms of chronic rhinosinusitis: obstruction, postnasal drip or discharge, loss of smell and facial pain. Sphenoid sinusitis classically, but not reliably, causes pain at the **vertex** or deep behind the eyes. Look for features that suggest something other than inflammation: loss of vision or colour vision, double vision, dry eye, an **orbital apex syndrome** (reduced vision, ophthalmoplegia, proptosis) or a **superior orbital fissure syndrome** (ophthalmoplegia, proptosis), and neurological features: confusion, facial numbness, severe headache. In chronic sinusitis, record recent antibiotics and oral steroids, topical treatment and saline, the response, asthma, allergy, aspirin sensitivity and immunodeficiency. Tailor further questions: unilateral watery rhinorrhoea or meningitis for a meningocele; immunosuppression, diabetes and country of origin for chronic invasive fungal disease.

Assess fitness for anaesthesia, and screen for systemic disease that presents in the nose: granulomatosis with polyangiitis, sarcoidosis, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg-Strauss), aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease and cystic fibrosis. Ask about previous sinus, nasal or skull base surgery and its complications: revision surgery proceeds through distorted anatomy. Ask about coagulopathy and atopy in the family, antiplatelet drugs, and herbal products such as garlic, ginseng and ginkgo.

Examine the head and neck and perform **diagnostic nasal endoscopy** in every patient. After topical anaesthetic and decongestant, pass a 30 degree scope angled upward along the floor of the nose, following the inferior turbinate, to see the **sphenoethmoidal recess**; a trail of pus from the sphenoid or posterior ethmoid may be seen there. In an inflamed nose the ostium may not be visible without moving the turbinates.
## Imaging
**CT** in axial, coronal and sagittal planes, ideally with cuts under 1 mm, defines the pneumatisation and bony walls: look for **dehiscence of the optic canal, the carotid canal or the skull base**, a deviated septum, ethmoid disease and a lateral recess. Read bone windows, and soft tissue windows when the sinus is opaque: **heterogeneous opacification** suggests a fungal ball, eosinophilic mucin or a tumour. **MRI** characterises an opaque sinus or a soft tissue lesion and shows spread outside the sinus: a suspected neoplasm, encephalocele or meningocele, or complicated sinusitis.
## Preparation
Consider oral steroids and antibiotics for 3 to 5 days beforehand to reduce inflammation and bleeding. Obtain medical clearance if needed and manage anticoagulants.

# Anatomy you must see | Finding the ostium.
## The landmarks
- **The superior turbinate:** the sphenoid ostium is **always medial** to it. Its anterior end is found by lateralising the middle turbinate (transnasal), or in the superior meatus just behind and medial to the basal lamella of the middle turbinate (transethmoidal).
- **The sphenoid face (rostrum):** in the unopened sinus it looks convex compared with the ethmoid skull base. The ostium lies in its **inferior and medial** quadrant, just lateral to where the septum joins the face.
- **The choanal arch:** the ostium is typically about **2 cm above** the arch of the choana.
- **The skull base:** the ostium averages **11 mm below the skull base**, about halfway between the floor of the sinus and the planum sphenoidale; it is closer to the skull base in a poorly pneumatised sinus.
- **The orbital floor:** after a maxillary antrostomy, the ostium lies **below** the level of the orbital floor, which is your upper limit when searching.
!mf 1 | The right sphenoid ostium medial to the superior turbinate, which is retracted laterally, after total ethmoidectomy and maxillary antrostomy.
## What surrounds the sinus
- **The planum sphenoidale:** the roof, just in front of the thick tuberculum sellae.
- **The optic nerve and lamina papyracea:** the face of the sphenoid inserts laterally onto one or both. In an **Onodi cell**, a posterior ethmoid cell pneumatised behind the coronal plane of the sphenoid face, the optic nerve may be dehiscent in its wall.
- **The internal carotid artery:** dehiscent in the sinus in **up to 22%**. The **intersinus septum inserts onto the carotid in 16%**.
- **The posterior septal branch of the sphenopalatine artery:** runs **2 to 6 mm below the ostium** in the mucosa above the choana; it is the pedicle of the nasoseptal flap, and is met when the ostium is widened downward.
- **Olfactory epithelium:** on the upper septum and the medial surfaces of the superior and middle turbinates, most concentrated in the **posterior** superior turbinate.
- **The lateral recess:** in some sinuses, pneumatised between the vidian and trigeminal nerves; reached by a transpterygoid extension.
!mf 3 | The right sphenoid sinus after sphenoidotomy: the pituitary (P) and the clival recess (C) on the posterior wall, and the impression of the optic nerve (asterisk) in the superolateral corner.

# The operation, stage by stage | Transnasal and transethmoidal.
## Review the scan and prepare the nose
Before the patient comes in, review thin-cut CT in all three planes and register it to the navigation system after intubation. For the transethmoidal route, note the ethmoid disease, the position of the anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries, and the shape and integrity of the orbit and skull base. Place pledgets of 1:1000 adrenaline on the mucosa medial to the middle turbinates.
## Transnasal: lateralise the middle turbinate
Place a Freer elevator on the lower part of the head of the middle turbinate and press it laterally, step by step from front to back, until its basal lamella is displaced and the anterior end of the **superior turbinate** comes into view.
!see What you should see
- The narrow sphenoethmoidal recess between septum and turbinates, with the superior turbinate ahead and the sphenoid face behind it.
!!
## Transethmoidal: through the ethmoid
Perform a total ethmoidectomy (see the endoscopic ethmoidectomy chapter). Lower the basal lamella of the middle turbinate to see the lower part of the superior turbinate, and clear the posterior ethmoid cells of the superior meatus so the turbinate can be moved and trimmed. This route gives a wider corridor and shows the skull base and lamina papyracea early, before the ostium is opened.
## Find the ostium
Gently move the superior turbinate laterally with a straight navigation probe or a Cottle elevator and look **medial** to it for the ostium. In the transethmoidal route with an antrostomy, keep below the level of the orbital floor.
!danger Entering the wrong place
Entering the sphenoid anywhere but its natural ostium, or mistaking an Onodi cell or posterior ethmoid cell for the sphenoid, is how the optic nerve and orbit are injured. Find the true ostium; confirm with navigation if in doubt.
!!
## Trim the superior turbinate
Remove the lowest **one fifth to one third** of the superior turbinate, which may lie below the ostium and block its drainage, with straight through-cutting forceps or a powered instrument. In the transnasal route a limited posterior ethmoidectomy, taking cells within the superior meatus, gives more room and shows the lamina papyracea and ethmoid skull base early.
!mf 2 | The right sphenoid ostium in view after resecting the lower third of the superior turbinate.
## Enter and widen
Enter the ostium bluntly with a J curette or Cottle elevator and enlarge it enough to pass a Kerrison or mushroom punch. Then widen it **laterally toward the lamina papyracea and superiorly toward the planum sphenoidale**, which gives lasting patency. Preserve mucosa, except in tumour surgery: stripped mucosa scars and closes the opening.
!see What you should see
- A wide opening into the sinus, showing its posterior wall: the pituitary, the clival recess and the optic nerve impression (figure 2).
!!
!danger Widening downward
Going down toward the nasal floor meets the **posterior septal branch of the sphenopalatine artery**. If you open toward it, cauterise the mucosa over the sphenoid face and upper choana first. Remember that it is the pedicle of a nasoseptal flap you may need later.
!!
!danger The intersinus septum and the back wall
The intersinus septum may insert on the carotid, and the carotid may be dehiscent. Reduce or remove the septum only with sharp through-cutting instruments and great caution, and do not touch the posterior wall while you are looking for or widening the ostium.
!!
!danger Stripping the face mucosa into the sinus
Peeling the mucosa of the sphenoid face off the rostrum and pushing it into the sinus loses your landmarks and causes heavy bleeding inside the cavity.
!!
## The midline approach
For bilateral access, or when oedema or polyps hide the ostium, disarticulate the posterior septum from the rostrum with a Cottle elevator and remove the rostral bone with rongeurs or a drill to open both sinuses, then enlarge the opening with Kerrison rongeurs. The face can be safely punctured with a Frazier suction **inferomedial to the ostium**, where the bone is usually thinnest.
!senior Make it wide in osteitic disease
In chronic inflammatory disease with osteitis, circumferential stenosis of a sphenoidotomy is common. A wide opening down to the floor of the sinus, or across the rostrum and intersinus septum, prevents it.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Arterial bleeding from the inferior edge
Recognise | the posterior septal branch of the sphenopalatine artery, low on the sphenoid face
Control | cauterise the mucosa over the sphenoid face and upper choana
Then | continue, keeping further enlargement lateral and superior
!!
!path Suspected optic nerve or orbital injury
Causes | imprecise lateral dissection of the face, an unrecognised Onodi cell containing the nerve, or entry away from the natural ostium
Act | immediate ophthalmology referral for assessment of vision
Know | it is devastating and difficult to correct ; prevention is precise entry through the natural ostium
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Failing to find the true natural ostium; confusing an Onodi or posterior ethmoid cell with the sphenoid.
- Dissecting downward through the posterior septal artery.
- Too little blunt opening, so the punch will not pass.
- Touching the posterior wall during identification or dissection.
- Not preserving mucosa, outside tumour surgery.
- An opening too small, which restenoses.
- Stripping the face mucosa into the sinus.
!!

# Aftercare | Keep it open.
## After the operation
Give the standard instructions after sinus surgery, start **saline irrigation on day 1**, and debride and clear the cavity in clinic at **7 to 14 days**. Typical medicines are an antistaphylococcal antibiotic, oral prednisone and short-course analgesia. Survey endoscopically in clinic for persistent inflammation and restenosis.
## Complications
!table Complications of sphenoidotomy
Complication | Notes
Synechiae and stenosis | Limited by early follow-up; symptomatic scar can be cut in clinic with through-cutting and powered instruments, and a circumferential stenosis enlarged under local anaesthesia with an elevator and mushroom punch
Loss of smell | From over-resection of the superior turbinate, which carries olfactory filaments
Arterial bleeding | Posterior septal branch of the sphenopalatine artery
CSF leak | Skull base breach
Optic nerve or orbital injury | Imprecise lateral dissection, an unrecognised Onodi cell, entry away from the natural ostium; immediate ophthalmology review
Internal carotid artery injury | A dehiscent carotid, or an intersinus septum inserting on it
!!
## Alternatives
- **Balloon dilatation** of the ostium, under local anaesthesia in the office or with other surgery in theatre, with image guidance to confirm entry; for chronic sinusitis without polyps or recurrent acute sinusitis.
- **Continued medical treatment and observation**, appropriate for most patients with inflammatory sphenoid disease.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Does trimming the superior turbinate cost smell?
The superior turbinate carries olfactory epithelium, most densely at its back, and loss of smell lowers quality of life and is dangerous (spoiled food, smoke, gas). Yet in a histological study of the inferior superior turbinate removed at sinus surgery, olfactory fibres were found in only **16%** of specimens, and none of the **12%** of patients who reported smell loss afterwards had olfactory neurons in their specimen. The evidence so far suggests that removing the inferior part of the superior turbinate carries a **low risk** to smell; over-resection is what to avoid.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Searching too high or too lateral for the ostium.
- Opening the sinus through the posterior ethmoid without confirming you are in the sphenoid.
- Removing the intersinus septum without sharp instruments and great caution.
- Taking too much superior turbinate.
- A small opening in osteitic disease.
!!
