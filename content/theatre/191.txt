@stand Adenoidectomy removes the lymphoid tissue of the nasopharyngeal roof and back wall. It is one of the first operations a resident does, usually with a tonsillectomy or ventilation tubes, and it is done partly by feel and partly with a mirror around the corner of the soft palate. The danger is in what you cannot see: the Eustachian tube cushions at the sides, the vertebrae behind, the palate that may not close after the adenoid has gone, and bleeding from a raw surface you cannot press on. This chapter covers the indications, the palate check that prevents velopharyngeal insufficiency, the operation by curette and by suction diathermy, and the aftercare.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Adenoidectomy
 | Summary
Indications | Obstructive sleep apnoea or sleep-disordered breathing from adenotonsillar hypertrophy; chronic nasal obstruction, mouth breathing and snoring; otitis media with effusion with the second set of tubes, or with the first set if the adenoid also causes symptoms; chronic or recurrent rhinosinusitis in children failing medical treatment (first-line surgery, especially under six); chronic adenoiditis with postnasal discharge
Contraindications | Cleft palate, submucous cleft or existing velopharyngeal insufficiency (risk of hypernasal speech; superior-pole partial adenoidectomy if essential); uncorrected coagulopathy; acute upper respiratory infection within about two weeks; unstable atlantoaxial joint
Anaesthesia | General, oral endotracheal tube secured in the midline; no sedative premedication in sleep apnoea
Position | Supine, shoulder roll, neck extended (Rose position), head ring, eyes lubricated and taped
Antibiotics | Not routine; endocarditis prophylaxis when indicated
Tray | Boyle-Davis or McIvor gag with a slotted blade and Draffin rods or a Mayo stand suspension, 6 or 8 French rubber catheters, nasopharyngeal mirror, adenoid curettes of several widths (with and without a guard), St Clair Thompson forceps, suction diathermy, gauze roll throat pack, oxymetazoline 0.025%, postnasal packs; optional microdebrider, coblation wand, 0- and 70-degree telescopes
Time | 10 to 20 minutes
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the parents
- **Why:** to relieve blocked breathing and snoring, reduce glue ear or sinus infections.
- **Bleeding:** usually at the time and controlled in theatre; occasionally a pack in the back of the nose overnight, rarely a return to theatre.
- **Damage to teeth, lips and gums** from the mouth gag; loose milk teeth may come out.
- **A nasal, hypernasal voice** for a few weeks; very rarely persistent velopharyngeal insufficiency needing speech therapy or surgery.
- **Neck pain and stiffness**, usually muscle spasm; rarely an inflammatory loosening of the top joint of the neck (Grisel syndrome).
- **Regrowth** of adenoid needing further surgery in about 1% to 3%.
- **Narrowing of the back of the nose** by scarring, rare.
- The risks of anaesthesia, and an overnight stay for children at higher risk.
!!
## Before you start
Ask about mouth breathing, snoring and witnessed apnoeas, restless sleep, daytime sleepiness, irritability or hyperactivity and bedwetting; hearing loss and speech delay; persistent effusion; chronic rhinorrhoea, cough and nasal obstruction. Ask about allergic rhinitis and asthma, a personal or family history of abnormal bleeding (and screen for von Willebrand disease if present), anticoagulants and nasal steroids, previous surgery, and **nasal regurgitation of fluids or hypernasal speech**, which suggest velopharyngeal insufficiency.
Examine for adenoid facies (open mouth, lack of lip seal, high-arched palate, malocclusion, gummy smile), hyponasal speech, effusions and drum retraction, and look at the palate for a **bifid uvula**. The adenoid can be seen on nasendoscopy (or anterior rhinoscopy after decongestion); a lateral soft-tissue radiograph gives the **adenoid-nasopharyngeal ratio** (normal about 0.63 to 0.73). Postnasal discharge suggests adenoiditis; pus in the middle meatus suggests sinusitis.
For sleep apnoea, polysomnography grades severity in children with severe symptoms, comorbidity or uncertain diagnosis; look for cardiac complications (pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular hypertrophy) with an echocardiogram when severe. Children with Down syndrome need assessment of the cervical spine, because the operation is done with the neck extended. Postpone surgery for two weeks after an upper respiratory infection.

# Anatomy you must see | The nasopharynx, seen around a corner.
## The nasopharyngeal box
The nasopharynx lies behind the **choanae**, framed by the posterior edge of the **vomer** in the midline and the posterior ends of the **inferior and middle turbinates** laterally. Its roof and back wall slope down over the body of the sphenoid, the basiocciput, and the anterior arch of the **atlas** with the longus capitis and colli muscles. The **adenoid** (nasopharyngeal tonsil) sits on the roof and posterior wall, in the midline, with lateral bands extending toward the tubes.
## The lateral walls
On each lateral wall, the **torus tubarius** is the cartilaginous cushion around the pharyngeal opening of the **Eustachian tube**, at the level of the inferior turbinate; behind it is the **fossa of Rosenmüller**. Lymphoid tissue on and around the torus (Gerlach's tonsil) is left alone: the curette's edges must stay medial to both tori.
## The palate that must still close
At speech and swallowing, the soft palate lifts and meets the posterior pharyngeal wall, often on the adenoid pad itself (the adenoid "pad" helps closure in some children). If the palate is short, scarred or clefted, removing the adenoid opens a gap that air and fluid escape through. A **submucous cleft** is recognised by the triad of a bifid uvula, a notch in the posterior edge of the hard palate felt with a finger, and a translucent midline zone (zona pellucida) where the muscle is deficient.
## Structures at risk
- **Eustachian tube cushions**, injured by a curette swept too laterally; scarring causes tube dysfunction.
- **Prevertebral muscles, the atlas and the atlantoaxial joint**, injured by a curette pressed too deep or a hyperextended neck.
- **Posterior pharyngeal wall mucosa**, torn by deep curettage.
- **The palate**, which must close after surgery.

# The operation, stage by stage | Six stages.
## Position, gag and check the tube
After intubation, the anaesthetist secures the tube in the midline. Place the shoulder roll and head ring; avoid hyperextension. Tape the eyes, and drape so that no clip or tube rests on them. Insert the gag with a slotted blade over the tube and open it, protecting the lips and teeth, and suspend it. Ask the anaesthetist to **recheck the airway pressures and tube position** after the neck is extended and again after the gag is opened.
!mf 6 | Positioning with the gag suspended and the soft palate retracted by catheters through the nose.
!see What you should see
- The tube held centrally in the blade groove, the uvula and palate in view, steady ventilation.
!!
!danger The gag
A gag can kink or push the tube into a bronchus, and can break a loose tooth or catch the lip. Look at the lips and teeth every time you open it.
!!
## Feel the palate
Before you remove anything, palpate the hard and soft palate with a finger for a **notch** in the posterior edge of the hard palate, and look at the uvula. A bifid uvula, a notch or a translucent midline means a submucous cleft: stop and reconsider (a superior-pole partial adenoidectomy or no adenoidectomy). Many surgeons also feel the nasopharynx to judge the size of the adenoid and sweep lateral tissue medially.
!mf 5 | Palpating the palate to exclude a submucous cleft.
!see What you should see and feel
- A single uvula, a smooth posterior border to the hard palate, and muscle across the soft palate.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
The palate check takes ten seconds and prevents the most regretted complication of this operation: a child who speaks through the nose for life.
!!
## Retract the palate and see the adenoid
Place a throat pack of rolled gauze in the oropharynx to keep blood out of the larynx, and put a few drops of 0.025% oxymetazoline into each nostril. Pass a 6 or 8 French rubber catheter through each nostril, retrieve the tip from the mouth and clamp both ends together over the drape to pull the soft palate forward. With the **nasopharyngeal mirror** (warmed to prevent fogging) in your left hand, look up behind the palate and identify **both tori** and the adenoid between them.
!mf 7 | The soft palate retracted by catheters; the adenoid (A) between the two tori (T).
!see What you should see
- The adenoid mass, both tori tubarii on either side, and the choanae above if the adenoid is small.
!!
## Curette the adenoid
Choose the widest curette that fits between the tori. Holding the mirror in your left hand, introduce the curette with your right, using your right thumb as a fulcrum, place it high in the nasopharynx against the posterior edge of the vomer, press it gently back onto the adenoid and sweep **downward in one smooth stroke**, flexing the head slightly as you pass the atlas. Most of the adenoid comes away in the first one or two sweeps; remove remnants with a narrower curette, the St Clair Thompson forceps or suction diathermy. With a guarded curette, many surgeons work by feel, with the guard against the septum.
!mf 3 4 | Adenoid curettes of different sizes, and the St Clair Thompson forceps for grasping and cutting remnants.
!see What you should see
- The adenoid mass removed in one piece and a smooth, flat bed down to the prevertebral fascia, not deeper.
!!
!danger Too deep or too lateral
Deep curettage tears the prevertebral muscles and bleeds heavily; lateral sweeps scar the Eustachian tube openings. Do not cut blindly into a field full of blood.
!!
## Control the bleeding
Pack the nasopharynx with a gauze pack soaked in oxymetazoline or saline and wait a few minutes (or proceed to the tonsils, which are removed after the adenoid). Irrigate saline through the nose, suction it from the oropharynx and look with the mirror. Treat persistent bleeding points with suction diathermy under vision. If bleeding still continues, leave a postnasal pack for 24 hours.
!see What you should see
- A dry nasopharyngeal bed and **a complete view of both choanae**, with no tags left in the choanae or on the tori.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Most bleeding from the adenoid bed is from the raw surface, not a vessel. Time, pressure and vasoconstrictor stop most of it; suction diathermy and a pack deal with the rest.
!!
## Remove the packs and finish
Remove every pack (count them with the scrub nurse), suction the nasopharynx and oropharynx, remove the catheters and the gag, and check the teeth and lips.
!mf 8 | The removed adenoid mass.
!see What you should see
- A clean pharynx, no clot in the nasopharynx or behind the palate, and the throat pack accounted for.
!!
!danger The forgotten pack
A throat or postnasal pack left in place obstructs the airway on extubation. State its removal aloud.
!!

# Other techniques | The same aims with different tools.
## Suction diathermy, microdebrider and coblation
**Suction diathermy adenoidectomy** under mirror or endoscopic view ablates the adenoid layer by layer with less blood loss than curettage and good control near the tori. The **microdebrider** under a 70-degree telescope (or mirror) shaves the adenoid precisely, including tissue in the choanae. **Coblation** removes tissue with a radiofrequency plasma field at lower temperatures. Revision rates are similar for all (around 1% to 2%); curettage is quickest but bloodiest. Laser adenoidectomy has been associated with nasopharyngeal stenosis.

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Brisk bleeding from the nasopharynx
Pack | oxymetazoline-soaked gauze pack ; wait
Look | irrigate, mirror or endoscope ; remove tags left behind (residual tissue bleeds)
Coagulate | suction diathermy under vision
Escalate | postnasal pack for 24 hours ; check clotting
!!
!path Desaturation at induction or pulmonary oedema
Recognise | a child with long-standing obstruction: airway collapse at induction, or pink frothy secretions when the obstruction is relieved
Act | intubate ; ventilate with positive end-expiratory pressure ; diuretic on anaesthetic advice
Prevent | no sedative premedication ; warn the anaesthetist in severe sleep apnoea or pulmonary hypertension
!!
!path Rising pulse and dark vomit after surgery
Recognise | swallowed blood from postoperative bleeding
Act | resuscitate, examine the nasopharynx and oropharynx ; nasal vasoconstrictor
Escalate | return to theatre for haemostasis and a postnasal pack under general anaesthesia
!!

# Aftercare | Most go home the same day.
## On the ward
Healthy children from three years of age without bleeding history can go home the same day. Admit and monitor overnight the higher-risk children: those under three, severe sleep apnoea on polysomnography, cardiac complications of sleep apnoea, craniofacial or neuromuscular disorders, obesity and failure to thrive. Nurse in the semiprone (tonsil) position with continuous oxygen saturation monitoring, watching for residual sedation. Look in the oropharynx periodically for blood trickling down from the nasopharynx; vasoconstrictor drops treat minor ooze. Paracetamol is usually enough; codeine adds nothing and is no longer used in children. Oral intake is easy after adenoidectomy alone.
## Complications
!table Complications of adenoidectomy
Complication | When | Notes
Injury to eyes, lips, teeth, oral mucosa | Perioperative | Tape the eyes; care with the gag
Tube displacement or kinking | Perioperative | Recheck after extension and gag
Airway obstruction, pulmonary oedema | Induction and recovery | Severe sleep apnoea and pulmonary hypertension
Primary haemorrhage | First 24 hours | Pack, diathermy, postnasal pack; a rising pulse and dark vomit signal hidden bleeding
Eustachian tube injury | Perioperative | Keep medial to the tori
Velopharyngeal insufficiency | Early, usually transient | Prevent by the palate check; speech therapy; rarely surgery
Regrowth | Months to years | Symptomatic regrowth under 1% to 3%
Nasopharyngeal stenosis | 3 to 10 weeks | Rare; from scarring of the raw surface
Grisel syndrome | Within a week | Painful torticollis from inflammatory atlantoaxial subluxation; imaging, neurosurgical opinion, anti-inflammatories, antibiotics, immobilisation
!!
## Alternatives
Antibiotics for adenoiditis, intranasal steroids, and montelukast for mild sleep-disordered breathing.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: uses the Boyle-Davis gag, examines the nasopharynx by palpation and mirror, and uses a guarded adenoid curette introduced until its edge touches the back of the septum, swept down with the head slightly flexed to protect the odontoid; it lists dental malocclusion and recurrent discharge in mucosal chronic otitis media as indications, warns that 10% to 20% of children with Down syndrome have atlantoaxial instability (flexion and extension radiographs), and that hyperextension should always be avoided.
Lee: emphasises the AAO-HNS indications, adenoidectomy as first-line surgery for paediatric chronic rhinosinusitis, and the polysomnography criteria in sleep apnoea.
Myers retracts the palate with catheters and curettes under mirror vision, and gives the guideline criteria for overnight admission and the revision rates for each technique.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Indications, and adenoidectomy with the **second** set of tubes for effusion.
- **Contraindications**: cleft and submucous cleft (the triad), bleeding disorders, recent infection.
- The **tori** as lateral limits; the atlas behind.
- How you control bleeding from the bed.
- **Grisel syndrome** and velopharyngeal insufficiency.
- Who stays overnight.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Skipping the palate palpation.
- Curetting blindly in a field full of blood.
- Sweeping lateral to the tori.
- Hyperextending the neck, especially in Down syndrome.
- Leaving tags in the choanae (they cause bleeding and persistent obstruction).
- Forgetting the throat or postnasal pack.
!!
