@stand Facial reanimation follows one question: what is left to work with? If the nerve can be joined, join it; if the proximal nerve is gone but the facial muscles are still alive, give them a new nerve from the hypoglossal or masseteric nerve; if the muscles have died, bring in new muscle, by transposing the temporalis or by a free gracilis flap; and wherever movement cannot be restored, support the face statically. Throughout, the eye comes first.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Facial reanimation
 | Summary
Under about 12 to 24 months, muscles alive (fibrillations on EMG), proximal nerve available | Primary repair or interposition graft (greater auricular, sural)
Muscles alive, no proximal nerve (after cerebellopontine angle surgery, trauma) | Nerve transfer: hypoglossal-facial (full, split or jump graft) or masseteric-facial; cross-facial nerve graft for spontaneity
Muscles atrophied (long-standing palsy, congenital) | Muscle transfer: gracilis free flap (masseteric or cross-facial innervation) or temporalis transposition (lengthening myoplasty)
Any stage, or as an adjunct | Static support: fascia lata sling for the mouth corner, alar suspension for the nasal airway, brow lift, eyelid weight and lower lid tightening
Lower lip only | Digastric transfer, or weakening the opposite depressor
Anaesthesia | General
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- Reinnervation takes **4 to 12 months** to show movement; free muscle transfer takes longer.
- No technique gives a normal face: expect **synkinesis** after nerve repair and a smile that may need effort (masseteric) rather than being spontaneous.
- **Hypoglossal transfer:** weakness and wasting of one side of the tongue (less with the split or jump graft techniques).
- **Masseteric transfer:** smiling by clenching the teeth, which most learn to do automatically.
- **Static slings** stretch and may need tightening; synthetic materials can become infected or extrude.
- **Donor site** scars and numbness (neck, leg, thigh).
!!
## Before you start
Establish the cause, the duration, the status of the proximal nerve, and whether the muscles are alive (needle EMG fibrillations or voluntary potentials). Grade the face (House-Brackmann or Sunnybrook) region by region, photograph and video it. Examine the eye: closure, Bell's phenomenon, corneal sensation, lower lid laxity. Check the nose for valve collapse. Check the donor nerves (tongue, masseter bulk on clenching).

# The operation, stage by stage | From nerve to muscle to sling.
## Protect the eye first
Lubricants and taping at night; a gold or platinum weight in the upper lid for lagophthalmos; lateral canthal tightening for an everted lower lid. These come before or with any reanimation.
!see What you should see
- A cornea covered on gentle closure, without exposure keratitis.
!!
## Hypoglossal-facial transfer
Through a modified Blair (parotidectomy) incision, find the facial nerve trunk at the stylomastoid foramen and follow it to just before the pes anserinus; divide it there, keeping a strip of deep epineurium to stop it retracting. Find the hypoglossal nerve between the internal jugular vein and carotid deep to the digastric, divide it just beyond the descendens hypoglossi, bring the proximal end medial to the digastric for length, match the fascicles and repair with fine perineurial sutures without tension.
!mf 3 | Before and after hypoglossal-facial transposition.
!see What you should see
- A tension-free coaptation of matched nerve ends under the microscope.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
To spare the tongue, use a partial (split) hypoglossal or an interposition "jump" graft from a nick in the hypoglossal to the facial nerve, or use the masseteric nerve instead. The masseteric nerve, found in the subzygomatic triangle, reaches the facial nerve branches directly and gives a strong smile.
!!
## Interposition and cross-facial grafts
For a gap, harvest the greater auricular nerve (across sternomastoid, parallel to the external jugular vein) or the sural nerve for longer grafts. A cross-facial nerve graft tunnels a sural nerve from a redundant buccal branch on the normal side across the upper lip, to power the paralysed side or a later muscle flap, giving a spontaneous but weaker smile.
!see What you should see
- Grafts lying loosely in their beds with matched fascicles at both ends.
!!
## Free gracilis transfer
Harvest a segment of gracilis with its pedicle (from the profunda femoris vessels) and its branch of the obturator nerve. Inset it in the cheek from the modiolus and lip to the zygoma and temporal fascia along the vector of the normal smile, anastomose the vessels (usually to the facial vessels) and the nerve to the masseteric branch, a cross-facial graft, or both.
!mf 4 | Muscle transposition for lip depressor paralysis (digastric).
!see What you should see
- A muscle under appropriate tension, perfused, with its nerve coapted.
!!
## Static sling for the mouth corner
Measure from the zygomatic arch to the modiolus and harvest a 2.5 cm strip of fascia lata. Through a nasolabial or vermilion incision and a temporal hairline incision, tunnel between the subcutaneous fat and the SMAS. Split the distal end and fix it to the upper and lower lip and the modiolus with permanent sutures, then pull the sling up and out along the vector of the normal smile, overcorrect, and fix it around the zygomatic arch or to temporal fascia.
!see What you should see
- A slightly overcorrected mouth corner, the sling invisible under the skin.
!!
!danger Wrong plane, wrong tension
Too superficial a sling shows as a ridge (the "sabre" sign); too deep and it dampens any residual movement. Wrapping the lips circumferentially narrows the mouth opening. Undercorrection is the commonest failure because the graft relaxes in the first weeks.
!!
## Alar suspension
For nasal obstruction from a collapsed ala, incise in the alar crease, expose the anterior maxilla (stay 1 cm below the orbital rim to protect the infraorbital nerve), and suspend the ala laterally with fascia or a bone anchor.
!mf 2 | Lateral suspension of the nasal ala with a bone anchor.
!see What you should see
- An open nasal vestibule on the paralysed side at rest and on inspiration.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Free gracilis flap looks congested or pale
Act | check the pedicle at once ; return to theatre for anastomosis revision
Lost | debride ; plan a second free flap or a temporalis transfer
!!
!path Sling infected or extruding
Act | antibiotics ; remove synthetic material
Later | replace with autologous fascia
!!

# Aftercare | Training the new face.
## After surgery
Facial physiotherapy with mirror and biofeedback training is essential after nerve transfers and muscle transfers (patients learn to smile by pushing the tongue or clenching). Botulinum toxin treats synkinesis and balances the normal side. Revision of tension is common after slings and free muscle transfer.
## Complications
Flap failure, haematoma, infection, synkinesis, tongue weakness, sling stretch or extrusion, donor site morbidity, asymmetry.
!update Current evidence
**Free gracilis.** A meta-analysis of 31 studies (1,739 gracilis flaps) found flap failure in about 3% and larger smile excursion with masseteric nerve coaptation (about 10 mm) than with cross-facial nerve grafts (about 6.8 mm) (Roy et al., *J Plast Reconstr Aesthet Surg* 2019). A second meta-analysis confirmed greater commissure excursion with the masseteric nerve, while cross-facial grafts may give a more spontaneous smile (Vila et al., *JAMA Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2020).
**Temporalis or gracilis.** For long-standing palsy, both lengthening temporalis myoplasty and the free gracilis improve symmetry; gracilis outcomes depend on the donor nerve, and dual innervation gave results similar to masseteric alone (Bos et al., *J Craniomaxillofac Surg* 2016).
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Myers: static suspension, alar lateralisation, hypoglossal-facial transposition and grafting (with the most consistent results), masseteric transfer, digastric transfer and the gracilis free flap.
K. J. Lee: the reanimation ladder by duration of palsy and the eye procedures.
The literature: masseteric-powered gracilis gives more excursion; cross-facial grafts give more spontaneity.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- The **decision tree**: nerve available, muscles alive, muscles dead.
- **Timing:** muscle survival of about 18 to 24 months.
- **Donor nerves:** hypoglossal, masseteric, cross-facial; their trade-offs.
- **Static versus dynamic** procedures.
- **Eye protection** as the first priority.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Forgetting the eye.
- Offering nerve transfer after the muscles have atrophied.
- Undercorrecting a static sling.
- Repairing nerves under tension.
!!
