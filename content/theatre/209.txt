@stand Prominent ears come from two faults, often together: an antihelical fold that never formed and a conchal bowl that is too deep. Otoplasty fixes each one directly. Mustardé sutures fold the antihelix, conchomastoid (Furnas) sutures set the bowl back, and the lobule is tucked if needed. The aim is a natural ear seen from the front, with a smooth fold, a helix visible behind the antihelix, and both ears matching, not pinned flat.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Otoplasty for prominent ears
 | Summary
Indications | Prominent ears causing distress, with realistic expectations; usually from about 5 to 6 years, when the ear is near adult size and before teasing at school; adults at any age
Contraindications | A child who does not want it; unrealistic expectations; skin infection
Non-surgical | Moulding (splinting) in the first weeks of life, while maternal oestrogen keeps the cartilage soft
Anaesthesia | General in children; local in older children and adults
Position | Supine, head turned, both ears in the field for comparison
Tray | Fine scissors, rasp or Adson-Brown forceps for scoring, 4-0 clear nylon for Mustardé and conchomastoid sutures, 5-0 nylon for temporary marking sutures, light pressure dressing
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient or family
- **Haematoma** (urgent: pain on one side) and **infection** of skin or cartilage.
- **Recurrence** of some prominence over time; slight overcorrection allows for it.
- **Asymmetry;** perfect symmetry is not natural or achievable.
- **Visible or palpable sutures,** suture extrusion or granuloma.
- **Contour problems:** a sharp fold, the "telephone" or "reverse telephone" deformity.
- **Scarring,** including rarely a keloid.
!!
## Before you start
Measure and photograph: helix-to-mastoid distance (normal about 15 to 20 mm) at the top, middle and lobule, the auriculocephalic angle (normally under about 30 degrees), and identify which part is at fault: an absent antihelical fold, a deep concha, a prominent lobule, or several.

# The operation, stage by stage | Cartilage-sparing otoplasty.
## Excise skin behind the ear
Inject local anaesthetic with adrenaline. Excise a fusiform strip of postauricular skin no wider than about 15 mm, biased onto the back of the auricle so the sulcus is not pulled, and dissect down onto the perichondrium behind the antihelix.
!see What you should see
- The posterior perichondrium exposed over the planned fold.
!!
## Mark the new fold
Fold the ear back with a finger to show where the antihelix should be, and pass temporary 5-0 nylon mattress sutures from the front through to the back to mark it.
!see What you should see
- The marking sutures sitting along a fold that looks natural when pressed back.
!!
## Weaken the cartilage (optional)
If the cartilage is stiff, score the front of the planned fold through a small subperichondrial pocket with a fine rasp or the serrated edge of Adson-Brown forceps, and wash out every cartilage fragment.
!see What you should see
- A cartilage that bends smoothly along the fold without kinking.
!!
## Mustardé sutures for the antihelix
Place about three horizontal mattress sutures of 4-0 clear nylon through cartilage and anterior perichondrium, without breaching the front skin, along the new fold. Tighten each until a natural fold forms, then a touch beyond to allow for relaxation. Remove the marking sutures as you go. Add one more for the superior crus if needed.
!mf 4 | The surgical steps: skin excision, Mustardé sutures and conchomastoid sutures.
!see What you should see
- A rounded antihelical fold with the helix just visible behind it from the front.
!!
## Set back the concha
For a deep bowl, place conchomastoid mattress sutures from the back of the concha to the mastoid periosteum, radially and not too far back, so the meatus is not narrowed or the skin tented. Excise a small fusiform strip of conchal cartilage if needed, keeping the perichondrium and canal skin intact.
!see What you should see
- The concha set back with an open, round ear canal.
!!
!danger The narrowed meatus
Conchomastoid sutures that pull the concha forwards and backwards in the wrong vector close the ear canal. Check the meatus before closing.
!!
## Lobule, close and dress
Correct a protruding lobule with a small skin excision behind it (M-plasty) or a suture. Close in layers and apply a lightly compressive, well-padded head dressing that holds the new contour.
!mf 2 | After correction: a natural antihelix and conchal set-back.
!see What you should see
- Both ears at similar distances from the head at the top, middle and lobule, viewed from the front and above.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Judge the result from the front, not the side, and correct the upper, middle and lower thirds in proportion. Pinning only the middle produces the telephone deformity; overdoing the top and lobule produces the reverse.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Severe one-sided pain after otoplasty
Act | remove the dressing and look now
Haematoma | evacuate in theatre ; re-dress
Infection or perichondritis | culture, antipseudomonal antibiotics, drain any pus
!!

# Aftercare | Protect the fold.
## After surgery
Keep the head dressing for a few days, then wear a headband at night for several weeks. Avoid contact sports for a while. Remove skin sutures if non-absorbable.
## Complications
Haematoma, infection, perichondritis, suture extrusion or granuloma, recurrence, asymmetry, contour deformities, meatal narrowing, keloid.
!update Current evidence
In 1,060 ears, cartilage-sparing (Mustardé, Furnas) and cartilage-cutting techniques had similar complication rates (about 20%, mostly minor) and similar reoperation rates (6% to 7%), but cartilage-sparing techniques scored better for shape and satisfaction (Smittenberg et al., *Plast Reconstr Surg* 2018). After suture-technique otoplasty, all adults were satisfied with the appearance and quality-of-life scores improved in both children and adults (Braun et al., *Plast Reconstr Surg* 2010).
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Myers: a cartilage-sparing technique with temporary marking sutures, optional scoring, Mustardé and conchomastoid sutures, and slight overcorrection.
K. J. Lee: lists the classic techniques by the fault corrected (Mustardé for the fold, Furnas for the concha, cartilage-cutting techniques such as Chongchet for stiff cartilage) and neonatal moulding.
The literature: sparing and cutting techniques have similar complications, but sparing techniques look better.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Excising too much skin to pin the ear back.
- Over-tightening sutures into a sharp fold.
- Treating only the middle third.
- Narrowing the meatus with conchal sutures.
!!
