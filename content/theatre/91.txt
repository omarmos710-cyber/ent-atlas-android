@stand The submandibular gland sits in a small triangle surrounded by three nerves you must not injure: the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve above and outside it, the lingual nerve looping over its duct, and the hypoglossal nerve running beneath it. Removing the gland for stones and chronic infection is far less common since sialendoscopy arrived, but it is still needed for gland masses, for ducts and glands ruined by chronic infection, and as part of neck dissection. This chapter covers how to assess the gland, the traditional transcervical operation with the Hayes Martin manoeuvre, how to find each nerve safely even in a scarred field, and the newer intraoral, endoscopic and robotic routes.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Excision of the submandibular gland
 | Summary
Indications | A mass or suspected neoplasm of the gland (excision is diagnostic when FNA is non-diagnostic and the mass is confined to the gland; more extensive surgery with neck dissection for a known malignancy); chronic sialadenitis with ectatic ducts that has failed gland-sparing treatment; stones not removable endoscopically; chronic drooling in neuromuscular swallowing disorders (bilateral excision with parotid duct ligation)
Contraindications | Acute infection (planes are lost; drain an abscess, securing the airway if the floor of mouth is raised, and operate later); try sialendoscopy first for stones, stricture, chronic sialadenitis, radiation or Sjögren-related disease
Anaesthesia | General, without long-acting muscle relaxants so the nerves can be stimulated; local is possible for benign disease in unfit patients
Position | Shoulder roll, head up slightly and turned away; oral tube taped to the opposite side (nasotracheal tube for the intraoral approach)
Antibiotics | None for the transcervical (clean) operation; 24 hours of oral-flora cover for the transoral (clean-contaminated) route
Monitoring | Not routine; a hand-held stimulator helps in malignancy and scarred, chronically infected fields
Imaging | Ultrasound for stones, dilated ducts and guided FNA; non-contrast CT for stones; contrast CT for abscess or bone involvement; **gadolinium MRI** for a suspected tumour, including perineural spread (adenoid cystic carcinoma)
Tray | Head and neck set; bipolar; hand-held nerve stimulator; small right-angled retractors; salivary endoscope if retained stones are a concern; suction drain
Before theatre | Review the FNA and imaging; stop antiplatelets and anticoagulants if possible
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **What it is:** removing the salivary gland under the jaw through a skin crease in the upper neck.
- **Lower lip weakness** on that side, usually temporary, from the small branch of the facial nerve that runs near the gland; more likely after repeated infection.
- **Numbness of the side of the tongue** (lingual nerve) and, rarely, **tongue weakness** (hypoglossal nerve).
- **Bleeding** under the jaw, which can push the tongue up and affect breathing; it is watched overnight for this reason.
- **A stone left in the remaining duct**, causing swelling or infection later.
- **Infection**, a salivary leak, and the scar.
- **Dry mouth** is usually not noticeable from one gland.
- **The final diagnosis** comes from the specimen; a cancer may need further treatment.
!!
## Before you start
### The history
- **Sialadenitis:** one-sided redness, swelling and pain under the jaw that worsen with meals, and foul-tasting saliva, especially when the gland is pressed.
- **A mass:** tumours here are uncommon but are proportionally more often malignant than in the parotid; weakness of the tongue or lower lip, or numbness of the tongue, suggests malignancy.
- **Drooling:** how long, how severe, the cause and whether it will improve.
- Sjögren syndrome; previous neck irradiation or radioiodine (raising the risk of sialadenitis and salivary tumours); previous drainage, biopsy or stone removal; other neck surgery.
- Occupational exposure to silica dust or nitrosamines; tobacco and alcohol; antiplatelets, anticoagulants and supplements.
### The examination
- A full head and neck examination; **neck nodes** need full work-up (about a quarter of submandibular cancers have spread to the neck).
- **Inspect and feel the floor of the mouth** for a stone in the end of Wharton's duct.
- **Bimanual palpation:** size, discrete mass or stone, tenderness, fixation.
- **Milk the gland** and compare the saliva on both sides: cloudy or purulent saliva means sialadenitis; no flow from a big gland means obstruction.
- **Nerves:** tongue movement (XII), tongue sensation (lingual), lower lip movement (marginal mandibular).
- In the elderly, a **ptotic gland** can drop below and outside the greater cornu of the hyoid and be mistaken for a tumour.

# Anatomy you must see | The triangle and its three nerves.
## The gland and its duct
- The gland lies in the **submandibular triangle**, above and lateral to the hyoid and digastric sling, below the mandible; it wraps around the back edge of the **mylohyoid**, with a superficial part outside it and a deep part inside.
- **Wharton's duct** runs forward deep to the mylohyoid, beside the **sublingual gland**, under the floor-of-mouth mucosa, to open at the **sublingual papilla** beside the frenulum. The opening is the narrowest point, so stones impact there.
- About 80% to 90% of salivary stones form here: the duct runs uphill and the saliva is richer in mucin.
!mf 1 | The opening of Wharton's duct (arrow) beside the frenulum.
## The three nerves
- **Marginal mandibular nerve:** runs beneath the platysma, superficial to the **facial vein**, over the gland. With the neck extended and the head turned, it lies **lower**, often right over the gland, and behind the gland it can be well below the mandible.
- **Lingual nerve:** a broad band coming from under the mandible, looping down close to the front of the gland and sending branches to the **submandibular ganglion**, then rising forward to the tongue; it lies **above** the duct.
- **Hypoglossal nerve:** runs forward along the floor of the triangle, **deep** to the gland and to the posterior belly of digastric, parallel to the duct, with accompanying veins.
## Landmarks
- The **facial vein** on the outer surface of the gland.
- The **anterior belly of digastric**, reflected back to show the mylohyoid.
- The **mylohyoid**, whose fibres run from back-above to front-below, at a clearly different angle from the digastric; the lingual nerve, duct, sublingual gland and front of the submandibular gland lie just deep to it.
- The **posterior belly of digastric**, below which the hypoglossal nerve can be found in a scarred field.
- The **facial artery**, which grooves the back-upper surface of the gland.

# The operation: transcervical excision | Seven stages.
## Incision and flap
Make the incision in a natural skin crease at the lower border of the gland, usually just above the hyoid; a finger in the mouth pushing the floor of the mouth down helps you find a small gland. Cut through skin, fat and platysma. Once you are through the platysma the marginal nerve is at risk, so aim your dissection at the **lower** part of the gland.
!see What you should see
- The capsule of the lower gland, with the facial vein running up over its outer surface.
!!
## Protect the marginal mandibular nerve
Incise the fascia over the lower gland and lift the upper fascial flap off the gland's outer surface. Find the **facial vein**, tie and divide it **low on the gland**, and lift the vein upward with the fascia: the nerve, which lies superficial to the vein, is lifted up out of harm's way (the **Hayes Martin manoeuvre**). For a malignancy or a neck dissection, dissect the nerve itself off the vein and remove the **prevascular and postvascular facial nodes**. In a scarred, chronically infected gland, you often need to find the nerve with magnification and a stimulator.
!mf 2 | The skin and platysma flap lifted off the gland; the marginal mandibular nerve (arrow) crossing the facial vein.
!mf 3 | Dividing the facial vein low on the gland and lifting the nerve with it; the prevascular and postvascular nodes (arrows).
!see What you should see
- The nerve lifted up out of the field with the vein, and the gland's capsule bare below it.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
For benign disease you do not have to see the nerve; you have to stay out of its plane. Dissect directly on the gland, deep to the fascia, and the nerve stays in the flap above you.
!!
## Free the front of the gland and retract the mylohyoid
Free the upper gland bluntly from the soft tissue beneath the mandible. Free the front of the gland at the anterior belly of digastric: incise the fascia over the anterior belly and reflect it backward, which shows the **mylohyoid**. Dissect along the back edge of the mylohyoid (it can be hard to find, or partly absent, in a chronically infected gland), divide the small vessels entering it, and pull the muscle **forward** with a small right-angled retractor to show the deep part of the gland.
!mf 4 | The back edge of the mylohyoid retracted forward to show Wharton's duct and the lingual nerve.
!see What you should see
- Deep to the retracted mylohyoid: the front of the gland, the duct with the sublingual gland stuck to it, and the lingual nerve above.
!!
## Free the lingual nerve
Find the lingual nerve as a broad band looping down toward the front of the gland. Divide the **branches running from it to the submandibular ganglion** so the nerve springs up, away from the gland. Only then look for the duct.
!danger The lingual nerve and the duct
The nerve lies directly above the duct. Dividing the duct before freeing the nerve, especially in a scarred gland, cuts the nerve and leaves the side of the tongue numb for good.
!!
!see What you should see
- A flat, broad white band looping down from under the mandible, tethered to the gland by the ganglion, which springs up out of the field once the ganglion branches are cut.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
The nerve is found by pulling the gland down and the mylohyoid forward at the same time. If you cannot see the loop, the retraction is wrong; do not start cutting to find it.
!!
## Find the hypoglossal nerve, then divide the duct
Identify the duct in the fascia of the floor of the triangle, and tie its accompanying vein separately. Identify the **hypoglossal nerve** running forward on the floor of the triangle from beneath the posterior belly of digastric. If inflammation hides it, find it **below the posterior belly of digastric** and follow it forward. With both nerves safe, follow the duct forward **to the floor-of-mouth mucosa** and divide it there, feeling bimanually for any stone left in the duct; pass a salivary endoscope retrograde through the duct if you are unsure.
!mf 5 | The lingual nerve's branches to the ganglion divided to free the nerve; Wharton's duct divided (arrow) and the specimen removed.
!see What you should see
- The lingual nerve lifted clear above, the hypoglossal nerve safe on the floor of the triangle, and the duct traced right up to the mucosa with no stone in it.
!!
!danger A stone left behind
A stone left in the remaining duct causes chronic infection after the gland has gone. Follow the duct all the way forward and feel along it before you divide it.
!!
## The facial artery
Pull the gland down, with countertraction above, and free its upper surface from beneath the mandible, staying right on the gland to protect the marginal nerve. The **facial artery** grooves the back-upper surface: divide and **double-tie** it where it leaves the gland above and where it enters below. In an uninflamed gland it can be kept by tying each small branch into the gland. Remove the gland.
!see What you should see
- The artery entering the back of the gland from beneath the posterior belly of digastric and leaving over the mandible, tied twice at each end, and the gland free.
!!
!danger The slipped tie
The proximal stump of the facial artery retracts under the digastric if its tie slips, and bleeds from a place that is hard to reach. Double-tie or suture-ligate it.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
For a benign, uninflamed gland, staying inside the gland's capsule and beneath the facial vein (which carries the marginal nerve up with the flap) protects the nerve without having to dissect it.
!!
## Haemostasis and closure
Inspect the whole triangle for bleeding, especially the front part deep to the mylohyoid. Irrigate, place a suction drain, and close platysma, subcutaneous tissue and skin in separate layers.
!see What you should see
- A dry triangle, a securely tied facial artery stump, and the three nerves intact.
!!

# Other approaches | When to avoid a neck scar.
## Intraoral excision
Through a nasotracheal tube, inject the floor of the mouth, put a probe into the papilla, and incise the floor-of-mouth mucosa from the duct opening back to the lingual side of the retromolar area. Removing the sublingual gland exposes the duct; follow the duct (beside the lingual nerve) and retract the tongue, floor of mouth and lingual nerve to see the mylohyoid. Pull the back edge of the mylohyoid forward to reach the gland, push the gland up from the neck, tie or avoid the facial vessels, remove it, and close the mucosa (with a drain if needed). It avoids a scar and the marginal nerve, but the heavy retraction of the floor of the mouth and lingual nerve often causes **temporary tongue numbness** (in one series about 81%) and temporary reduced tongue movement from swelling (about 68%), settling within about two weeks.
## Endoscopic and robotic excision
Endoscope-assisted excision through the mouth or a small neck or hairline incision follows the same steps. Robotic excision through a retroauricular incision, or three small neck ports in a balloon-dissected subplatysmal pocket, works from below upward under the capsule to protect the marginal nerve, with clips on the duct and facial vessels.
!table Approaches compared
Approach | Advantage | Drawback
Transcervical | Familiar, direct view of all three nerves | Neck scar; marginal nerve injury (transient in 7.7% to 36% across reports)
Intraoral | No scar; marginal nerve not at risk | Temporary tongue numbness and stiffness; nasotracheal tube; harder to learn
Submental or retroauricular | Hidden scar; small series show no extra marginal nerve injury | Small studies only
Endoscopic or robotic | Small or hidden incisions | Equipment, time; early experience
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Swelling under the jaw with breathing difficulty after surgery
Recognise | expanding neck swelling, a raised floor of mouth and tongue, a drain filling with blood
Act | call for senior and anaesthetic help ; open the wound to release the clot if the airway is threatened
Then | return to theatre to control the bleeding, usually the facial artery stump
!!
!path Lower lip weakness afterwards
Likely | neuropraxia of the marginal mandibular nerve, especially after chronic infection
Act | reassure; most recover ; if the nerve was known to be cut, repair or later reanimation
!!
!path Swelling and pus from the duct opening months later
Suspect | a stone left in the duct remnant
Act | imaging ; sialendoscopy or transoral removal of the stone and duct remnant
!!

# Aftercare | One night of watching the airway.
## After the operation
- Observe overnight, mainly for **airway swelling or bleeding**, with the head of the bed raised.
- Remove the drain when the output falls; **persistent heavy drainage means re-exploration**, because a haematoma here can push the tongue back and obstruct the airway.
- Check the lower lip, tongue sensation and tongue movement.
## Complications
!table Complications of submandibular gland excision
Complication | Cause or prevention
Marginal mandibular nerve palsy | Commonest nerve injury; dissect on the gland, Hayes Martin manoeuvre, find the nerve in scarred fields
Lingual nerve injury | Dividing the duct before freeing the nerve
Hypoglossal nerve injury | Nerve stuck to the deep surface in inflamed glands; find it below the posterior belly
Haematoma and airway compromise | Facial artery; double ligation, careful haemostasis
Retained stone | Duct not followed to the mucosa
Infection, fistula, poor scar | Uncommon
Dry mouth | Rarely noticeable after one gland
!!
## Alternatives
**Sialendoscopy** has changed the treatment of stones, strictures and chronic sialadenitis, and often saves the gland; transoral removal of stones in the duct is another gland-sparing option. A mass in the gland needs excision for diagnosis and treatment; a patient who declines must at least be followed up.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: explains that stones form from calcium phosphate on a mucin or debris matrix, that about 90% occur in the submandibular gland and 10% in the parotid, that about 80% are radio-opaque, and that stones near the duct opening can be removed through the mouth while stones at the hilum or in the gland need the gland removed.
Lee: notes that sialolithiasis can scar and dilate the ducts, that half of chronic sialadenitis improves with antibiotics, hydration and sialogogues, that removing the stone can restore function and spare the gland, that submandibular stones are usually in the duct (parotid stones in the gland), that sialendoscopy baskets cannot remove stones over 5 mm, and that a Küttner tumour (chronic sclerosing sialadenitis) can mimic a neoplasm.
Myers gives the operation: protecting the marginal nerve with the Hayes Martin manoeuvre or by identifying it, freeing the lingual nerve before dividing the duct, finding the hypoglossal nerve below the posterior belly of digastric, following the duct to the mucosa, double-tying the facial artery, observing the airway overnight, and the alternative approaches.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Operating during acute infection.
- Dissecting above the gland's fascia, into the marginal nerve's plane.
- Forgetting that the nerve lies lower with the head extended and turned.
- Dividing the duct before freeing the lingual nerve.
- Not seeing the hypoglossal nerve on the floor of the triangle.
- Leaving a stone in the duct.
- A single tie on the facial artery stump.
!!
