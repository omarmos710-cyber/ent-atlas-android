@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand The pectoralis major myocutaneous flap, introduced by Ariyan in 1979, made single-stage reconstruction of the head and neck reliable, and let patients reach adjuvant radiotherapy on time. Free flaps have since taken over its first-line role, but it remains the flap you reach for when a free flap is not possible or not worth it: a frail patient, a vessel-depleted neck after many operations, a failed free flap, a pharyngeal leak after salvage laryngectomy, or an exposed or bleeding carotid. Its pedicle is the pectoral branch of the thoracoacromial trunk on the deep surface of the muscle. The operation is safe when you see that pedicle, take only the muscle you need, denervate it, ligate the deltoid and acromial branches, and make a tunnel wide enough that nothing (fascia, radiated skin or a tracheostomy tie) compresses the pedicle. This chapter covers selection, the anatomy, harvest through the skin paddle, the tunnel and inset, and the errors that lose the paddle.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Pectoralis major myocutaneous flap
 | Summary
Indications | Carotid exposure or blowout from infection or radiotherapy; complications of carotid endarterectomy or stenting; neck skin defects with vessels exposed after neck dissection, pharyngeal or oesophageal fistula, or a failed free flap; aerodigestive reconstruction in a vessel-depleted, multiply operated neck; comorbidity or poor physical state that rules out microvascular transfer
Contraindications | **Absolute:** anatomical compromise of the muscle or its blood supply (a subpectoral pacemaker, surgery near the pedicle, congenital absence as in **Poland syndrome**). **Relative:** a non-functioning latissimus dorsi on that side (shoulder dysfunction must be weighed against a critical defect); cosmetic concerns in women and deformity in men; too short an arc or too small a flap for the defect
Before | Negative tumour margins and an uninfected wound
Anaesthesia | General
Position | Supine, the bed turned 90 or 180 degrees from the anaesthetist
Antibiotics | A first-generation cephalosporin; nothing extra for the harvest when combined with the resection
Monitoring | Standard; blood gases and haemoglobin when indicated
Tray | Head and neck set with retractors; monopolar and bipolar diathermy; lighted retractors for minimal-access harvest; laparoscopic harmonic scalpel
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to bring healthy muscle and skin from the chest up into the neck, to cover vital structures or close a leak.
- **Chest scar and change in shape** of the chest or breast; in women, the paddle is placed at the fold under the breast where possible.
- **Weakness of the shoulder**, especially if the back muscle on that side does not work.
- **Partial loss of the flap skin**, a **leak of saliva** or wound breakdown, which may need further surgery or another flap.
- **Bleeding**, and rarely injury to the lung lining (pneumothorax).
- **Bulk** in the neck at first, which shrinks as the denervated muscle wastes; the skin paddle may later be trimmed under local anaesthetic.
!!
## Before you start
Ask about anything that may have damaged the muscle or its pedicle: a **subpectoral pacemaker**, operations near the pedicle, or congenital absence of the muscle (**Poland syndrome**). Check the **latissimus dorsi** on the same side: if it is paralysed, losing pectoralis will impair the shoulder. Assess and correct, where you can, conditions affecting survival (cardiac and respiratory disease, alcoholism) and healing (diabetes, poor nutrition, smoking, thyroid dysfunction).

Examine the function and integrity of pectoralis major and the latissimus dorsi. Judge the fat and breast tissue over the muscle to decide between a **myocutaneous** and a **myofascial** flap. Imaging is usually unnecessary; if needed, the authors favour surgeon-performed duplex ultrasound.

# Anatomy you must see | The muscle and its vessels.
## The muscle
Pectoralis major lies below the clavicle and deltoid and above the rectus abdominis and external oblique. It arises from the medial half of the clavicle, the front of the sternum, the costal cartilages of the upper seven ribs and the external oblique aponeurosis, and inserts into the lateral lip of the bicipital groove.
## The pedicle
The **dominant pedicle** is the **pectoral branch of the thoracoacromial artery** with its venae comitantes, from the subclavian vessels. It emerges through the **clavipectoral fascia** between subclavius and pectoralis major, at a point just medial to the deltopectoral triangle (Mohrenheim's fossa, bounded by deltoid, pectoralis major and clavicle), and runs on the **deep surface** of the muscle along a line from that point to the **xiphoid**.
- **Minor pedicle:** the pectoral branch of the lateral thoracic artery, entering the upper border 3 to 4 cm lateral to the thoracoacromial branches; in a few patients it is the main supply.
- **Segmental:** internal mammary perforators through the first to sixth intercostal spaces, entering within 1.5 to 2 cm of the sternum; and branches of the fifth to seventh intercostal arteries entering 4 to 6 cm from the sternal origin.
- **The acromial and deltoid branches** of the thoracoacromial trunk must be ligated during the dissection.
## The nerves
The **lateral pectoral nerve** (lateral cord) enters the deep surface near the middle of the muscle; the **medial pectoral nerve** (medial cord) enters laterally after passing through pectoralis minor. Sensation comes segmentally from the second to seventh intercostal nerves.

# The operation, stage by stage | Harvest, tunnel, inset.
## Design the paddle
Mark the thoracoacromial vessels and the territory of the **deltopectoral flap**, which you may need one day. Design the skin paddle next to the **inferior border of the muscle**, or at the **inframammary fold** in women: the more distal the paddle, the better the arc of rotation. Keep the paddle at least partly **over the muscle**. Duplex ultrasound can confirm the vessels.
!mf 1 | Exposure of spinal hardware through the posterior pharynx and cervical oesophagus after chemoradiotherapy for tonsil cancer: (A) the flap raised through the perimeter of its paddle and inset, with a stretched 1.5:1 meshed graft to offset the tension of radiated skin, and the tracheostomy flange trimmed and sutured; (B) later, graft contracture reduces the deformity, the muscle atrophies and the paddle is excised under local anaesthetic.
## Raise the paddle
Start with the skin paddle; its perimeter is often enough exposure for the whole harvest. If part of the paddle lies beyond the muscle (a random portion), include the deep fascia just above the intercostal muscles to keep that part alive.
!danger Shearing the paddle
The skin can shear off the muscle. Handle the paddle gently and bevel outward as you dissect a small paddle, which maximises its viability.
!!
## Raise the muscle on its pedicle
Dissect a narrow central strip of muscle containing the pedicle toward the clavicle, using lighted breast retractors or a headlight; a laparoscopic harmonic scalpel gives secure haemostasis. Keeping the lateral remnant preserves the anterior axillary fold. Take the **whole muscle** when you need it to cover vessels, a pharyngeal suture line or a large defect. Keep the pedicle on the deep surface **in view**, and feel its pulse.
!mf 3 | Harvest in a woman during salvage surgery: the paddle from the inframammary fold on a narrow, denervated strip of muscle.
!see What you should see
- The pedicle pulsing on the deep surface of the muscle strip, all the way up to the clavicle.
!!
## Ligate branches and divide the nerves
At the clavicle, divide and ligate the **deltoid and acromial branches** of the thoracoacromial trunk: this lengthens the arc and prevents postoperative bleeding. Identify and divide the **pectoral nerves** so the muscle atrophies and does not twitch in the neck.
!mf 2 | Pharyngeal leak after salvage laryngectomy and neck dissection for failed chemoradiotherapy: (A) bevelled dissection of a small paddle; (B) the whole muscle used to cover pharynx and neck; (C) radiated neck and chest skin divided completely to keep pressure off the pedicle, the exposed muscle grafted with sheet skin.
## Make the tunnel
Tunnel subcutaneously from the chest to the neck, wide enough to pass **four fingers**. The clavipectoral fascia, once detached from the clavicle, forms a band that must be released or it will compress the pedicle. Radiated neck skin does not stretch: divide it and skin-graft the muscle rather than force the flap through a tight tunnel.
!danger A tight tunnel
A tight tunnel, a fascial band or stiff radiated skin compresses the pedicle and kills the flap. Make it generous and release what constricts it.
!!
## Inset without twisting
Pass the flap into the neck without twisting the pedicle. Anchor the deep parts to the **prevertebral fascia** or to structures such as the mandible so that gravity does not pull the flap down and open the suture lines. Avoid excessive traction on the pedicle: design, dissection, inset of the paddle and suture stabilisation of the muscle all help.
## Preserve the next flap and close
Preserve the **internal mammary perforators 2, 3 and 4**, to keep a future deltopectoral or internal mammary perforator flap alive. Close most donor sites directly; for larger defects, use negative pressure or staged closure devices. Skin grafts are rarely needed.
!senior Nothing around the neck
No ties around the neck after surgery. Cut off the tie openings on the tracheostomy flange and suture it to the neck skin, and post a "Nothing around neck" sign over the bed. This applies to free flaps too.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Salivary leak or dehiscence after inset
Recognise | saliva in the drain or wound, breakdown of the suture line
Act now | drain the saliva and debride
Carotid exposed | further flap cover is urgent
Prevent | anchor the flap to prevertebral fascia or mandible ; avoid excess bulk
!!
!path The paddle is failing
Look for compression | tunnel, fascial band, radiated skin or a tracheostomy tie
Release | open the tunnel, remove ties, graft the muscle if the skin will not close
Expect | partial necrosis mainly in a random part of the paddle beyond the muscle
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Too much bulk, so the neck or defect will not close.
- A tight tunnel compressing the pedicle.
- A tracheostomy tie compressing the pedicle.
- Too short a pedicle or arc: measure before you cut, and remember body habitus limits reach.
- Excess traction on the pedicle.
- A paddle lying off the muscle, which is lost.
- Dehiscence from a flap pulled down by gravity.
!!

# Aftercare | Warm, hydrated, nothing tight.
## After the operation
Control pain, keep the room warm and the patient well hydrated to avoid vasospasm. Look after the tracheostomy so nothing compresses the pedicle. Give perioperative antibiotics, and refer to physiotherapy for the shoulder.
## Complications
!table Complications
Complication | Notes
Dehiscence and fistula | From bulk and tethering by the pedicle; drain and debride; further flaps urgently if the carotid is exposed
Partial flap necrosis | Usually the random portion of the paddle
Shoulder and neck dysfunction | Worse if latissimus is weak
Bleeding | Unligated deltoid and acromial branches
Haemopneumothorax | Rare
Pedicle damage | During dissection
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Pectoralis or free flap?
The pectoralis flap is reliable and quicker to raise, but in the comparisons quoted it brings **longer hospital stays** and **higher costs** (about 41,000 dollars against 28,500 for a free flap), probably from more partial necrosis, salivary fistula, wound infection and delayed healing: its bulk, its tethering pedicle and a donor site that communicates with the reconstruction all play a part. Delayed healing matters, because radiotherapy should start **within 6 weeks** and all treatment finish **within 100 days**. There is a physiological reason too: the pectoralis skin paddle lies at least one **angiosome** away from its pedicle and is supplied mainly by internal mammary perforators (Taylor and Palmer), whereas a free flap's skin lies in its own pedicle's angiosome.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Today's indications: salvage, the vessel-depleted neck, the unfit patient, carotid cover.
- The pedicle: pectoral branch of the thoracoacromial trunk, its surface marking (Mohrenheim's fossa to xiphoid) and the minor pedicles.
- Contraindications: Poland syndrome, pacemaker, a paralysed latissimus.
- Denervate the muscle; ligate the deltoid and acromial branches.
- A four-finger tunnel; release the clavipectoral band.
- Keep the paddle over the muscle; distal for reach.
- Preserve internal mammary perforators 2 to 4 for a deltopectoral flap.
- Why free flaps are now preferred.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Not checking for a pacemaker or the state of latissimus.
- Losing sight of the pedicle.
- Tracheostomy ties around the neck.
- Forcing the flap through radiated skin.
!!
