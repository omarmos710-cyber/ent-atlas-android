@stand Mastoidectomy is sculpting in bone: you remove the air cells of the mastoid in a fixed order, finding one landmark before you look for the next, with the dura above, the sigmoid sinus behind, the labyrinth in the depth and the facial nerve running through the middle of the field. It is the operation for cholesteatoma and complicated mastoiditis, and the doorway to cochlear implantation, facial nerve and skull base surgery. This chapter covers the choice between keeping the canal wall and taking it down, the landmarks in the order you meet them, each stage of the intact canal wall operation, the facial recess, the canal wall down cavity and meatoplasty, and what to do when the sinus, dura, labyrinth or nerve is injured.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Mastoidectomy
 | Summary
Indications | Cholesteatoma of the attic or mastoid; chronic suppurative mastoiditis without cholesteatoma; acute coalescent mastoiditis, with or without temporal bone or intracranial complications; access for cochlear implantation, facial nerve surgery (decompression, tumour, grafting), endolymphatic sac surgery, labyrinthectomy, temporal bone resection and approaches to the posterior fossa and petrous apex
Canal wall up (intact) | Drainage of acute mastoiditis; cholesteatoma that can be fully removed or will have a planned second look; implant and facial nerve surgery
Canal wall down | Small, sclerotic mastoid with limited exposure; low tegmen or forward sigmoid sinus; disease that cannot be fully removed and must be exteriorised; labyrinthine fistula; matrix on an eroded footplate or a widely dehiscent facial nerve; an only hearing ear; unreliable follow-up; revision surgery with higher risk
Contraindications | Unfit for general anaesthesia; poor wound healing; cholesteatoma extending beyond the temporal bone (mastoidectomy alone is inadequate). An auto-atticotomy or auto-mastoidectomy cavity may be managed by cleaning in clinic
Anaesthesia | General, **without muscle relaxation** after induction, whether or not the facial nerve is monitored; controlled hypotension helps
Position | Supine, head turned away, neck neutral or slightly flexed; strapped to the table at several points because the table will be rotated; facial nerve electrodes placed and tested
Antibiotics | First-generation cephalosporin; clindamycin or vancomycin in penicillin allergy
Tray | Tympanoplasty and mastoidectomy set, otological drill with cutting and diamond burs in a full range of sizes, suction irrigation, operating microscope (225 to 300 mm lens), otoendoscopes for hidden areas, facial nerve monitor, 1:1000 adrenaline pledgets (counted), Gelfoam, Surgicel and bone wax
Imaging | Axial and coronal CT of the temporal bones available in theatre
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to remove infection or skin trapped in the ear (cholesteatoma) and make the ear safe and dry. Hearing is a secondary aim and may not improve; it may be worse.
- **Further surgery:** a planned second look after 9 to 12 months, a staged hearing reconstruction, or revision for recurrence.
- **A mastoid cavity** if the canal wall is taken down: a wider ear opening, lifelong cleaning in clinic, care with water, and sometimes dizziness with cold air or water.
- **Hearing loss**, including a small risk of total loss in that ear, and **tinnitus**.
- **Dizziness**, usually temporary.
- **Facial weakness:** under 1% in a first operation, about 4% to 10% in revisions; usually temporary when it occurs.
- **Altered taste** on that side of the tongue, often recovering over months.
- **Leak of brain fluid, meningitis** or bleeding from the large vein behind the ear: rare.
- **Wound problems**, numbness of the ear, and a change in the position of the pinna after meatoplasty.
- A **hearing aid** may still be needed.
!!
## Before you start
Ask about the duration and pattern of discharge, hearing loss, otalgia and dizziness; trauma, water exposure and barotrauma; response to treatment; and red flags: rapid progression, headache or altered mental state (intracranial complication), facial weakness and vertigo. Take the otological past: childhood infections and surgery, previous mastoid surgery or implants, noise, ototoxic drugs, radiotherapy and hearing aid use. **The other ear** decides a great deal: bilateral cholesteatoma, a contralateral perforation or hearing loss change how conservative you must be. Assess the patient's ability to attend for cavity care and second-stage surgery.
Examine both ears under the microscope after cleaning, and again after treating infection: an attic retraction pocket, keratin, a polyp, erosion of the scutum, the state of the pars tensa and a narrow canal that will need canalplasty. Record Rinne and Weber. Examine the nasopharynx, the cranial nerves and the fundi. A postauricular swelling, an acute facial palsy or papilloedema needs urgent action. Perform a fistula test when vertigo is reported.
Obtain bilateral audiometry (auditory brainstem responses in a child who cannot be tested), but never delay emergency surgery for it. **CT** is the anatomical map: on axial cuts, the pneumatisation and the position of the sigmoid sinus; on coronal cuts, the level of the tegmen; and erosion of the lateral semicircular canal, tegmen, facial canal and ossicles. CT cannot reliably show the stapes or tell cholesteatoma from fluid or mucosa unless bone is eroded. **MRI** shows encephalocele (coronal T2), intracranial complications, and, on non-echo-planar **diffusion-weighted imaging**, cholesteatoma itself. Control infection with topical and systemic antibiotics and aural toilet before elective surgery.

# Anatomy you must see | Landmarks in the order you meet them.
## On the surface of the bone
- **The linea temporalis (supramastoid crest):** the ridge running back from the root of the zygoma at the lower edge of the temporalis; it marks roughly the level of the **middle fossa dura**.
- **The spine of Henle:** the small spur at the posterosuperior margin of the bony meatus.
- **MacEwen's (suprameatal) triangle,** with the **cribriform area** of small vessel holes: bounded by the linea temporalis above, the posterosuperior meatal wall in front and a tangent to the back of the canal behind. The **antrum** lies deep to it, about 12 to 15 mm in the adult and only a few millimetres in an infant.
- **The mastoid tip,** with the digastric groove on its inner side.
## In the cavity
- **The tegmen mastoideum,** the plate of bone under the middle fossa dura: it shows as a pink blush through thin bone, sometimes with small veins.
- **The sigmoid sinus,** behind and medial: a blue blush through its bony plate. Between them is the **sinodural angle**.
- **Koerner's septum** (the petrosquamous lamina), a plate of bone that can be mistaken for the medial wall of the antrum; the true antrum is deep to it.
- **The lateral (horizontal) semicircular canal:** a smooth, dense, ivory-coloured dome in the floor of the antrum. It is the most reliable landmark in the mastoid.
- **The short process of the incus** in the **fossa incudis**, seen in front of the canal through the aditus.
- **The digastric ridge** at the tip: its front end leads to the stylomastoid foramen.
## The facial nerve
The **tympanic segment** runs back above the oval window, below the lateral canal. The **second genu** lies just below and medial to the dome of the lateral canal, at the posterior edge of the oval window and medial to the short process of the incus, which points to it; this is the segment injured most often. The **mastoid (vertical) segment** descends from there to the stylomastoid foramen at the front of the digastric ridge, lying deeper above and more lateral as it descends. The **chorda tympani** leaves it a few millimetres above the foramen and runs up and forward to the annulus. The **facial recess** is the triangle between the facial nerve medially, the chorda laterally and the incus buttress above.

# The operation, stage by stage | Intact canal wall (cortical) mastoidectomy.
## Set up, inject and incise
Prepare the field, check the monitor, and examine and clean the ear under the microscope to confirm the findings. If a tympanomeatal flap will be raised, inject lidocaine with adrenaline in four quadrants at the bony-cartilaginous junction (the medial limit of the hair-bearing skin), bevel to the bone and subperiosteal, until the skin blanches down to the drum; then make canal incisions at 12 and 6 o'clock joined by a vertical cut halfway between the annulus and the junction, forming a laterally based vascular strip and a medially based tympanomeatal flap. Make the postauricular incision a few millimetres behind the crease, from above the root of the helix to the tip, down to the loose areolar tissue over the temporalis fascia. In children, keep the lower limb further back and off the tip.
!mf 1 | The H-shaped canal incisions: a laterally based vascular strip and a medially based tympanomeatal flap.
!see What you should see
- Canal skin blanched to the drum, and the glistening temporalis fascia with loose areolar tissue over it.
!!
!danger The child's facial nerve
The mastoid tip is undeveloped in small children and the nerve leaves the skull superficially. Curve the incision back and stop short of the tip.
!!
## Expose the cortex and the landmarks
Incise the periosteum along the linea temporalis with a vertical limb to the tip (a "T" or "7"), elevate it forward to expose the posterior bony canal, lift the vascular strip, and retract the pinna forward with a tape or Penrose passed through the canal. Harvest temporalis fascia now and set it to dry. Identify the linea temporalis, the spine of Henle and the cribriform area.
!see What you should see
- The whole mastoid cortex from the root of the zygoma to the tip, the posterior canal wall, and MacEwen's triangle with its small pits.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Exposure decides the operation. If you cannot see the zygomatic root and the tip, you will drill a deep narrow hole, which is how facial nerves are injured.
!!
## Make the first cuts and saucerise
With the largest cutting bur that fits and continuous suction irrigation, make the first cut along the **linea temporalis**, the second parallel to the **posterior canal wall**, and join them behind along the line of the **sigmoid sinus**, forming a triangle. Remove the cortex and the air cells within it in long strokes parallel to the structure you are approaching, bevelling (saucerising) the edges widely so light and instruments reach the depth.
!mf 2 | The first bone cuts on the cortex outline the linea temporalis, the posterior canal wall and the sigmoid sinus.
!see What you should see
- A wide, shallow, kidney-shaped cavity with sloping edges, deepest at its front and top.
!!
!danger A deep hole
Keep **the deepest point over the antrum**, behind the spine of Henle. Beginners drift down and back, away from the antrum and into the plane of the facial nerve and the sigmoid sinus. If you cannot find the antrum, go forward and up, never down.
!!
## Define the tegmen and the sinus
Thin the bone above until the **tegmen** shows as a pink plate, and behind until the **sigmoid sinus** shows blue through its plate, leaving a thin shell on each. Open the sinodural angle between them. These two plates are the upper and posterior walls of your cavity and are followed forward to the antrum.
!see What you should see
- A smooth pink tegmen above, a blue sinus plate behind, and the posterior canal wall thinned in front.
!!
!danger Dura and sinus
A cutting bur run across exposed dura or the sinus tears it. Change to a diamond bur as you approach them, and stroke along them, not into them.
!!
## Enter the antrum
Using the tegmen as the roof and the canal wall as the front, remove **Koerner's septum** and enter the antrum. Confirm it by its size and by what lies in its floor: the dense, pale dome of the **lateral semicircular canal**.
!see What you should see
- A large air space or sac of disease, and in its floor the smooth ivory bulge of the lateral canal.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
If you think you are in the antrum but cannot see the lateral canal, you are probably still lateral to Koerner's septum. The antrum is deeper than you expect.
!!
## Open the aditus and find the incus
Change to a smaller bur and drill forward toward the root of the zygoma, between the tegmen and the canal wall, keeping the bur turning away from the ossicles, until the **short process of the incus** appears in the fossa incudis. Flood the cavity: the incus is often first seen as a refraction under the water. Remove remaining cells from the tip, exposing the **digastric ridge**, and from the sinodural angle, as the disease requires.
!mf 3 | A completed intact canal wall mastoidectomy: the aditus, the short process of the incus, the lateral semicircular canal and the digastric ridge.
!see What you should see
- The incus in its fossa, the lateral canal behind and below it, the digastric ridge at the tip, and an intact thin posterior canal wall.
!!
!danger Touching the incus
A rotating bur touching the incus transmits its energy to the inner ear and causes high-tone sensorineural loss. Leave a sliver of bone over the short process and remove it with a pick or curette.
!!
## Close
Replace the vascular strip and pack the canal with Gelfoam or Telfa. Close the periosteum (which stops the scar retracting into the cavity), then the deep dermis with 3-0 or 4-0 absorbable sutures and the skin with a fine absorbable suture. Apply a mastoid dressing for 24 hours. Count the pledgets.
!see What you should see
- The periosteum closed over the cavity, the vascular strip back in the canal and every pledget counted.
!!

# The operation: facial recess (posterior tympanotomy) | Into the middle ear with the canal wall intact.
## Thin the canal wall and set the landmarks
The recess is bounded by the **incus buttress** above, the **facial nerve** medially and behind, and the **chorda tympani** laterally and in front. Before entering it, have the lateral canal, the fossa incudis and the digastric ridge in view, and thin the posterior canal wall well: a thick wall hides the recess and forces you medially toward the nerve.
!see What you should see
- The short process of the incus pointing to the recess, and a canal wall thin enough to see instruments through.
!!
## Find the nerve and open the recess
With a small diamond bur and copious irrigation, drill just below the incus buttress in strokes **parallel to the course of the facial nerve**, never across it. Identify the nerve through a thin covering of bone as a pink-white cord with its fine vessels, and the chorda coming off it laterally. Enter the middle ear at the **upper end** of the recess, where it is widest.
!see What you should see
- The facial nerve seen through bone medially, the chorda laterally, and a dark slit between them opening into the middle ear.
!!
!danger Heat and the nerve
A diamond bur generates heat quickly; without irrigation it burns the nerve through intact bone. Keep the water running and leave a shell of bone on the nerve.
!!
## Widen and inspect
Widen the opening by removing bone medial to the chorda. Through it you see the incudostapedial joint, the stapes and stapedius tendon, the promontory and the round window niche. In an **extended facial recess**, the chorda is sacrificed and bone removed down to the inferior annulus to expose the hypotympanum and the medial surface of the drum.
!mf 4 | The middle ear through the completed facial recess: chorda tympani, incudostapedial joint, stapes, promontory and round window niche.
!see What you should see
- The incudostapedial joint and round window niche, with the sinus tympani hidden medial to the nerve (use an angled endoscope for it).
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Dissect disease off the stapes from back to front, in the direction of the stapedius tendon, which braces the stapes against your pull.
!!

# The operation: canal wall down | The open cavity.
## Decide and deal with the incus
Complete the cortical mastoidectomy first. If the incus is diseased or the chain must be interrupted, **separate the incudostapedial joint before** taking the wall down, so that drilling on the bridge cannot be transmitted to the stapes. If the incus can stay, leave a thin bridge of bone over its short process and remove it with a pick or curette.
!see What you should see
- A divided incudostapedial joint, or a protected incus under a thin bony bridge.
!!
## Take down the wall
Remove the posterior canal wall with a 4 mm cutting bur. Think of it as three parts: the **anterior buttress** (at the tympanosquamous suture and the lateral attic wall), the **posterior buttress** (which becomes the facial ridge), and the **bridge** between them over the aditus. Remove the bridge, then lower both buttresses so the attic roof runs smoothly into the tegmen and the anterior attic is open.
!mf 5 | A completed canal wall down cavity.
!see What you should see
- The attic, antrum and mastoid as one cavity with the canal, with no overhang at the anterior attic.
!!
## Lower the facial ridge
Thin the bone over the second genu and vertical segment with a diamond bur, in strokes parallel to the nerve, until the ridge is level with the floor of the canal and the nerve is covered by only a thin plate. A **high facial ridge** leaves a sump behind it that collects debris and discharges for life.
!see What you should see
- A low, smooth ridge with the nerve's course visible through bone, the lateral canal dome above it, and a round rather than kidney-shaped cavity.
!!
!danger The second genu
This is where the nerve is injured: just below the lateral canal, at the level of the fossa incudis. Above the canal you are safe; at and below it, use the diamond bur and the monitor.
!!
## Saucerise and remove the disease
Bevel the upper and posterior edges of the cavity widely so the soft tissues fall in and reduce its volume, and remove the tip cells or amputate the tip so the cavity has no dependent pocket. Remove the cholesteatoma matrix in continuity from the mastoid forward. Over a **fistula of the lateral canal**, leave the matrix until the end, then either leave it in place (exteriorised) or lift it under irrigation and cover the fistula at once with fascia or bone pate.
!see What you should see
- A shallow, smooth bowl with no overhanging edges and no cells left full of disease.
!!
!danger The open labyrinth
Suction on an open fistula, or peeling matrix off it carelessly, causes a dead ear. Never suction directly on it.
!!
## Reconstruct and graft
Pack the middle ear with Gelfoam. Lay the dried fascia as an underlay graft, draped onto the tympanic facial nerve above and the facial ridge behind, to close off a shallow middle ear space, with ossicular reconstruction now or at a second stage. Pack lateral to the graft and drum remnant.
!see What you should see
- The graft draped from the facial ridge to the tympanic facial nerve, closing a shallow middle ear under the drum remnant.
!!
## Meatoplasty
Always perform a meatoplasty when the wall is taken down: the opening must let you see and clean the upper, posterior and lower parts of the bowl, and it will contract as it heals, so make it generous. Either extend the upper canal incision into the **incisura** and suture the conchal bowl back to the postauricular tissues with 3-0 absorbable sutures (too aggressive and the ear cups forward), or remove a **crescent of conchal cartilage** from behind, leaving the anterior skin, and suture the meatal skin back to the periosteum behind the cavity. Stent the meatus with rolled Telfa, an expanding wick or antibiotic gauze for 10 to 14 days.
!see What you should see
- A meatus that admits a fingertip and shows the whole cavity with the microscope.
!!

# Other operations | Bondy and radical.
## Bondy modified radical mastoidectomy
For an attic cholesteatoma that has grown **lateral to the ossicles** with an intact pars tensa and middle ear, often in an only or better hearing ear: the disease is exteriorised through an endaural approach, the anterior buttress removed and the superior canal wall lowered to the level of the incus, without entering the middle ear. The matrix is left as the lining of the exteriorised attic.
## Radical mastoidectomy
For irreversible middle ear disease that a canal wall down procedure cannot control. After a canal wall down mastoidectomy, the drum remnant, malleus and incus are removed (the stapes is kept), the mucosa is removed, the **Eustachian tube orifice is obliterated** with muscle, fat or fascia, and no reconstruction is attempted: the whole middle ear is exteriorised into the cavity.
## Reconstruction and obliteration
Rebuilding the canal wall gives the exposure of an open operation without a cavity, at the cost of foreign material in a contaminated field, and needs a second look. Obliterating the cavity makes long-term care easier but can bury matrix.

# When it goes wrong | Drills at the drill.
## Crisis drills
!path Bleeding from the sigmoid sinus or jugular bulb
Do not panic or pack blindly into the lumen | take the bur away
Control | a large piece of Surgicel or adrenaline-soaked Gelfoam over the hole with a flat pledget and gentle pressure ; lower the head slightly if air embolism is a concern
Continue | leave the patch in place and work elsewhere ; bone wax for emissary vein bleeding
!!
!path Dura exposed or torn, CSF in the field
Exposed dura | healthy exposed dura under 1 cm needs nothing
Defect over 1 cm or any tear | repair now: fascia under and over the defect, cartilage or bone for a large gap, tissue glue
Afterwards | head up, no straining ; watch for meningitis
!!
!path Labyrinth opened (snail-track, endosteum or fluid seen)
Stop | no suction on it
Seal | cover at once with fascia, bone wax or bone pate
Afterwards | steroids and antibiotics ; test hearing and document
!!
!path Facial weakness in recovery
Immediate, complete | wait for local anaesthetic to wear off (a few hours) ; if it persists and you did not positively see an intact nerve, re-explore early
Complete, but you are sure the nerve is intact | steroids, eye care, electroneuronography after 3 days ; explore if degeneration exceeds 90%
Integrity in doubt | electromyography and CT ; re-explore, decompress, or graft
Delayed or partial | loosen the packing, steroids, eye care ; recovery is the rule
!!

# Aftercare | The cavity must heal dry.
## After the operation
In recovery, check the **face**, a **tuning fork** (Weber should go to the operated ear) and for **nystagmus**. Most patients go home the same or next day with restricted activity, keeping the ear dry. Remove the meatal packing at 10 to 14 days and start antibiotic-steroid drops or rinses. An open cavity needs regular cleaning in clinic until it is fully epithelialised (often 6 to 12 weeks) and then at intervals for life. After an intact canal wall operation for cholesteatoma, plan a **second look** at 9 to 12 months or follow with diffusion-weighted MRI.
## Complications
!table Complications of mastoid surgery
Complication | Notes
Bleeding, haematoma | Usually the sigmoid sinus, jugular bulb or emissary vein; pressure with Surgicel or Gelfoam
Facial paresis or palsy | Under 1% in primary and 4% to 10% in revision surgery; the second genu is the commonest site; mastoid surgery accounts for most otological facial nerve injuries
Conductive hearing loss | Ossicular or drum injury; reconstruct
Sensorineural loss, vertigo | Drill on the incus, stapes subluxation, fistula of the lateral canal or opened labyrinth
Dural injury, CSF leak, encephalocele | Repair defects over 1 cm and all tears
Altered taste | Chorda stretched or cut; often recovers in months
Wound infection, perichondritis | Antibiotics; drain a collection
Discharging cavity | High facial ridge, inadequate saucerisation, small meatus, residual cells, tubal reflux
Residual or recurrent cholesteatoma | More frequent after intact canal wall surgery; second look or MRI
Meatal stenosis, cupped ear | Too small or too aggressive a meatoplasty
!!
## Alternatives
If surgery for cholesteatoma is deferred (frail patient, only hearing ear), regular microsuction, home irrigation and intermittent topical or systemic antibiotics may control the disease.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: describes three classical operations. Cortical (Schwartze) mastoidectomy exenterates the mastoid cells without disturbing the canal wall or middle ear, for acute coalescent mastoiditis and as an approach. Radical mastoidectomy makes the mastoid, middle ear, attic and antrum one cavity, removing the drum remnant, malleus and incus and closing the Eustachian tube. Modified radical mastoidectomy removes the disease and the canal wall but preserves as much of the drum, ossicles and hearing as possible. It gives the antrum's depth as about 12 to 15 mm beneath MacEwen's triangle, and stresses lowering the facial ridge, saucerisation and an adequate meatoplasty.
Lee: frames the choice as canal wall up versus canal wall down, with higher residual and recurrent disease after canal wall up surgery balanced against the lifelong cavity of the open technique.
Myers gives the landmark-by-landmark sequence, the facial recess, and the evidence on facial nerve injury and monitoring.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Drilling a deep, narrow hole instead of saucerising.
- Drifting down and back in search of the antrum.
- Mistaking Koerner's septum for the medial wall of the antrum.
- Touching the incus with the bur.
- A small bur, or a cutting bur, deep at the tip; a diamond bur without irrigation over the nerve.
- A high facial ridge and an inadequate meatoplasty.
- Using muscle relaxant with the nerve monitor on.
!!
