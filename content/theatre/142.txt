@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand Glomus tumours, paragangliomas, are slow-growing, invasive, highly vascular neuroendocrine tumours from neural crest chemoreceptor cells. In the temporal bone there are two kinds with very different stakes. The **glomus tympanicum** arises on the promontory of the middle ear; it rarely reaches the lower cranial nerves, so treatment risks only the facial nerve and the inner ear, and surgery, through the canal or a mastoidectomy, cures it. The **glomus jugulare** arises in the jugular foramen on the upper and lateral wall of the jugular bulb, beside the carotid and the nerves of the pars nervosa (IX, X, XI and sometimes XII), whose loss leaves patients unable to swallow, speak or lift the shoulder. Two decades ago the answer was aggressive total removal; now, because radiation controls these tumours well and nerve loss is so disabling, the trend is to preserve function: observation, radiation, or subtotal removal that leaves the medial wall of the bulb, followed by radiation of the remnant. This chapter covers assessment, the Fisch and Glasscock-Jackson classifications, surgery for tympanicum and jugulare tumours, facial nerve management, and the evidence for each strategy.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Glomus tumours of the temporal bone
 | Summary
Aim | Halt troublesome symptoms (pulsatile tinnitus, hearing loss, dizziness, dysphagia, hoarseness, shoulder weakness, catecholamine effects) without adding new ones
Tympanicum | Most symptomatic tumours removed with little risk: transcanal for small tumours, postauricular with mastoidectomy and extended facial recess for larger ones
Jugulare | Many removable completely with modest risk **unless** the pars nervosa, significant carotid or intracranial extension is involved; then consider observation, subtotal resection with observation or radiation, or primary radiation
Small to medium tumours | Observation with serial MRI or primary radiation are options
Contraindications | Age and comorbidity making anaesthesia too risky; existing dysphagia (from stroke or other disease) that would not tolerate further lower cranial nerve injury
Preparation | Alpha and beta blockade for catecholamine excess; **embolisation 1 to 2 days before** most jugulare surgery; balloon occlusion with xenon perfusion (or a protective stent) if the carotid is at risk; cross-match 2 to 4 units for jugulare tumours
Anaesthesia and position | General; supine, head turned away
Antibiotics | First-generation cephalosporin; clindamycin in allergy
Monitoring | Facial nerve; IX to XII for extensive jugulare tumours; somatosensory potentials if the carotid is involved
Tray | Microscope; ear set; otological drill; neurosurgical set and retractors for intracranial extension; haemostatic agents (Surgicel, Avitene, Surgifoam)
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to remove a benign but growing, very vascular tumour of the ear and skull base, or to control it.
- **Alternatives:** watching it with scans, or focused radiation, which stops growth in most cases but does not remove it.
- **Bleeding**, sometimes heavy; transfusion may be needed.
- **Hearing loss and dizziness.**
- **Facial weakness**, especially if the facial nerve has to be moved.
- **Swallowing, voice, shoulder and tongue weakness** if the lower cranial nerves are injured; a feeding tube or tracheostomy and voice surgery may be needed. Leaving tumour on these nerves reduces this risk.
- **Leak of brain fluid**, stroke and carotid injury in large tumours.
- **Family screening** if the tumour is of the inherited type.
!!
## Before you start
About **20%** are familial; these are more often multicentric and bilateral and present younger in successive generations. Ask about **pulsatile tinnitus**, hearing loss, vertigo or imbalance, otalgia, facial weakness or twitching, dysphagia, hoarseness, shoulder weakness or pain, dysarthria and tongue weakness, and **labile hypertension, tachycardia, headache, sweating, pallor and nausea**, which suggest catecholamine release needing medical treatment. Record previous surgery or radiation, heart and lung disease, bleeding disorders and a family history of vascular or endocrine tumours.

Look at the ear with the microscope or endoscope: a vascular middle ear mass, drum involvement, canal extension, and **Brown's sign** (the mass blanches when the drum is pressurised). Test with forks, gait, tandem gait and Romberg, the facial and lower cranial nerves, and feel for a mass in the upper neck. Obtain pure tones, speech discrimination and a tympanogram.
## Imaging
- **CT** of the temporal bone at 0.6 mm: soft tissue in the middle ear, attic or mastoid; erosion along the **jugular bulb and foramen**; carotid involvement; posterior petrous erosion (posterior fossa extension); otic capsule erosion.
- **MRI with gadolinium** of brain, internal auditory canal and neck: the enhancing tumour's extent, flow voids in the sigmoid, bulb and jugular vein, and synchronous tumours.
- **Angiography** (four-vessel), usually at embolisation: tumour blush, feeders, other vascular tumours. MR or CT angiography is a less sensitive alternative.

# Anatomy you must see | Nerves and vessels at the foramen.
## Landmarks
- **The facial nerve** in its horizontal and vertical segments, found from the oval window niche, lateral canal and stylomastoid foramen.
- **The lower cranial nerves** leaving the jugular foramen: **IX medial to the jugular vein**; **X between the jugular vein and carotid**; **XI crossing lateral to the jugular vein** toward the posterior neck; **XII lateral to the carotid**, running forward deep to the digastric.
- **The petrous carotid**, medial to the Eustachian tube, foramen ovale and foramen spinosum.
- **The medial wall of the jugular bulb**, behind which lies the pars nervosa: leave it intact and avoid pressing on it to keep the nerves.

# The operation: glomus tympanicum | Through the canal or the mastoid.
## Choose the route
Small tumours of the meso- and hypotympanum without bulb involvement come out **through the canal**, if the canal anatomy allows. Larger ones need a **postauricular** approach, perhaps with mastoidectomy and facial recess, and a **canaloplasty** if the inferior annulus cannot be seen because of a tortuous canal or hypotympanic extension.
## The tympanomeatal flap
Inject the canal and postauricular sulcus. For the postauricular route, make a lateral and posterior canal skin flap with superior, inferior and connecting incisions halfway down the bony canal; incise 3 mm behind the sulcus, join the incisions, retract the meatal flap with a Penrose and the pinna with a Weitlaner. Raise the tympanomeatal flap and enter the middle ear medial to the fibrous annulus, lifting it as far forward as the tumour requires, **without cutting into the tumour**.
!mf 2 | The transtympanic approach to a glomus tumour confined to the middle ear.
## Find and divide the pedicle
For small tumours the vascular pedicle is usually on the **promontory**: manipulate the tumour gently until it shows, divide it, and control bleeding with bone wax or Surgicel. Use microbipolar sparingly: too much risks the cochlea.
## Larger tumours
Larger tumours may have pedicles in the hypotympanum and protympanum that cannot be reached before debulking: shrink with bipolar, debulk, then find and divide them; adrenaline-soaked pledgets help. If the tumour reaches the attic or mastoid, add a **canal wall up mastoidectomy**. Remove the middle ear, hypotympanic and protympanic tumour first to take its blood supply.
## The ossicles and the footplate
The incus and malleus head may need removing (reconstruct afterwards), or only a temporary separation of the incudostapedial joint. Tumour in the obturator foramen or on the footplate can be vaporised with a **diode or argon laser** (0.2 mm fibre, 1 to 2 W, 0.2 s single pulses) or a CO2 laser at 2 to 5 W, sparing the footplate and horizontal facial nerve.
## Toward the bulb
Rarely the tumour extends below the hypotympanum toward the bulb (usually without invading its wall, so the sigmoid need not be opened): extend the **infratympanic facial recess**, and if needed skeletonise the distal vertical facial nerve as a **fallopian bridge**.
!mf 3 | (A) The extended facial recess approach to the middle ear and hypotympanum, with retrofacial infralabyrinthine cells opened to show the tumour's back edge; (B) the operative view.
## Close
Repair any drum perforation with a medial graft, pack the middle ear with Gelfoam or Surgicel and the canal with Gelfoam, close in layers and apply a mastoid dressing.

# The operation: glomus jugulare | Ear, neck and bulb.
## Incision and neck control
Most involve the middle ear, bulb and hypotympanic cells without intracranial spread (Fisch C1 to C2, Glasscock-Jackson jugulare I and II). Make a postauricular incision **3 cm behind the pinna**, over the mastoid tip and into an upper neck crease toward the greater horn of the hyoid, through platysma, preserving the great auricular nerve. Without cervical tumour, the neck dissection is for **inferior control of the jugular vein**; keep it separate from the mastoid wound so CSF cannot leak into the neck if the dura is opened.
!mf 4 | The postauricular incision carried into the neck: carotid, jugular vein and lower cranial nerves isolated, sternomastoid and digastric retracted.
## Temporal bone and fallopian bridge
Perform the mastoid dissection described above. Open the facial recess, sacrificing the chorda, as an **extended facial recess**; remove the retrofacial and infralabyrinthine cells to the stylomastoid foramen, skeletonising the nerve to eggshell as a **fallopian bridge**.
## Expose the sigmoid and bulb
Outline the digastric ridge and continue forward to the stylomastoid foramen, removing the mastoid tip if needed. Expose the posterior fossa dura in front of and behind the sigmoid and decompress the sinus, **leaving a shelf of bone over its proximal part** near the sinodural angle (above Donaldson's line), and free the sinus from its bony roof. Follow it down to the bulb, removing all bone.
!mf 5 | A canal wall down dissection isolating the sigmoid sinus, the vertical facial nerve along the extended recess, the digastric ridge, the neck vessels and the lower cranial nerves.
## Control the sigmoid above and the jugular below
Before opening the sigmoid, gain control: pack **Surgicel extraluminally** under the bony shelf over the proximal sinus, or, if that fails, **ligate** it: make small dural openings in front of and behind the sinus, pass an aneurysm needle from back to front hugging its deep surface (away from the cerebellum), draw through a long 2-0 silk, cut it into two ligatures, and tie both over a small piece of muscle. Doubly ligate the jugular vein in the neck. If tumour enters the cervical jugular vein, dissect its proximal stump up to the jugular fossa, passing it medial to the **accessory nerve**, which must be preserved; otherwise leave the tied vein. Keep the distal extratemporal carotid in view; remove the styloid and its muscles if needed.
!mf 6 | The extended facial recess exposing tumour in the hypotympanum, the vertical facial nerve left in place; the proximal sigmoid controlled by extraluminal packing or suture ligation.
!mf 7 | The proximal sigmoid controlled; the jugular vein divided and its proximal segment dissected into the jugular fossa, the carotid marking the anterior plane.
## Remove the tumour
Remove tumour piecemeal down to the remnant around the sigmoid and bulb, with bipolar and adrenaline 1:1000 pledgets. Open the sigmoid below the ligatures, incising its inferior wall lengthwise toward the bulb; its medial wall is usually free unless there is intracranial spread. Free the tumour from back to front, separating the superior, lateral and inferior walls from the medial wall, and dissect the lateral and upper bulb.
## The inferior petrosal sinus
Once the tumour comes out of the bulb, **brisk bleeding** comes from the openings of the inferior petrosal sinus and condylar vein: pack the medial bulb with oxidised cellulose. This is done deep to the vertical facial nerve: do not press on its deep surface. Remove the remaining hypotympanic and middle ear tumour from both sides of the nerve.
## Leave the pars nervosa
Dissection **medial to the medial wall of the bulb** means dissecting the lower cranial nerves. The current trend is **not to remove** tumour there unless absolutely necessary, and to observe or irradiate it.
!danger The medial wall of the bulb
Overaggressive dissection of the pars nervosa leaves lasting dysphagia, hoarseness and shoulder weakness. Leave the medial wall, and do not compress it with packing.
!!
## When to go canal wall down
For more exposure of tumour invading the carotid, or when the retrofacial and infralabyrinthine space is too tight (which may also mean **transposing the facial nerve**); the drum and ossicles can still be reconstructed.
## Close
CSF is met only if the medial bulb, sigmoid or posterior fossa dura is breached; then partly obliterate dead space with abdominal fat, otherwise leave it aerated. Pack the canal as before. Use a Penrose under the neck flaps after significant neck dissection (a suction drain should be turned off after a day, as it sucks air through the Eustachian tube). Close in three layers.

# The operation: large jugulare tumours | Into the infratemporal fossa.
## Exposure
Tumours of the petrous apex, horizontal carotid or cavernous sinus (Fisch C2 and beyond) add a **postauricular infratemporal** exposure to the temporal bone dissection. The canal is usually removed and the meatus closed; after significant dural resection, close the Eustachian tube and obliterate the defect with abdominal fat.
## Facial nerve transposition
When the vertical facial nerve blocks access to a tumour filling the jugular fossa and invading the carotid, decompress it from the **geniculate ganglion to the stylomastoid foramen** (cutting then diamond burs; favourable anatomy may allow mobilisation from the second genu only). Divide the remaining tendon and posterior digastric within the mastoid next to the stylomastoid foramen, lift the muscle with the foramen's contents from back to front, free the nerve from the fallopian canal, and **suture its fibrous cuff forward to the periparotid fascia**.
!mf 8 | The facial nerve mobilised forward, exposing tumour in the mastoid, middle ear and bulb; the styloid tendons divided and the mandible retracted to isolate the carotid.

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Brisk bleeding from the bulb
Source | inferior petrosal sinus and condylar vein openings
Act | pack the medial bulb with oxidised cellulose ; avoid pressure on the facial nerve and pars nervosa
!!
!path CSF leak
Act | lumbar drainage 3 to 5 days ; pressure mastoid and neck dressings ; surgical repair if needed
Prevent | keep the neck wound separate ; fat obliteration ; Eustachian tube closure when the dura was resected
!!
!path New dysphagia or hoarseness
Assess | barium swallow or FEESST
Manage | diet to suit ; nasogastric or gastrostomy feeding while nil by mouth ; medialisation laryngoplasty, with or without cricopharyngeal myotomy
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Too little infratemporal exposure from incomplete planning.
- Not being ready to control a CSF leak.
- Overaggressive pars nervosa dissection.
- No plan for the carotid, inviting catastrophic bleeding or stroke.
!!

# Aftercare | Blood, CSF and the lower nerves.
## After the operation
Check the haematocrit after heavy bleeding. Watch for CSF rhinorrhoea, otorrhoea or a wound leak. Assess swallowing, voice and vocal fold movement, shoulder and tongue. Physiotherapy for shoulder weakness.
## Complications
Uncontrolled haemorrhage; facial and lower cranial nerve injury; cochleovestibular damage; CSF leak; carotid injury; arterial or venous stroke.
## Alternatives
Observation with serial imaging; radiation: single-fraction radiosurgery, fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy or external beam, as primary treatment, after recurrence or after planned subtotal resection.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## How well are they controlled?
Gross total resection rarely recurs, especially for tympanicum tumours. For jugulare tumours, a meta-analysis found control of **69%** after subtotal resection, **86%** after gross total resection, **71%** after subtotal resection with radiosurgery and **95%** with radiosurgery alone, though groups differed. Lower cranial nerve function is usually good if the pars nervosa is not dissected and often poor if it is. After radiosurgery (Martin and colleagues), existing neuropathies improved in **20%** and were stable in 77%, intact nerves were all preserved, and 5- and 10-year control was 97% and 94%.
## Genetics
About 20% are familial: four paraganglioma loci (PGL1 to 4), with **SDH** gene mutations inherited as autosomal dominant traits; finding one means searching for other tumours and screening relatives.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Tympanicum versus jugulare: origin, nerves at risk, treatment.
- Symptoms, Brown's sign, catecholamine excess and blockade.
- CT, MRI and angiography findings; embolisation 1 to 2 days before.
- Fisch and Glasscock-Jackson classifications.
- The nerves at the foramen and their relation to the jugular vein and carotid.
- The extended facial recess, the fallopian bridge, transposition.
- Controlling the sigmoid and jugular; the inferior petrosal sinus.
- Leaving the medial bulb wall; the function-preserving trend.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Operating without screening for catecholamine excess.
- Cutting into a tympanicum tumour before finding the pedicle.
- Opening the sigmoid before controlling it.
- Joining the neck and mastoid wounds.
!!
