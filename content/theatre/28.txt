@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand Partial glossectomy is the operation for early cancer of the oral tongue: most are squamous cell carcinomas on the lateral border, and they spread early to the neck. Early cancers are controlled equally well by surgery or radiotherapy, but surgery leaves fewer long-term effects. Before you start, three questions decide the operation: how far does the cancer extend, and can it come out through the mouth; has it reached the neck, and what neck dissection is needed; and will the defect need reconstruction? In theatre the finger, not the eye, sets the margin, because infiltrating cancer extends beyond what you can see, and the lingual artery at the back of the wound is ligated, not just cauterised. This chapter covers staging and selection, the margin and how to deal with a close one at the time, haemostasis, when to graft or flap, the elective neck, and the bleed two weeks later.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Partial glossectomy
 | Summary
Indications | **T1 and T2 squamous cell carcinoma of the oral tongue**; other tumours arising in the oral tongue
Not suitable | Locally advanced (most T3 and T4) cancers, which need hemiglossectomy or subtotal glossectomy; unacceptable comorbidity; poor functional status; lack of social support; the patient does not consent
Neck | Selective dissection of levels I to III, and IV, for stage 1 and 2; **comprehensive** dissection if imaging shows nodes; bilateral when the cancer nears the midline, the anterior floor of mouth or the base of tongue; sentinel node biopsy is an alternative in selected cN0 cases
Anaesthesia | General, oral or nasal intubation as access, teeth, site and preference dictate; full muscle relaxation helps mouth opening and stops the tongue moving under diathermy
Position | Supine
Antibiotics | Not needed if the mouth will not communicate with the neck; cover oral flora if it will (as with free flap reconstruction)
Tray | Neck dissection soft tissue set; side mouth retractor and bite block; guarded diathermy tip; headlight
Prerequisites | Diagnostic endoscopy, oral cavity anatomy, neck dissection
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to remove the cancer with a margin of healthy tongue, usually with an operation on the neck at the same time.
- **Speech and swallowing** change; how well they recover depends on doing the exercises afterwards.
- **Bleeding,** most dangerous in the first 2 to 3 weeks: if you spit bright red blood or clots at home, call an ambulance.
- **A further operation** if the margins are close or involved.
- **Further treatment** with radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, depending on the final pathology.
- **Pain, difficulty swallowing**, weight loss, and constipation from painkillers.
- **A tracheostomy** if a graft or flap is needed.
- **Infection** of the neck if the mouth and neck connect; chest infection from aspiration.
!!
## Before you start
Ask how long the lesion has been there and who found it, and whether it has been biopsied. Cancer pain is dull and persistent for weeks or months, "a sore"; **otalgia** suggests deep infiltration. Ask about dysphagia, change of diet, weight loss, dysarthria and a neck lump: a greater symptom burden before treatment predicts worse survival, independent of stage. Record previous treatment and analgesia, smoking and alcohol (how much), cardiac and respiratory symptoms, and the support at home, which will shape rehabilitation.

Examine the size and depth of the cancer; whether it is exophytic, infiltrative or ulcerated; extension to the floor of mouth; the teeth; trismus; invasion of the mandible directly or through tooth sockets; extension into the base of tongue on palpation; and the nasopharynx, larynx and hypopharynx with a mirror or endoscope. Palpate the neck for nodes and their levels.
!mf 1 | (A) An irregular plaque with raised edges suggests invasion at least 2 to 3 mm deep; palpation tells you more. (B) Partial glossectomy removes an adequate margin of mucosa and muscle; at the back of the oral tongue it often needs no formal reconstruction.
!senior Feel it
Tongue cancer may be a beefy red patch, an ulcer or arise in leukoplakia. A thin cancer (under about 2 to 3 mm) feels different from a deeply infiltrating one, which may be a hard mass under normal mucosa. **Deviation of the protruded tongue** means hypoglossal nerve infiltration or invasion of the extrinsic muscles.
!!
!mf 2 | (A) Deviation of the tongue should raise concern about deep infiltration into the tongue muscles. (B) CT confirms involvement of the deep muscles.
## Imaging and preparation
Obtain a chest radiograph. Image the neck and primary with contrast CT or gadolinium MRI when deep (extrinsic) muscle involvement or neck disease is suspected, and CT when bone invasion is a concern. Imaging is more accurate than examination for nodes, and nodes on imaging **upstage** the cancer and call for a comprehensive rather than selective dissection. Review the outside biopsy and scans. Consider formal detoxification for heavy drinkers with a history of delirium tremens; offer smoking cessation; stop antiplatelet drugs if possible; arrange medical and anaesthetic assessment and dental review with extractions as needed. Cross-match is not usually necessary.
!senior Rehabilitation starts before surgery
Exercises for trismus, tongue mobility and neck and shoulder movement work best if started before dysfunction sets in. Involving the speech therapist and physiotherapist around the operation improves adherence.
!!

# Anatomy you must see | The vessel and the nerve.
## Landmarks
- **The lingual artery,** met posterolaterally in the tongue as you complete the deep cut: it is the source of both intraoperative bleeding and the dangerous late bleed.
- **The lingual nerve** in the floor of the mouth.
## Patterns of spread
Larger and deeper cancers are more likely to metastasise. Lateral cancers usually spread to the same side of the neck, but the risk to the other side rises as the cancer nears the midline; involvement of the **anterior floor of mouth or base of tongue** brings more bilateral and contralateral metastasis and calls for bilateral neck dissection.

# The operation, stage by stage | Endoscopy, resection, margins, neck.
## Endoscopy first
Under the same anaesthetic, perform direct laryngoscopy and oesophagoscopy to look for a **synchronous primary**.
## Airway and exposure
A tracheostomy is rarely needed unless a skin graft or free flap is planned; then make it **before** the resection, which also improves exposure, and secure the tube to the neck skin to keep the tubing out of the way. Open the mouth and retract the cheek with retractors, use a headlight, and pass a suture through the midline of the tip to draw the tongue out.
## Mark the margin with your finger
Feel along the cancer with your index finger and make the first incisions about **1 cm** into normal tongue around what you feel, not just what you see. Use a guarded diathermy tip so the lips are not burnt. Place a silk suture on the anterior specimen for retraction and orientation.
!danger Cutting into the cancer
Infiltrating cancer extends further than it looks. Palpate again and again during the resection; a cut through the tumour gives a positive margin and worse local control. Bleeding that hides the field makes this more likely.
!!
## Ligate the lingual artery
As the deep cut reaches the back of the wound, clamp and ligate the **lingual artery**. Deep sutures in the posterior tongue muscle looped around the artery reduce the risk of a severe late bleed. Ligation is more effective than extensive cautery and gives less pain and dysphagia afterwards. Closing the residual tongue primarily does not lower the bleeding risk and makes little difference to function.
!see What you should see
- A specimen with a cuff of muscle all round, and a dry bed with the lingual artery tied posteriorly.
!!
## Check the margins now
Ink and open the specimen with the pathologist to judge the gross margin; a **gross margin under 5 mm** calls for more resection. If any margin is in doubt, assess the whole margin, peripheral and deep, all the way round. Mark the residual tongue on the patient's side with **blue dye** and excise and orient a further layer **2 to 4 mm** thick, mucosa and deep muscle; this layer becomes the true margin and avoids sampling error. While frozen sections are processed, do the neck dissection.
!senior Fix it today
A close or positive margin is best dealt with during the first operation. If it is found only on final pathology, readmit the patient for further resection.
!!
## Close, graft or flap
Most T1 and many T2 defects are left to **heal by secondary intention**. A substantial loss of floor of mouth mucosa tethers the tongue and slurs speech; a split-thickness skin graft from the thigh (0.016 to 0.018 inch), firmly secured over a bolster, or a radial forearm free flap reduces contracture. Hemiglossectomy and subtotal glossectomy defects are usually reconstructed with microvascular tissue from the forearm or thigh. Larger, deeply infiltrating cancers may need a transcervical delivery or a mandibulotomy for safe resection, and bone invasion needs a segmental mandibulectomy.
## The neck dissection
For stage 1 and 2 disease, a selective dissection of levels I, II, III and possibly IV. If the mouth and neck connect, repair the communication, give antibiotics and place a drain.
!senior Level IV and skip metastases
The editor includes level IV in every elective dissection for lateral tongue cancer, because a small number of patients have **skip metastases** to level IV with nothing in levels I to III.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Bleeding during the resection
Expect it | from the lingual artery, posterolaterally
Control | clamp and ligate early ; cautery alone is usually not enough
Then | resume palpating the margin once the field is dry
!!
!path Bleeding at home in the first 2 to 3 weeks
Patient calls | spitting bright red blood or clots: call an ambulance to the nearest emergency department
Assess | airway first
Treat | ligation in theatre with a secure airway is the safest ; suture ligation at the bedside only for a well seen site in a stable, cooperative patient
!!
!path Close margin on frozen section
Mark | blue dye on the residual tongue
Excise | a further oriented layer 2 to 4 mm thick, mucosa and deep muscle
Assess | the whole margin, peripheral and deep
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Operating without a confirmed diagnosis, or after inadequate staging.
- Inadequate or positive margins; cutting through the tumour.
- Poor haemostasis, which hides the field.
- Burning the lips with an unguarded diathermy tip; damaging the teeth.
!!

# Aftercare | Eat, speak, watch for bleeding.
## After the operation
Without reconstruction, most patients take a modified oral diet soon after surgery if pain is controlled; high-calorie drinks prevent weight loss. After a skin graft or free flap, feed by nasogastric tube until the wound has healed. Opioids are usually used rather than NSAIDs because of the bleeding risk, with stool softeners, fluids and laxatives. Arrange a routine speech and swallowing assessment early. Further treatment depends on the final pathology.
## Complications
!table Complications of partial glossectomy
Complication | Notes
Tongue oedema | Uncommon without reconstruction; tracheostomy at the time if reconstructing or if oedema develops; it can cause aspiration
Haemorrhage | Within 2 to 3 weeks; often needs theatre
Neck infection | From a communication between mouth and neck; explore and drain
Aspiration pneumonia | Especially with oedema; a temporary tracheostomy helps pulmonary toilet
Dehydration and weight loss | Supplement nutrition
Pain, dysphagia, dysarthria | Expected; reduced by early rehabilitation
Constipation | From opioids; use a bowel regimen
Medical | Cardiac events, venous thrombosis
!!
## Alternatives
- **External beam radiotherapy.**
- **Supportive or palliative care.**

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## The elective neck
In the large randomised trial of D'Cruz and colleagues, elective neck dissection in node-negative patients improved **overall and disease-free survival** compared with waiting for nodes to appear. Nodal metastasis fell from **16.9% at 4 mm** depth of invasion to **5.6% at 3 mm**, so a neck dissection may be avoidable in cancers **3 mm deep or less**, though this needs more study. Sentinel node biopsy finds micrometastases in experienced hands, but unlike selective neck dissection it has not been shown equivalent in a randomised trial. Early-stage disease has 85 to 90% five-year cancer-free survival; nodal metastasis halves it.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- The three questions before surgery: the primary, the neck, the reconstruction.
- Depth of invasion and nodal risk; the 3 mm threshold.
- Signs of deep infiltration: tongue deviation, otalgia, a hard mass.
- Margin: 1 cm by palpation; gross margin under 5 mm means more resection; the blue-dye re-resection of 2 to 4 mm.
- Ligation of the lingual artery, and the late bleed.
- When to leave the wound open, graft or flap.
- Elective neck levels, including IV, and when to go bilateral.
- Endoscopy for a synchronous primary.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Trusting the eye rather than the finger for the margin.
- Relying on cautery for the lingual artery.
- Omitting the neck in a thick cancer.
- Forgetting rehabilitation and support at home.
!!
