@stand A bone-anchored hearing device bypasses the outer and middle ear: a titanium screw osseointegrates in the skull behind the ear and carries sound vibrations straight to the cochlea. It helps two groups: the ear that cannot use a hearing aid (atresia, a discharging cavity, a closed canal) with a usable cochlea, and single-sided deafness, where it carries sound across the head to the good ear. The operation is short; the craft lies in a perpendicular fixture in good bone and skin that stays healthy for decades.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Bone-anchored hearing device
 | Summary
Indications | Conductive or mixed loss with a bone-conduction average of about 45 dB or better (55 dB for the most powerful processors) where a conventional aid or middle ear surgery is unsuitable: atresia, chronic discharge or a mastoid cavity, a closed canal after temporal bone resection, mould allergy. Single-sided deafness with a normal other ear (air-conduction average 20 dB or better)
Contraindications | Bone too thin or poor quality; inability to care for the site; unrealistic expectations; MRI needs (magnet devices)
Trial | Test the device on a softband or headband before surgery
Anaesthesia | Local or general (general in children)
Position | Supine, head turned, hair shaved around the site
Choice | Percutaneous abutment (stronger sound, skin care for life) or transcutaneous magnet (passive or active; intact skin, weaker passive transmission)
Tray | Device drill system (guide drill, countersink, fixture inserter), templates, soft tissue gauge, otology drill, bone wax, methylene blue
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Skin reactions** around a percutaneous abutment, from redness to overgrowth, are the commonest problem.
- **Failure of osseointegration** or later loss of the implant, needing revision or a new site.
- **Numbness** of the scalp, especially with a transcutaneous flap; **pain or pressure** under a magnet.
- **Bleeding,** rarely from the dura or sigmoid sinus; very rarely a **CSF leak.**
- **MRI:** magnets may need removal or cause artefact.
- The processor is fitted after healing: weeks for modern fixtures.
!!
## Before you start
Confirm the indication with pure tone and speech audiometry, and a trial on a softband: the patient should hear the difference before agreeing to an operation. In children, the skull must be thick enough (traditionally from about 5 years, staged under that). Choose the site 50 to 70 mm behind the canal, level with the top of the auricle, away from glasses, previous scars and where a future microtia reconstruction will go.
!mf 1 | For a transcutaneous device the site is marked and a C-shaped incision planned around it.

# The operation, stage by stage | Transcutaneous and percutaneous fixtures.
## Mark and incise
Mark the site with the template and tattoo the bone through the skin with methylene blue on a fine needle. Inject local anaesthetic with adrenaline. For a transcutaneous magnet make a C-shaped incision at least 15 mm from the magnet, based posteriorly, and elevate above the periosteum until the dye is found. For a percutaneous abutment use a short linear incision beside the site, or a punch through the skin (the minimally invasive technique).
!mf 4 | For a percutaneous abutment a 2 to 3 cm incision exposes the bone beside the planned site.
!see What you should see
- The blue mark on intact periosteum, on a flat area of squamous temporal bone.
!!
## Choose the bone
Make a cruciate incision in the periosteum and inspect the bone. Move a few millimetres if the surface dips: place the fixture on a "mountain", not in a "valley", so that a magnet can vibrate without touching bone.
!see What you should see
- A flat, solid bone surface large enough for the fixture and its magnet.
!!
## Drill the guide hole
Use the guide drill with its spacer at about 2000 rpm under copious irrigation, *perpendicular to the bone*. Probe the hole: bone at the bottom is needed for integration. If 3 mm of bone is present, remove the spacer and drill to 4 mm; the 4 mm fixture is preferred in adults.
!mf 2 | The guide drill, held perpendicular, with copious irrigation.
!see What you should see
- A clean hole with bone, not dura or sinus, at its base.
!!
!danger The tilted fixture
An angled guide hole tilts everything that follows: the countersink, the fixture and the magnet, which then catches on bone. Check the angle from two directions before you drill.
!!
## Countersink and insert the fixture
Widen the hole with the countersink at 2000 rpm, irrigating, until a shallow 360 degree ring appears. Load the fixture from its ampoule without touching it, irrigate away all bone dust, then screw it in at low speed (torque about 20 to 30 Ncm in soft bone, 40 to 50 Ncm in hard bone) with no irrigation until the first threads have engaged. The drill stops at the set torque.
!see What you should see
- The fixture flange sitting flush in the countersink ring, firm in the bone.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Titanium integrates only with bone that has not been burnt or contaminated. Keep the drill cool, keep your fingers off the fixture, and never force it.
!!
## Attach the magnet or abutment
**Transcutaneous:** rotate the bone bed indicator around the fixture and drill away any bone it catches; screw on the magnet (arrow up) to 25 Ncm; measure the flap and thin it *evenly* to 6 mm or less; place tacking sutures between magnet and incision to close the dead space, never over the magnet. **Percutaneous:** choose an abutment length for the skin thickness and keep the soft tissue (modern practice no longer thins it); bring the abutment through a punch hole or the incision.
!mf 3 | Tacking sutures close the dead space around the magnet, away from it.
!see What you should see
- A magnet that sits free of bone under a uniformly thin flap, or an abutment standing clear of the skin.
!!
## Close and dress
Close in layers. Dress a percutaneous abutment with a healing cap and folded antiseptic gauze under it; apply a light pressure dressing over a magnet flap.
!mf 5 | Folded iodine-impregnated gauze between the skin and the healing cap.
!see What you should see
- A dry wound, the abutment upright, and no tension on the skin.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Dura or sigmoid sinus exposed in the guide hole
Bleeding | bone wax and gentle pressure
Next | choose another site with adequate bone ; a 3 mm fixture if bone is thin everywhere
!!
!path Skin reaction around an abutment (Holgers grade)
Grade 1 to 2 | cleaning, topical antibiotic or steroid cream, silver nitrate
Grade 3 | oral antibiotics by culture ; triamcinolone injection
Overgrowth or grade 4 | longer abutment or surgical revision ; remove if it will not settle
!!
!path Implant loose or lost
Early | failure of integration: remove and re-site after healing
Late | trauma or infection: revise at a new site
!!

# Aftercare | Skin for life.
## After surgery
Remove the dressing at about a week. Clean around a percutaneous abutment daily with a soft brush. Fit the processor once the fixture is stable (commonly 3 to 6 weeks with modern fixtures). Review the skin regularly and teach the patient what a reaction looks like. Sound processors are upgraded over the years without further surgery.
## Complications
Skin reactions and overgrowth; failure of osseointegration and implant loss; revision surgery; numbness, flap thinning or magnet exposure; pain under the magnet; haematoma; dural or sinus injury.
!update Current evidence
**Complications.** A meta-analysis of 2,134 patients and 2,310 implants found Holgers grade 2 to 4 skin reactions in 2.4% to 38%, failure of osseointegration in 0% to 18% of adults (0% to 14% of children), revision in 1.7% to 34.5% and implant loss in 1.6% to 17.4%; major complications were rare (Kiringoda and Lustig, *Otol Neurotol* 2013).
**Keep the skin.** A systematic review of 381 implants placed without skin thinning found grade 3 reactions in 2.5% (punch) and 5.9% (linear incision), with shorter surgery and faster healing than skin-thinning techniques (Verheij et al., *Otol Neurotol* 2016). In a multicentre randomised trial, the minimally invasive punch technique matched the linear incision for inflammation and gave less numbness, better cosmesis and shorter surgery (Calon et al., *Otol Neurotol* 2018).
**Atresia.** Bone conduction implants give larger, more predictable hearing gains than atresiaplasty (Nadaraja et al., *Otol Neurotol* 2013).
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Myers: detailed technique for the transcutaneous magnet and the percutaneous abutment, with the move from skin thinning to tissue preservation.
K. J. Lee and the Oxford books: indications by bone-conduction thresholds, single-sided deafness, and the softband trial before surgery.
The literature: tissue preservation and the punch technique reduce skin problems and operating time.
!!

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Implanting without a softband trial.
- Drilling at an angle.
- Burning the bone or touching the fixture.
- Thinning only the skin over the magnet, leaving a step.
- Placing the device where a future microtia reconstruction will go.
!!
