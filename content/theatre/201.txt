@stand Branchial anomalies are the second commonest congenital neck lesions after thyroglossal cysts. Each follows the embryology of its arch: a first cleft tract runs towards the ear canal through, or past, the facial nerve; a second cleft tract runs up the anterior border of sternomastoid, between the carotids, to the tonsil; a third or fourth pouch tract starts in the pyriform fossa and presents as recurrent left-sided neck abscess or thyroiditis. Cure means removing the entire epithelial tract once, without rupturing it, because every infection and every failed operation makes the next one harder.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Excision of branchial cysts, sinuses and fistulae
 | Summary
Indications | Any branchial cyst, sinus or fistula, ideally when free of infection; recurrent infection makes surgery more urgent
Contraindications | Acute infection (treat first; drain only if needed, through a site that can be excised later); an adult "branchial cyst" not yet proven benign
Imaging | Ultrasound for a simple cyst; CT or MRI for first cleft and pouch anomalies; CT after barium swallow or a fistulogram to show a tract
Anaesthesia | General
Position | Supine, shoulder roll, head turned away
Monitoring | Facial nerve monitoring for first cleft type II; recurrent laryngeal nerve monitoring for third and fourth pouch anomalies
Tray | Lacrimal probes, methylene blue, fine catheter, neck set, parotid set for first cleft anomalies, laryngoscopes and cautery electrodes for pyriform fossa openings, drains
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient or family
- **Infection, haematoma or seroma,** and the scar.
- **Recurrence** if any epithelium is left: about 3% for a first operation on an uninfected lesion, around 20% after previous failed surgery, and higher for tracts than for cysts.
- **Nerve injury:** the facial nerve with first cleft anomalies; nerves IX to XII with second to fourth; the recurrent laryngeal nerve with pouch anomalies.
- **A pharyngeal leak** if the pharynx is opened and not closed.
- For pouch anomalies, possible **thyroid lobectomy**.
!!
## Before you start
Ask about recurrent swelling, discharge and infection, especially after colds. Examine for pits and openings: in front of the tragus or under the jaw (first cleft), along the anterior border of sternomastoid (second), and look in the ear canal and the tonsillar and pyriform fossae. In an adult with a cystic lateral neck mass, exclude a cystic metastasis from the oropharynx before calling it branchial.

!table The tracts and their courses
Anomaly | Opening | Course
First cleft type I (ectodermal) | Preauricular or near the ear | Parallel to the canal, lateral to the facial nerve, superficial to the parotid
First cleft type II (ecto- and mesodermal) | Below the jaw angle | Through or close to the parotid and facial nerve to the cartilaginous canal
Second cleft (commonest) | Anterior border of sternomastoid, lower third | Deep to platysma, up along the carotid sheath, between internal and external carotids, over IX and XII, to the tonsillar fossa
Third pouch | Pyriform fossa (base) | Behind the internal carotid, over XII, through the thyrohyoid membrane above the superior laryngeal nerve
Fourth pouch | Pyriform fossa (apex) | Through the cricothyroid region below the superior laryngeal nerve, beside the recurrent laryngeal nerve, often to the left thyroid lobe
!!

# The operation, stage by stage | By arch.
## Define the tract
Before injecting local anaesthetic, cannulate the opening with a lacrimal probe or fine catheter, or inject methylene blue, so the tract can be felt and seen throughout the dissection.
!see What you should see
- A probe or blue staining marking the tract from the skin opening.
!!
## First cleft, type I
Excise an ellipse around the opening, oriented in or near the auricular crease (or add a separate crease incision). Dissect a cuff of tissue around the tract towards the ear canal, staying superficial to the parotid and temporalis fasciae, without entering it. Shave the tragal or canal cartilage and perichondrium where the tract ends.
!mf 5 | Excision of a type I tract from a preauricular pit, and the parotidectomy approach for type II.
!see What you should see
- The tract removed in continuity to its termination at the canal cartilage.
!!
## First cleft, type II
Combine an ellipse around the neck opening with a standard parotidectomy incision. Find the facial nerve trunk first, as for a superficial parotidectomy, then follow the tract in relation to the branches (it may pass deep to, between or superficial to them) up to the cartilaginous canal, excising a piece of canal cartilage if needed.
!see What you should see
- The facial nerve and its branches displayed, with the tract dissected free of them.
!!
!danger The facial nerve in a first cleft anomaly
A type II tract can run straight through the facial nerve. Never follow it blindly from the neck: identify the trunk first and work forwards with the monitor on.
!!
## Second cleft cyst
Use a skin-crease incision over or just below the cyst, incorporating any old drainage scar. Raise subplatysmal flaps, retract sternomastoid and dissect the cyst circumferentially without rupturing it; aspirate a tense cyst to make it easier to handle. Close over a drain.
!see What you should see
- An intact cyst lifted off the carotid sheath, with the hypoglossal nerve and carotids safe.
!!
## Second cleft sinus or fistula
Excise an ellipse around the opening with a cuff of tissue, and follow the tract up along the anterior border of sternomastoid, between the carotids and over the hypoglossal and glossopharyngeal nerves. Use a second, higher "stepladder" incision for a long tract rather than a long vertical scar. Most tracts end blindly; a true fistula reaches the tonsillar fossa, where an ipsilateral tonsillectomy may be needed to close it.
!mf 6 | Stepladder incisions for a long second cleft tract.
!mf 4 | A fistula opening in the tonsillar fossa.
!see What you should see
- The whole tract excised to its pharyngeal end, which is ligated or closed.
!!
## Third and fourth pouch anomalies
Begin with direct laryngoscopy and pharyngoscopy to find the opening in the pyriform fossa (base for third, apex for fourth) and cannulate it. Endoscopic cautery of the opening (diathermy, silver nitrate or trichloroacetic acid) is now the first treatment for many. For open excision, use a lateral neck incision, identify the thyrohyoid and cricothyroid membranes, follow the tract to the pyriform fossa and close the opening (a pharyngoplasty); an ipsilateral thyroid lobectomy is often needed, with the recurrent laryngeal nerve identified and monitored.
!mf 7 | Closing the pyriform sinus opening: hypopharyngeal pharyngoplasty.
!see What you should see
- The tract traced to the pyriform fossa and the opening securely closed, with the recurrent laryngeal nerve intact.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
A child with recurrent left-sided neck abscesses or acute suppurative thyroiditis has a pyriform sinus fistula until proved otherwise. Look in the pyriform fossa at the time of drainage, once the inflammation has settled.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Tract ruptured during dissection
Act | wash out ; excise the surrounding cuff widely
Then | pursue every visible epithelial remnant ; warn of recurrence
!!
!path Pharynx opened during pouch surgery
Act | close in layers ; consider a sternomastoid muscle flap
After | nil by mouth or nasogastric feeding briefly ; watch for a fistula
!!

# Aftercare | Drain, then watch.
## After surgery
Remove the drain when output is low; antibiotics if there was recent infection. Review for recurrence, especially after previous infection or surgery.
## Complications
Infection, haematoma, seroma, recurrence, facial nerve paresis, injury to IX to XII or the recurrent laryngeal nerve, pharyngocutaneous fistula.
!update Current evidence
Branchial anomalies make up about 20% of paediatric congenital neck lesions, and second arch anomalies are by far the commonest; CT fistulography best shows a complete tract when there is a skin opening (Goff et al., *Curr Opin Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2012; Adams et al., *Insights Imaging* 2016). For third and fourth pouch sinuses, a systematic review found success after primary endoscopic cauterisation of 67% to 100%, rising to 78% to 100% after a second cauterisation, comparable to open surgery with less morbidity, supporting cauterisation as the first treatment (Derks et al., *Laryngoscope* 2016).
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Myers: complete excision by arch, superficial parotidectomy for type II first cleft anomalies, stepladder incisions, and endoscopic cauterisation as an initial option for pouch anomalies with cure rates of about 75% to 78%.
Dhingra and K. J. Lee: the embryology and the course of each tract between the arch structures.
The literature: endoscopic cauterisation of pyriform sinus openings is now first-line for third and fourth pouch sinuses.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- The **embryology** and the course of each tract relative to the carotids and nerves.
- **Work** classification of first cleft anomalies and the facial nerve.
- **Imaging** for each anomaly.
- **Recurrent left neck abscess** and the pyriform sinus fistula.
- The **cystic metastasis** that mimics a branchial cyst in adults.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Incising and draining through a site that cannot be excised later.
- Following a first cleft tract without finding the facial nerve.
- Rupturing the cyst or leaving tract epithelium.
- Calling a cystic neck mass in an adult a branchial cyst without excluding cancer.
!!
