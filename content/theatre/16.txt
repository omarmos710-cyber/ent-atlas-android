@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand Total laryngectomy removes the larynx and separates the airway from the food passage for good: the patient breathes through a permanent stoma in the neck and must learn to speak again. Conservation surgery, transoral laser and robotic surgery and organ preservation protocols have narrowed its place, but it remains the operation for most T4 cancers, for cartilage and soft tissue invasion, for salvage after failed non-surgical treatment, and for a larynx destroyed by chondroradionecrosis or by aspiration. Oncologically it is excellent, and quality of life can match chemoradiotherapy, but only if the detail is right: a secure airway at the start, a generous stoma whose cartilage edge is covered, both hypoglossal nerves seen, as much uninvolved pyriform mucosa saved as is safe, a watertight pharyngeal closure, and, if a primary puncture is made, an intact party wall and a constrictor myotomy. This chapter takes you through selection, the apron flap, freeing the larynx from below upward, entering the pharynx, the tracheoesophageal puncture and the closure.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Total laryngectomy
 | Summary
Absolute indications | Most **T4** tumours; invasion of the **thyroid cartilage**; extension into the soft tissues of the neck; **salvage** after failed non-surgical treatment; chondroradionecrosis not responding to conservative care
Relative indications | **T3** cancer (laryngectomy versus chemoradiotherapy is debated); unfit for chemoradiotherapy; life-threatening aspiration from neurological disease; severe COPD ruling out conservation surgery
Contraindications | A tumour suitable for conservation surgery, radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy; comorbidity ruling out general anaesthesia; the patient's refusal
Anaesthesia | General; oral intubation, awake fibreoptic intubation or awake tracheostomy under local, agreed with the anaesthetist according to the tumour
Position | Supine, shoulder roll, arms tucked; bed turned 90 or 180 degrees
Antibiotics | First dose before incision: clindamycin 900 mg with gentamicin 5 mg/kg once, then clindamycin 900 mg 8-hourly for 3 doses (especially in penicillin allergy); or ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g 6-hourly for 4 doses
Tray | Head and neck set with retractors; monopolar and bipolar diathermy; 14 Fr red rubber catheter for a primary puncture; **1% lidocaine** to inject into the carotid bulb adventitia for profound bradycardia
Landmarks | Thyroid notch, cricoid, sternal notch, carotid sheath, oesophagus
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to remove the voice box and the cancer, sometimes with the lymph nodes of the neck.
- **You will breathe through a permanent opening in the neck** (stoma) for the rest of your life; the nose and mouth no longer carry air, so the sense of smell is reduced.
- **Your voice:** the natural voice is lost. You can speak again with a valve in a puncture between windpipe and gullet, an electrolarynx, or oesophageal speech; the speech therapist will meet you before surgery.
- **Swallowing** changes, and narrowing of the gullet can occur.
- **A leak from the throat to the skin** (fistula), especially after radiotherapy (about 30% in salvage surgery), usually healing with tube feeding.
- **Bleeding, infection, chest infections, blood clots.**
- **Damage to nerves** of the tongue (hypoglossal) or to the parathyroid glands, affecting calcium.
- **Further treatment** with radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy may follow.
!!
## Before you start
Laryngeal cancer is commonest in the glottis, then the supraglottis, rarest in the subglottis; it is more frequent in men and mostly squamous. Tobacco is the main risk, with alcohol, HPV and asbestos as others. Hoarseness is the commonest complaint; ask about throat pain, odynophagia, dysphagia, **otalgia** and weight loss, and in a treated patient the return of the original symptoms. Record previous conservation surgery, radiotherapy or organ preservation; COPD, heart disease and previous DVT; the family history; tobacco, alcohol, asbestos and cannabis; antiplatelet drugs and warfarin; allergies.

Listen to the voice and look for breathlessness and stridor. Examine the larynx with a mirror or, better, a flexible scope (useful for teaching and follow-up): note the tumour's site, vocal fold movement, pooling and aspiration. Examine the whole head and neck for a second primary, the neck for nodes and **skin involvement** over the larynx, and the nutritional state.
## Imaging and work-up
Contrast CT of the neck in selected cases shows the **pre-epiglottic space**, thyroid cartilage and nodes; chest radiograph or CT looks for lung metastases, and some use PET-CT for distant disease, the primary and the nodes. Then:
- **Endoscopy under general anaesthesia** to map the tumour, biopsy it and exclude a second primary.
- Review outside slides at your own institution.
- A baseline PET-CT if not already done.
- Medical and radiation oncology review and a **multidisciplinary tumour board**.
- Referral to dental oncology, nutrition, speech therapy, social work, patient advocacy, and spiritual or psychological support if wanted.
!senior See the speech therapist first
Counselling by the speech and language therapist before surgery is part of optimising the speech outcome, not an afterthought.
!!

# Anatomy you must see | Landmarks for the apron and the larynx.
## Landmarks
- **The thyroid notch, cricoid and sternal notch** for the incision and the stoma.
- **The carotid sheath**, retracted laterally with the sternomastoid.
- **The hypoglossal nerves**, which must be seen on both sides above the hyoid.
- **The superior laryngeal neurovascular bundle and superior thyroid artery**, ligated.
- **The pyriform sinus mucosa**, peeled from the inner thyroid cartilage and kept when uninvolved.
- **The party wall**, the common wall between the back of the trachea and the front of the oesophagus, which must not be separated if a puncture is planned.
- **The oesophagus.**

# The operation, stage by stage | Apron, release, entry, closure.
## Secure the airway
Agree the airway plan with the anaesthetist. An obstructing tumour may need a tracheostomy beforehand; otherwise direct laryngoscopy or fibreoptic intubation usually works.
!see What you should see
- A secured airway and the tumour's extent confirmed at laryngoscopy.
!!
## The apron incision
Mark a **U-shaped apron** from the level of the greater horn of the hyoid on each side down along the anterior border of each sternomastoid to about **2 cm above the sternal notch**, taking in any existing tracheostomy. Infiltrating 1% lidocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline is optional. Divide the platysma and raise a **subplatysmal flap** up above the hyoid and down to the clavicles and sternal notch. Do any neck dissection now, before the laryngectomy.
!see What you should see
- The subplatysmal flap up above the hyoid with the anterior jugular veins left on the straps.
!!
## Separate the larynx from the lateral neck
Incise the fascia along the whole anterior border of each sternomastoid and retract the muscle laterally to show the **carotid sheath**. Dissect the tissue in front of and medial to the sheath down to the **prevertebral fascia**, freeing the laryngotracheal complex from the lateral neck along the full length of the muscle. Clamp, tie and divide the omohyoid, the **superior thyroid artery** and the **superior laryngeal neurovascular bundle**.
!mf 1 | The fascia along the whole sternomastoid incised and the muscle retracted laterally.
!mf 2 | The sternomastoid retracted laterally and the straps medially with the laryngotracheal complex: the carotid sheath, hypoglossal nerve, part of the ansa cervicalis and the superior laryngeal nerve.
!see What you should see
- The carotid sheath laterally, the prevertebral fascia behind, and the superior laryngeal pedicle tied.
!!
!danger Bradycardia at the carotid
Handling the carotid can cause profound bradycardia. Stop, and inject 1% lidocaine into the adventitia of the carotid bulb.
!!
## Straps and thyroid
Divide the infrahyoid strap muscles low, at the level of the stoma, and lift them upward to expose the thyroid lobes, and divide the isthmus. To **keep a lobe**, dissect it off the side of the trachea with diathermy; to **remove a lobe** with the specimen, divide the fascia over its capsule and dissect posteriorly and laterally so as to keep the blood supply of the parathyroids.
!mf 3 | The straps divided low and lifted, exposing the thyroid gland.
!see What you should see
- The trachea bare in front below the cricoid, and any preserved lobe pink on its lateral pedicle.
!!
## Free the hyoid
Identify **both hypoglossal nerves**. Divide the suprahyoid muscles off the body of the hyoid until you reach the mucosa of the vallecula, and free the greater horns, steadying the hyoid with a towel clip or Allis clamp.
!mf 4 | The hyoid skeletonised and freed, held in an Allis clamp.
!see What you should see
- Both hypoglossal nerves lying on the hyoglossus close to the greater horns, then a bare hyoid.
!!
!danger The hypoglossal nerves
They lie close to the greater horns of the hyoid. See them on both sides before you free the hyoid.
!!
## Release the constrictors and the pyriform mucosa
Rotate the larynx with double hooks and divide the middle and inferior constrictors off the posterolateral thyroid cartilage. With a Cottle elevator, lift the **pyriform sinus mucosa** off the inside of the cartilage and keep it, unless it is involved by tumour. Divide the thyrohyoid ligament to free the superior cornu.
!see What you should see
- The posterior border of the thyroid cartilage, and pyriform mucosa peeling off its inner surface intact.
!!
!senior Every millimetre of mucosa counts
Removing too much pyriform mucosa narrows the neopharynx and risks stricture or a tight closure.
!!
## Divide the trachea and make the stoma
Mobilise the distal trachea bluntly along its front so it can be fixed to the clavicular heads later. Cut through the trachea **between rings below the tumour**, bevelling its lateral sides with heavy Mayo scissors to widen the stoma. Secure the stoma to the skin, place a reinforced tube in the distal trachea and connect it to the circuit sterilely. Divide the posterior membranous trachea up to the party wall, **without separating the party wall** below, and lift the proximal trachea bluntly to the cricoid.
!mf 5 | After the tracheal incision, dissection in the plane of the party wall up to the back of the cricoid; the party wall and the remaining thyroid lobe are seen.
!see What you should see
- The tracheal lumen below the tumour, a bevelled stump, and the oesophageal muscle of the party wall behind it.
!!
## Enter the pharynx
Move to the head of the table with a headlight. Enter the pharynx through the **vallecula**, the **pyriform sinus** or the **postcricoid** mucosa, according to the tumour and your preference. To enter the vallecula, divide the hyoepiglottic ligament, which leads to the epiglottis, or press a suction tip into the vallecular mucosa and cut onto it. Grasp the tip of the epiglottis with an Allis clamp. Through the pyriform sinus, cut down onto a suction tip in the sinus and open the larynx like a book.
!mf 6 | After entering the vallecula, the epiglottis is grasped and the tumour incisions are made under direct vision.
!see What you should see
- The tip of the epiglottis, then the tumour from above, before you cut round it.
!!
## Resect with a margin
With the tumour in view, cut the mucosa with scissors including a **1 cm margin**, keeping uninvolved pyriform mucosa. Open the specimen, fracturing it through the posterior cricoid, to check the margins, and take more where needed. Irrigate, secure haemostasis, then **change gowns, gloves and instruments** for the reconstruction.
!see What you should see
- The specimen opened with a clear margin all round, and an open pharyngeal defect from base of tongue to oesophageal inlet.
!!
## Nasogastric tube and stoma
Pass an 18 Fr nasogastric tube through the nose and pharynx into the oesophagus, secured with a suture through the columella. Fix the trachea to the **clavicular heads** on both sides with 2-0 Vicryl through its lateral wall 2 to 3 rings below the cut edge, so the stoma does not drop into the chest. Sew the front of the stoma to the skin with **half vertical mattress** sutures of 2-0 Vicryl that cover the cartilage edge. The stoma should be about **3 cm** across.
!see What you should see
- A wide, bevelled stoma with skin covering the cartilage edges and no tension.
!!
!danger Microstoma
A small stoma stenoses. Bevel the trachea, cover the cartilage, and aim for 3 cm.
!!
## A primary tracheoesophageal puncture
1. **Buttress the party wall** to the back of the membranous trachea at its cut edge with 4-0 Vicryl. A separated party wall is an **absolute contraindication** to a primary puncture.
2. **Constrictor myotomy:** with a finger in the pharyngeal defect, cut the middle and inferior constrictors and the upper oesophageal muscle vertically from the oesophageal inlet to the base of tongue down to the mucosa, to prevent spastic speech. Close any accidental pharyngotomy with inverting sutures.
3. **The puncture:** place a right-angled clamp through the defect against the posterior membranous trachea **1 to 1.5 cm below its cut edge**, cut onto its tip with a No. 15 blade, push the tips through, grasp a Foley or red rubber catheter, pull it into the pharynx and direct it down the oesophagus. Fix it with a 2-0 silk stitch; the patient can be fed through it instead of a nasogastric tube. A voice prosthesis can be placed at once from a kit.
A flap reconstruction does not rule out a primary puncture, provided the party wall is intact.
!mf 7 | The front of the stoma secured with half vertical mattress sutures and the constrictor myotomy done; the puncture is made 1 to 1.5 cm below the cut edge of the membranous trachea.
!mf 8 | (A) A No. 15 blade cutting horizontally onto a right-angled clamp pushed through the posterior wall; (B, C) a catheter pulled through the puncture into the pharynx and directed down the oesophagus.
!see What you should see
- The puncture in the midline about 1 to 1.5 cm below the mucocutaneous edge, with the catheter in the oesophagus.
!!
## Close the pharynx
Close the mucosa with a running **inverted** 3-0 Vicryl suture, vertically, horizontally or as a T, depending on preference and on how much mucosa remains. Make any puncture before closing.
!mf 9 | The remaining pharyngeal mucosa closed watertight with inverted edges to prevent a fistula.
!see What you should see
- Mucosal edges turned in along the whole suture line, with enough width left to take your finger.
!!
!senior The editor's view of the closure
The editor, Johan Fagan, advises a capacious, relaxed pharynx: a cricopharyngeal myotomy, **avoiding a vertical closure**, a low threshold for augmenting a narrow pharynx with a pectoralis major flap, a correctly sited puncture, and dividing the sternal heads of the sternomastoids for a flat stoma. The chapter itself leaves the orientation of the closure to the surgeon.
!!
## Test, drain and close
Fill the pharynx from the mouth with half-strength **hydrogen peroxide** in a bulb syringe: bubbles along the suture line mark a leak. Place 10 mm suction drains on each side. Return the upper flap, sew the back of the stoma to the skin with interrupted 2-0 Vicryl, close the platysma and staple the skin. Place a laryngectomy tube if needed to keep the stoma open, and extubate in theatre if stable.
!see What you should see
- No bubbles along the suture line, and flaps lying flat over the drains.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Profound bradycardia
Recognise | heart rate falling as you handle the carotid
Act | stop handling it ; inject 1% lidocaine into the adventitia of the carotid bulb
!!
!path A leak on the peroxide test
See | bubbles along the suture line
Repair | reinforce the closure where it leaks and test again
Consider | flap augmentation if the closure is tight or the tissues irradiated
!!
!path Fistula after surgery
Recognise | saliva in the wound or drain, or a leak on barium swallow at 5 to 7 days
Manage | continue tube feeding and give antibiotics
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Inadequate perioperative antibiotic cover.
- Understaging the cancer.
- Removing too much pyriform sinus mucosa.
- A poor pharyngeal closure, giving a fistula or stenosis.
- Too small a stoma.
- Separating the party wall when making a puncture.
!!

# Aftercare | Stoma, nutrition and voice.
## After the operation
Admit to a step-down or intensive care unit. Antibiotics for 24 hours. Humidified air or oxygen to the stoma by tracheostomy collar, regular tracheal suction and cleaning of crusts; a laryngectomy tube if needed. Antiemetics; analgesia by PCA or scheduled opioid, then oral once feeds and pain allow. Start tube feeds by protocol. Physiotherapy from day 1, but **no neck extension for 10 days**. Teach stoma care to the patient and family from day 1, and start electrolarynx teaching before discharge if indicated. A **barium swallow at 5 to 7 days** looks for a small fistula: if none, start liquids and advance; if one, continue tube feeds and start antibiotics.
## Complications
!table Complications of total laryngectomy
Complication | Notes
Haematoma and seroma | Drains on both sides
Wound infection | Adequate antibiotic cover
Pharyngocutaneous fistula | About 30% after salvage surgery; tube feeds and antibiotics
Dehiscence of stoma or neck wound | Covered cartilage edge, tension-free stoma
Pharyngeal stricture | Too little mucosa, a tight closure
Deep vein thrombosis, chest infection | General risks
Puncture complications | Party wall separation, misplacement
Hypoglossal nerve injury, bradycardia, carotid sheath injury, positive margins, bleeding | Intraoperative risks
!!
## Alternatives
- Primary radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy.
- Palliative care for those who decline treatment.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Does a pectoralis flap prevent fistula after salvage laryngectomy?
Yes. Pharyngocutaneous fistula follows about **30%** of salvage laryngectomies; in a recent study, a **pectoralis major myofascial flap** reduced the risk by **22%** compared with controls, and the fistula rate did not differ between laryngectomy and pharyngolaryngectomy.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Absolute and relative indications; T3 is debated.
- The airway plan, including awake tracheostomy.
- The apron incision and the order of release from below upward.
- Seeing **both hypoglossal nerves**.
- Saving uninvolved pyriform mucosa.
- The stoma: bevelled, 3 cm, cartilage covered with half vertical mattress sutures, fixed to the clavicular heads.
- Primary puncture: intact party wall, 1 to 1.5 cm below the cut edge, **constrictor myotomy** against spasm.
- The watertight inverted closure and the peroxide test.
- Barium swallow at 5 to 7 days; fistula rates and the pectoralis flap.
- Carotid bradycardia and its treatment.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Dividing the trachea without a clear margin below the tumour.
- Forgetting the constrictor myotomy.
- Extending the neck in the first 10 days.
!!
