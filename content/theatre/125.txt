@stand Much of otology is done in the clinic chair with the patient awake: cleaning wax and mastoid cavities, draining a haematoma or abscess of the pinna, taking a biopsy from the canal, injecting gentamicin or steroid through the drum, and inserting ventilation tubes under local anaesthetic. These are the same techniques you use in theatre, done with a conscious patient who feels, hears and sees everything. This chapter gives you the anaesthesia that makes them possible, then each procedure stage by stage, with the judgement about when the clinic is the wrong place.

# Operating note | Choosing the clinic or the theatre.
## The cases at a glance
!table Office procedures in otology
Procedure | Main indications | Anaesthesia | Key risk
Wax and cavity cleaning | Impacted cerumen, mastoid cavity debris | None | Canal trauma, vertigo from suction in a cavity
Auricular haematoma drainage | Haematoma of the pinna after shearing trauma | Regional block of the pinna | Recurrence, cauliflower ear, perichondritis
Abscess drainage | Furuncle, auricular abscess, perichondritis with collection | Local or regional block | Cartilage necrosis and rim deformity
Canal biopsy | Mass in the external canal | Local canal block or none for a polyp | Facial nerve (schwannoma), bleeding (glomus)
Intratympanic gentamicin | Ménière's disease failing conservative treatment | Topical phenol | Hearing loss, imbalance, perforation
Intratympanic steroid | Sudden sensorineural hearing loss; Ménière's disease | Topical phenol | Perforation, pain, transient vertigo
Myringotomy and tube | Effusion, complicated acute otitis media, Eustachian tube dysfunction, hyperbaric therapy | Topical phenol | Bleeding, vascular anomaly mistaken for effusion
!!
## When not to do it in the clinic
Children, anxious or uncooperative adults, narrow canals, and complex or long procedures need sedation or a general anaesthetic. A pulsatile canal or middle ear mass (glomus tumour) must not be biopsied in the clinic. Tell the patient beforehand how long it will take, how much it will hurt, how loud it will be and whether they may feel dizzy; expectations shape satisfaction.
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Wax and cavity care:** noise, brief dizziness with suction, minor bleeding.
- **Haematoma drainage:** recurrence, infection, a lasting change in the shape of the ear, and the need to wear a bolster for a week.
- **Abscess drainage:** the infection may take weeks to settle and may recur; damaged cartilage may leave a notch in the rim.
- **Biopsy:** bleeding, infection, the need for imaging or a formal operation depending on the result.
- **Intratympanic gentamicin:** about 90% control of vertigo; hearing loss in roughly 17% to 30% (lower with low-dose regimens), more tinnitus, imbalance for days or longer, persistent perforation, and the chance that the other ear later develops Ménière's disease.
- **Intratympanic steroid:** pain and a burning sensation, brief dizziness, a small risk of persistent perforation or infection.
!!

# Anaesthesia in the clinic | The surgical site decides the technique.
## The tympanic membrane
The ear must be meticulously clean for any topical agent to work; a thickened drum in acute otitis media may not respond, and a perforated drum lets the agent reach the middle ear, which can cause transient facial palsy or vertigo.
- **Phenol** is the preferred agent: a controlled chemical burn that both anaesthetises and stops bleeding. Apply it sparingly with a phenol applicator (used like a quill pen), a wisp of cotton on a metal applicator or a sponge-tipped kit, to a dry drum: a line for a myringotomy, a dot for an injection. It stings for seconds and the drum **blanches** white at once. Excess causes scarring.
- **Lidocaine 4% to 10%** in saline or **tetracaine 8% to 10%** in alcohol fill the medial canal with the patient supine and head turned away, for about 15 minutes (lidocaine) or 60 minutes (tetracaine); a pledget or wick lets them walk about meanwhile. Remove all the solution and test before you start.
- **EMLA** (lidocaine and prilocaine 2.5% each) is safe on the drum but needs an hour and is not always enough.
!mf 1 | Phenol application. A: a prepackaged sponge-tipped kit. B: cotton on a metal applicator, and a phenol applicator.
## The pinna and canal
- **Topical** EMLA or LET gel (lidocaine 4%, adrenaline 0.1%, tetracaine 0.5%) under an occlusive dressing for at least 30 minutes (LET) or 60 minutes (EMLA), then test with a pinprick.
- **Local infiltration** of 1% lidocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline through a 25-gauge or finer needle on a 1 to 3 ml syringe; buffering with bicarbonate (1 part to 10) reduces the burning. Inject slowly through as few punctures as possible. Maximum dose 7 mg/kg with adrenaline (to 500 mg), 4.5 mg/kg without; onset 2 minutes, duration 4 to 6 hours.
## The nerves of the ear
The **great auricular nerve** (C2 and C3) supplies the lower pinna and the skin over the mastoid; the **lesser occipital nerve** the middle of the back of the pinna; the **auriculotemporal nerve** (mandibular division of the trigeminal) the upper pinna, tragus and front of the canal; the **auricular branch of the vagus** (Arnold's nerve) the concha and back of the canal; with contributions from the facial nerve.
!mf 2 | The sensory supply of the auricle: auriculotemporal, great auricular and lesser occipital nerves and the auricular branch of the vagus.
## The regional blocks
- **Outer ear (ring) block** for the whole pinna, through two entry points at the upper and lower attachment of the pinna to the head. With a 10 ml syringe of 1% lidocaine with adrenaline and a 25-gauge needle, advance subcutaneously, aspirate, inject 2 to 3 ml as you withdraw, redirect behind the pinna without leaving the skin and inject another 2 to 3 ml; repeat from the lower point. It does not numb the canal or drum.
- **Four-quadrant canal block** for the canal and drum but not the pinna: a 27-gauge needle on a 1 to 3 ml syringe enters at the **bony-cartilaginous junction** in four quadrants and advances a few millimetres along the bone; slight pressure with the speculum lateral to the site pushes the anaesthetic medially. Watch the medial canal blanch and stop before a **bleb** forms, which narrows the canal and can split the skin.
!mf 3 | Regional block of the pinna and external ear from two entry points above and below the attachment of the pinna.

# Cleaning wax and cavities | The commonest otological procedure.
## Wax
Wax is the commonest cause of conductive hearing loss in adults. The canal cleans itself by outward migration of epithelium from the drum; narrow canals, hearing aids, cotton buds and previous radiotherapy interrupt this. **Do not syringe** an ear with a perforation, a retracted or atrophic drum, or a history of discharge or ear surgery. Under the microscope, a well-shaped plug in a wide canal can be lifted with Hartmann or small crocodile forceps. In a narrow or occluded canal, find the **interface between wax and canal wall** with a small right-angled pick and follow the contour of the canal medially behind the plug. Choose the instrument by consistency: suction (5 French as a rule, 3 French near the drum, 7 French only to open a fully blocked canal and then step down to reduce noise), picks, forceps or curettes. Docusate liquid in the canal for 15 minutes softens hard wax.
## Mastoid cavities
Read the previous operation note first: the state of the drum and middle ear, the lateral semicircular canal, the facial nerve and the tegmen. Remove all squamous and ceruminous debris. Moisture beneath it can be dusted with **boric acid powder** to prevent infection. Suction in a cavity often causes vertigo by cooling or moving the lateral canal; mop with cotton on crocodile forceps or a metal applicator instead, and use the finest sucker you can.

# The operation: draining an auricular haematoma | Restore the blood supply to the cartilage.
## Understand the injury and block the ear
A shearing force (wrestling, boxing, rugby) lifts the skin and perichondrium off the cartilage and blood collects between them. Cartilage depends on the perichondrium for its blood supply, so it starts to die; fibrosis, contracture and new cartilage then produce the **cauliflower ear**, and a thickened concha can even close the canal. Treat early. Ask how and when it happened, and whether the patient is an athlete who will return to contact sport at once, in which case a bulky dressing and needle aspiration are poor choices. Prepare the pinna and give an **outer ear block**.
!see What you should see
- A tense, fluctuant swelling on the front of the pinna, obliterating the normal folds.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Incision and drainage recurs less often than needle aspiration and deals with loculated and intracartilaginous collections. Reserve aspiration for small, fresh haematomas in non-athletes.
!!
## Incise and evacuate
Make the incision along the **helical or antihelical fold**, where the scar hides. Express the blood and serum by gentle digital compression. Pass a fine haemostat into the cavity to break down loculations so that every pocket drains into the main one. A small sterile rubber band or Penrose drain may be left in.
!see What you should see
- The pinna's folds returning as the collection empties, and no residual fluctuant area.
!!
!danger Stripping the perichondrium
Do not peel the perichondrium further off the cartilage than the haematoma already has: every millimetre lifted is cartilage without blood supply.
!!
## Bolster
Success depends on the compression. Cut a dental roll to the size of the haematoma for the front of the pinna and a similar or slightly smaller roll for the back, with Silastic sheeting (1 mm) for support if wished. Pass a 3-0 nylon or silk suture on a Keith needle through the front roll and the pinna from lateral to medial, through the back roll and back through it, then from medial to lateral through the pinna and the front roll, and tie it as a **through-and-through mattress suture**. Coat the rolls and incision with antibiotic ointment.
!mf 4 | A Penrose drain and Silastic bolsters held with a through-and-through mattress suture after drainage of an auricular haematoma.
!see What you should see
- The bolster pressing the skin flat against the cartilage over the whole area of the haematoma, without blanching the skin edges.
!!
## Aftercare
Oral antibiotics while the bolster is in; remove it at **5 to 7 days**. A bolster allows an immediate return to contact sport with a low recurrence rate. Watch for recurrence and infection; a recurrent haematoma may need open exploration.
!see What you should see
- At removal of the bolster: skin lying flat on the cartilage with the folds of the pinna visible and no fluctuance.
!!

# The operation: intratympanic injection | Medicine delivered through the drum.
## Select and prepare the patient
**Gentamicin** is an ablative treatment for vertigo in **Ménière's disease** when diet and diuretics have failed. Confirm the diagnosis and exclude vestibular migraine, benign positional vertigo and superior canal dehiscence by history, examination, audiometry, vestibular testing and imaging. Discuss the non-ablative and surgical alternatives (betahistine, pressure devices, endolymphatic sac surgery, labyrinthectomy, vestibular nerve section). Do not use gentamicin in an only hearing ear, and test the other ear's vestibular function first: a weak opposite labyrinth is a contraindication. Take care in the elderly, the visually impaired and those with peripheral neuropathy, who compensate poorly; start vestibular physiotherapy before if possible. Gentamicin is chosen because it is more vestibulotoxic than cochleotoxic; it controls vertigo in about 90%.
**Dexamethasone** is used for **sudden sensorineural hearing loss**, as salvage after or with oral prednisolone, or as primary treatment when systemic steroids are unsafe; oral and intratympanic steroids give similar results in trials. Order a gadolinium MRI to exclude a vestibular schwannoma, but do not delay the first injection for it. Perform pure-tone audiometry **before every injection**.
!see What you should see
- A current audiogram and, for gentamicin, a documented normal opposite labyrinth.
!!
## Anaesthetise and set up
Lay the patient supine with the head turned **45 degrees away**, the treated ear up. Apply phenol to two small spots on the drum, **anterosuperior and posteroinferior**. Draw up **0.6 ml** in a tuberculin syringe with a 1.5-inch 25-gauge needle (a full syringe puts the plunger out of reach of your thumb) and bend the needle slightly so that it does not block your view down the speculum.
!mf 5 6 | Intratympanic injection. Left: the head turned 45 degrees away from the treated ear, held for 25 minutes. Right: a slight bend in the needle keeps the drum in view.
!see What you should see
- Two white phenol spots on the drum and a bent needle that leaves the drum in view.
!!
!danger The posterosuperior quadrant
The injection sites avoid the ossicles; never inject through the posterosuperior quadrant.
!!
## Inject
Make a puncture in the **anterosuperior** drum first: it lets air escape from the middle ear during the injection. Then, through the **posteroinferior** puncture, aimed toward the round window niche, inject slowly, watching the **air-fluid level rise** behind the drum up to the anterosuperior vent; about 0.4 ml fills the middle ear. Stop when a little fluid wells into the canal. Gentamicin is buffered with bicarbonate to about 30 mg/ml; dexamethasone is 10 to 24 mg/ml (methylprednisolone hurts more).
!see What you should see
- A rising fluid level reaching the anterosuperior puncture, and a little efflux into the canal.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
The round window is the gate to the inner ear. A mucosal fold or false membrane over the niche blocks absorption, which is one reason injections sometimes fail.
!!
## Keep the drug in the ear
The patient stays supine with the head turned for **25 minutes**, spitting saliva into a basin rather than swallowing, which would open the Eustachian tube and drain the drug away.
!see What you should see
- A fluid level still visible behind the drum at the end of the 25 minutes.
!!
## Aftercare
Keep the ear dry. **Gentamicin:** review at 4 weeks; repeat the injection if vertigo persists; audiometry before each injection and 2 months after the last; continue diet and diuretics. **Dexamethasone:** review weekly with audiometry and repeat weekly for three weeks, or until hearing returns to baseline or plateaus. The punctures usually close within weeks.
!see What you should see
- At review: closed punctures, a current audiogram and, after gentamicin, no further vertigo spells.
!!

# Other office procedures | Abscess, biopsy and tubes.
## Draining an abscess of the ear
A furuncle may settle with warm compresses and expression, but any acute, painful fluctuant swelling needs drainage. Early infection without a collection may respond to high-dose antibiotics with close review. Give antibiotic cover to the immunocompromised, diabetic, alcoholic, and those on chemotherapy or steroids. Plan incisions so they do not hinder later reconstruction when cartilage is likely to be lost. Infiltrate lateral to a furuncle and open it with a myringotomy knife in the canal or a size 11 blade on the pinna; for a larger auricular abscess, use a regional block, incise with a size 15 blade along the helical or antihelical fold, send pus for culture, break down every loculation, **debride necrotic cartilage** (staged debridement keeps the rim deformity small; extensive necrosis is better handled in theatre), irrigate with saline and leave the wound open, with a drain if needed. Auricular cartilage is relatively avascular, so systemic antibiotics reach it poorly. Warn that an auricular abscess can take weeks to clear and may recur; redrain at once if it does, and refer for rim reconstruction if a notch results.
## Biopsy of a canal mass
Masses in the canal may be benign (osteoma, exostosis, keratosis obturans, cholesteatoma, polyp, neurofibroma, paraganglioma or schwannoma, fibrous dysplasia, eosinophilic granuloma, temporomandibular joint herniation, vascular lesions) or malignant (squamous and basal cell carcinoma, ceruminous gland tumours, adenoid cystic carcinoma, melanoma, sarcoma, metastases). Osteomas and exostoses are diagnosed by their appearance and gentle palpation. A recently painful, discharging lesion should first have antibiotic-steroid drops. A polyp usually arises from a perforation or a cholesteatoma; but **about 13% of facial nerve schwannomas present as a canal mass**, so if a polyp arises from the posterior canal wall or its origin cannot be seen, obtain a CT before removing it. In a diabetic patient, granulation tissue at the bony-cartilaginous junction suggests **necrotising otitis externa**, but biopsy it to exclude carcinoma. A **pulsatile mass** (glomus tumour) must not be biopsied in the clinic. Have specula, a 5 French sucker, fine cup forceps, Bellucci scissors, 1:10,000 adrenaline or silver nitrate, a specimen pot and antibiotic ointment ready. Use antibiotic-steroid drops afterwards and review in a week with the histology.
## Myringotomy and tube under local anaesthetic
Most adults and many older children tolerate a tube in the clinic. Examine with the microscope and pneumatic otoscopy, confirm the conductive loss with tuning forks and an audiogram, and **examine the nasopharynx in any adult with a unilateral effusion**. Exclude an aberrant carotid, dehiscent jugular bulb or glomus tumour, which can look like fluid. Use lower magnification for easier handling; apply phenol to the site and make a radial myringotomy **anteroinferiorly or posteroinferiorly**, avoiding the posterosuperior quadrant (an overhanging anterior wall can make the anterosuperior quadrant hard to reach). Grasp a bobbin tube by its lateral flange, seat part of the medial flange in the incision and tease the rest through with a fine pick; a stepped insertion puts less tension on the drum and hurts less. Small tubes suit short-term use, mid-sized tubes chronic effusion and larger lumens mucoid effusions; a T-tube may need sedation. A myringotomy alone closes in days, so it does not treat chronic effusion. Serous effusions need no drops; bleeding, infection or thick mucus warrant antibiotic drops with or without steroid for 5 to 7 days. Review at a month, then six-monthly until the tube extrudes; much more closely after a complicated acute otitis media.

# When it goes wrong | Drills in the clinic.
## Crisis drills
!path Vertigo during cavity cleaning
Stop suctioning | the lateral canal is being cooled or moved
Settle | lie the patient flat, reassure, wait for it to pass
Change technique | mop with cotton ; the smallest sucker ; consider a fistula if vertigo follows pressure
!!
!path The patient faints in the chair
Recognise | vasovagal reaction, often from Arnold's nerve or pain
Act | lie flat, legs raised, airway and pulse
Next time | lie them down from the start ; better anaesthesia ; consider theatre
!!
!path Brisk bleeding from a canal biopsy
Pressure | pledget with 1:10,000 adrenaline ; silver nitrate
Reassess | was the mass pulsatile? suspect a paraganglioma or vascular anomaly
Escalate | pack the canal, imaging, senior review
!!

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: treats auricular haematoma by aspiration under strict asepsis with a pressure dressing that packs every concavity, repeated if needed, and incision with dental-roll bolsters tied by through-and-through sutures when aspiration fails, with antibiotics for all. For wax it describes syringing with water at body temperature (too hot or cold causes caloric vertigo), instrument removal with a hook or Jobson-Horne probe passed beyond the plug, and softening drops (sodium bicarbonate in glycerine and water, hydrogen peroxide, oil).
Lee: gives intratympanic gentamicin as a titrated, low-dose ablation and intratympanic steroid as salvage for sudden hearing loss.
Myers prefers incision and drainage over aspiration for haematoma, adds the regional nerve blocks in detail, and warns about the facial nerve schwannoma presenting as a canal polyp.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Phenol** on the drum: why it is preferred and why the drum must be dry.
- The **nerve supply of the pinna** and the two regional blocks; maximum lidocaine doses.
- **Auricular haematoma:** why it must be drained (perichondrial blood supply), and the bolster.
- **Intratympanic gentamicin:** indications, contraindications (only hearing ear, weak opposite labyrinth), outcomes (about 90% vertigo control, 17% to 30% hearing loss).
- Why the patient lies still and does not swallow.
- The canal polyp that may be a facial nerve schwannoma, and the pulsatile mass not to biopsy.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Syringing an ear with a perforation or a cavity.
- Needle aspiration of a haematoma with no compression, in an athlete going straight back to the mat.
- A bleb from the canal block that splits the canal skin.
- Injecting gentamicin without checking the opposite labyrinth, or into an only hearing ear.
- Removing a posterior-wall polyp without imaging.
- Biopsying a pulsatile mass in the clinic.
!!
