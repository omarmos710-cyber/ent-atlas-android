@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand Sialendoscopy brings a telescope of about a millimetre into the ducts of the major salivary glands, to remove stones, open strictures and wash out inflamed ducts, and so to keep glands that would once have been removed. It needs more patience than force. The papilla is the first and hardest step, especially in the submandibular gland, and once inside, the scope must stay in the centre of the lumen and never be pushed blind. Some stones are too large or too far back to come out through the duct, and then the scope guides a hybrid operation: a transoral sialolithotomy for the submandibular gland, a transfacial or transbuccal approach for the parotid. This chapter covers selection, imaging, dilating the papilla, the endoscopy of each gland, stones and stenosis, the hybrid approaches, and how to avoid the trapped basket and the perforated duct.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Salivary endoscopy
 | Summary
Indications | Obstruction by **calculus or stenosis**, compatible on history and imaging; inflammatory disease (Sjögren's syndrome, juvenile recurrent parotitis) frequent or severe enough to warrant intervention; sialadenitis after **radioactive iodine**
Contraindications | **Acute infection** (relative): it may worsen the infection or the duct may perforate. Comorbidity is not a contraindication, because most cases can be done under local anaesthesia or sedation
Anaesthesia | General is often preferred for comfort; nasal intubation can improve access for hybrid and bilateral cases; sedation or local anaesthesia (including in the office) are possible
Position | Supine, as for sinus surgery, head turned slightly toward the surgeon
Antibiotics | Not routine; recommended after recent infection or for combined approaches through mucosa: a first-generation cephalosporin for transfacial parotid work, co-amoxiclav for transoral hybrids, clindamycin in penicillin allergy
Monitoring | Facial nerve monitoring for parotid hybrid approaches is used by some but has no evidence of benefit
Tray | Camera and light tower; salivary set with mouth props, dilators of several types, fine scissors and forceps; sweetheart, malleable and self-retaining retractors; 20 mL syringe of saline with IV tubing for irrigation; scopes of 1.1, 1.3 and 1.6 mm; disposable baskets, balloons, dilators and stents; triamcinolone (Kenalog); local anaesthetic without adrenaline as irrigant under local
Scopes | Diagnostic 0.8 mm; therapeutic 1.1 to 1.6 mm, with rinsing and working channels
Skills | Endoscopy, basic open head and neck surgery, and patience
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to remove a stone, widen a narrowing or wash out the duct, and to keep the gland.
- **It may not work:** the stone may not come out or the problem may persist; removal of the gland may be needed later.
- **A cut inside the mouth or on the face** may be needed for a large stone.
- **A tear in the duct,** treated with antibiotics or a small tube (stent).
- **Swelling** of the gland, cheek or floor of mouth from the rinsing fluid, usually settling; rarely it can affect breathing.
- **Narrowing of the duct** afterwards.
- **Infection.**
- **Numbness of the tongue** (lingual nerve) after removal of a submandibular stone through the mouth, or **facial weakness** after a parotid approach through the cheek or face.
- **A broken basket or one that sticks on the stone**, needing an open operation.
!!
## Before you start
Patients usually complain of swelling and discomfort of the gland at mealtimes; inflammatory disease gives more constant symptoms. Ask about the duration and age at onset, swelling with meals, previous stones, dry eyes or mouth, radioactive iodine, previous salivary surgery, other illness, the family history and anticoagulants. Look for practical obstacles: the teeth, tori and piercings.

Palpate all the major glands. Examine the mouth, under the microscope if possible: note the **size and position of the submandibular papilla**, the quality and quantity of saliva you can express, and feel for stones along Wharton's duct and near the parotid papilla. Treat an acute infection with antibiotics first.
## Imaging
- **Ultrasound** is the first choice outside the United States and increasingly within it: it detects stones down to **2 mm**, shows ductal dilatation from any obstruction, and is dynamic, done with food stimulation or intraoral palpation. The anterior part of Wharton's duct is the hardest area to see.
- **Non-contrast CT** finds stones over **1 mm** but is little help for stenosis unless the duct is greatly dilated; add contrast if a tumour is in the differential.
- **Conventional sialography** is invasive and operator-dependent but helps in puzzling obstructions. **MR sialography** uses saliva as contrast; it is expensive, available in few centres and needs extra set-up.

# Anatomy you must see | The papillae and their neighbours.
## Landmarks
- **The submandibular papilla** lies behind the lower incisors, but its exact position varies: note it in clinic while stimulating the gland with food.
- **The parotid papilla** lies in the buccal mucosa opposite the **second upper molar**.
- **Stensen's duct** runs close to the **buccal branch of the facial nerve**, and curves around the **masseter**, the hardest part of the parotid endoscopy.
- **Hilar submandibular stones** lie close to the **lingual nerve**, which often has to be moved laterally in a hybrid approach.
!senior Two glands, two problems
The submandibular duct is hard to enter at its papilla but easy to navigate once inside. The parotid papilla dilates easily, but the duct bends around the masseter; straightening the duct helps you past it.
!!

# The operation, stage by stage | Dilate, inspect, treat.
## Choose the setting and find the papilla
Choose local anaesthesia, sedation or general anaesthesia according to the patient, the disease and comorbidity. View the papilla under magnification. Saliva milked from the gland marks the opening, but there is little of it, so use it sparingly.
!danger A false opening
Toothed forceps, injection needles, dilators or guidewires can make a dimple or puncture that looks just like the papilla. Use atraumatic instruments.
!!
## Dilate atraumatically
Dilate the papilla gently, either with rigid dilators of increasing size or by the **Seldinger technique** over a guidewire. This is the rate-limiting step of the whole procedure. In the parotid, metal dilators are used, and the papilla is usually easier to find.
## Prepare the scope
Choose the scope by the expected disease and the instruments that must pass through its channel. Outside the patient, check the optics, focus and white balance, and **decide which way is up** while you hold it in your hand. Flush both ports with saline to clear air bubbles.
## Enter and navigate
Insert the scope **without force**. Stay in the **centre of the lumen**, irrigating to keep it open; if you lose the lumen you have no real view. Never push without seeing. Remember that Stensen's duct is smaller.
!danger Perforation
Pushing without a view perforates the duct. Once it has perforated, stop irrigating: fluid escapes into the floor of the mouth or the cheek.
!!
!danger Too much irrigation
Over-irrigation balloons the gland, can injure the duct, and leaks into the floor of the mouth or the buccal space, swelling the floor of the mouth or the cheek; in the worst case it threatens the airway.
!!
## Submandibular stones
Choose a basket to suit the stone: **more wires for small stones, fewer for large**. Floating stones can usually be retrieved, and most need a **papillotomy** to come out. Stones larger than **4 mm** in their smallest dimension and not near the papilla need a hybrid approach or fragmentation first.
!danger Trapped basket syndrome
A basket closed on a stone that is not floating may grasp it but not let it go, and the basket is then stuck. Basket only stones that move; a fixed stone needs fragmentation or a hybrid approach.
!!
## Parotid stones
Stones in the ducts **proximal to the masseter** have a low endoscopic success rate. Non-floating stones over **3 mm** may need fragmentation or a hybrid approach.
## Stenosis
Stenosis is **commonest in the parotid ducts**. It may be a single diaphragm, the ideal case, or multilevel and severe. Dilate it with a balloon or with scopes in sheaths, then stent the duct.
!mf 1 | Parotid duct stenosis before and after dilatation, and a salivary duct stent with a guidewire through its lumen to ease introduction into the papilla.
!senior Do not break the scope
Scopes are fragile. Excess force, or bending the scope over the teeth, breaks it.
!!

# The operation: hybrid approaches | When the duct alone is not enough.
## Submandibular: transoral sialolithotomy
A stone at the papilla can be removed in the office. A medium-sized stone in the middle of the duct or the hilum can be removed by transoral sialolithotomy or fragmented and extracted endoscopically. A large stone in the mid-duct or hilum is removed by **transoral sialolithotomy**, seeing and protecting the **lingual nerve**. Afterwards, endoscope the duct to confirm that no fragment is left: residual fragments cause recurrence. The duct need not be repaired; close the mucosa loosely over the incision.
!danger The lingual nerve
At the hilum the lingual nerve lies close to the stone and usually has to be moved laterally. See it before you incise.
!!
## Parotid: the transfacial approach
Use a mini face-lift incision with standard parotid exposure, protecting the great auricular nerve. The stone must be found exactly: marked with methylene blue under ultrasound, easily palpable, lit by a scope left in the duct, or best of all **trapped in a basket** so it can be moved within the duct. Raise an anteriorly based U-shaped SMAS flap over it. If the buccal branch of the facial nerve is seen, free it from the point of entry. Open the duct over the stone with a scalpel and enlarge the opening with middle ear scissors only as much as needed. Close the duct **watertight**, and check it by running water at low pressure through the scope; the SMAS flap is the second layer. Vein grafts have been described for stenosis that will not dilate.
## Parotid: the transbuccal approach
Make a curved incision in front of the parotid papilla and carry it through the buccinator to expose Stensen's duct in the buccal space. Stenosis or stones **distal to the anterior border of the masseter** can then be treated by sialodochotomy and release of the stone, or by dilating the stenosis under direct vision. Endoscope through the natural opening past the treated segment into the proximal duct, place a guidewire and stent, and repair any duct incision over the stent. Consider **botulinum toxin** injected into the gland to prevent a sialocele. The same mid-buccal opening also allows endoscopy into the hilum. For a more distal stenosis, complete the incision around the duct opening, dissect the duct from the buccal space and draw it into the mouth (duct stretching), excise the distal 1 to 2 cm and suture the proximal duct to the buccal mucosa: a **sialodochoplasty**.

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Duct perforated
Stop | stop irrigating at once
Treat | antibiotics ; occasionally a stent
Later | watch for infection and stenosis
!!
!path Basket stuck on the stone
Recognise | a fixed stone grasped and the basket will not release
Free it | open hybrid procedure to retrieve the stone and basket
Prevent | basket only floating stones of a suitable size
!!
!path Duct avulsed by traction on a basket
Recognise | the duct comes out with the basket and stone
Treat | in most cases, removal of the gland
Prevent | never pull hard on an impacted stone
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Failing to dilate the papilla.
- Making a false opening with needles, dilators or guidewires.
- Losing the centre of the lumen.
- Pushing without a view and perforating the duct; irrigating on afterwards.
- Basketing a fixed stone.
- Breaking the scope with force or by bending it over the teeth.
- Over-irrigating, swelling the gland, floor of mouth or cheek.
!!

# Aftercare | Home the same day.
## After the procedure
Done in the clinic under local anaesthesia or in theatre, and the patient goes home the same day. Give antibiotics if infection was seen during the endoscopy.
## Complications
!table Complications of salivary endoscopy
Complication | Notes
Failure | Stone not retrieved or disease not controlled; gland excision may follow
Duct perforation | Antibiotics, occasionally a stent
Infection | From postoperative swelling of the duct and papilla
Blocked basket | A non-floating stone grasped but not released; open retrieval
Duct avulsion | From pulling hard on a basket and stone; usually needs gland removal
Stenosis | From instrumentation, laser heat or hybrid incisions
Airway swelling | From irrigation fluid in the floor of the mouth
Nerve injury | Lingual nerve after submandibular hybrids, buccal branch of the facial nerve after parotid hybrids; rare. No nerve or vessel risk in purely endoscopic work
Broken scope | Fragile instruments
!!
## Alternatives
- Observation.
- Excision of the gland, for patients whom endoscopy has not helped.
- Ultrasound-guided **botulinum toxin** into the parotid to temporise sialadenitis in a patient who does not want parotidectomy.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Does the gland work afterwards?
Large and hilar submandibular stones were once thought to rule out keeping the gland. After transoral removal of hilar submandibular stones, scintigraphy showed **stable or increased salivary flow in up to 66%** of glands, objective support for gland-preserving surgery. Flow is not the only measure: relief of obstruction and pain, and freedom from recurrent stones or stenosis, matter as much, and recurrence is what usually leads to salvage excision.
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Indications: stones, stenosis, Sjögren's, juvenile recurrent parotitis, radioiodine sialadenitis.
- Acute infection as a relative contraindication.
- Ultrasound as first-line imaging; its limits at the front of Wharton's duct.
- The papillae: behind the lower incisors, and opposite the second upper molar.
- **Size limits**: submandibular stones over 4 mm and parotid stones over 3 mm (not floating) need fragmentation or a hybrid.
- Stenosis is **commonest in the parotid**.
- The trapped basket and how to avoid it.
- The nerves at risk in hybrid approaches.
!!
!note The book's own numbers
In this chapter's text the size threshold is 4 mm for submandibular stones and 3 mm for non-floating parotid stones; one of its review questions instead pairs the parotid duct with stones larger than 4 mm. The thresholds in the text are the ones used here.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Forcing the scope or pushing blind.
- Irrigating through a perforation.
- Pulling hard on an impacted stone.
- Leaving fragments behind after a sialolithotomy.
- Forgetting the lingual nerve at the hilum.
!!
