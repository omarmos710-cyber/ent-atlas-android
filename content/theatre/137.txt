@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand A cochlear implant restores the perception of sound by stimulating the auditory nerve directly from inside the cochlea. It has transformed two groups above all: children born deaf or deafened early, and adults who lost their hearing after learning language and no longer gain from hearing aids. Candidacy has widened from profound deafness to people with useful low-frequency hearing and severe loss above it. The operation is an elegant extension of the cortical mastoidectomy: through the facial recess, between the facial nerve and the chorda tympani, to the round window, which lies just below the incudostapedial joint over the scala tympani of the basal turn. The electrode goes in slowly, contacts facing the modiolus, to spare what hearing and structure remain; the receiver-stimulator sits in a well at least 2 cm behind the mastoid so the external processor has room. This chapter covers candidacy, imaging, the facial recess and round window, insertion, fixation and the complications.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Cochlear implantation in adults
 | Summary
Adults (postlingual) | Bilateral moderate to profound sensorineural loss no longer helped by aids; open-set sentence scores under 60% best-aided, under 50% in the better ear (non-Medicare criteria)
Hybrid implant | Normal to moderate low-frequency hearing with severe to profound mid and high frequency loss; CNC word score 10 to 60% in the ear to be implanted (aided), the other ear equal or better but not above 80%
Children | Prelingual deafness; 1 to 2 years: PTA over 90 dB bilaterally; over 2 years: a bilateral threshold printed incompletely in the chapter (see the note at the end), or failure to progress with aids
Before | A suitable hearing aid trial if not already aided
Contraindications | **Cochlear nerve aplasia**; **cochlear aplasia**; **active middle ear infection**
Preparation | Chlorhexidine wash in the 24 hours before; **pneumococcal vaccination** (Pneumovax 23 and Prevnar 13); stop anticoagulants
Anaesthesia and position | General; supine, head turned away
Antibiotics | First-generation cephalosporin
Monitoring | Facial nerve monitor
Tray | Microscope; mastoid set; implant insertion instruments; smooth jeweller's forceps; 5910 Beaver blade for the round window
Skills | Complete mastoidectomy; fine motor control for insertion
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to place a device that lets you hear by stimulating the hearing nerve directly; hearing will be different from natural hearing and improves with programming and practice.
- **Remaining hearing** in the implanted ear may be lost.
- **Taste change** on that side (chorda tympani), usually resolving over months.
- **Facial weakness:** rare.
- **Infection** around the device, a collection of blood, and rarely removal of the device.
- **Meningitis:** slightly more likely than in the general population; vaccination lowers the risk.
- **A leak of brain fluid** if the bone above the mastoid is thin.
!!
## Before you start
Ask when the loss began and how it progressed (gradual, rapid or sudden), its cause (hereditary, noise, ototoxic drugs, **meningitis**), which ear is better, how long each ear has been aided and when aids were abandoned, lip-reading ability, difficult environments and social isolation. Record sudden loss, chronic ear disease, infections, radiotherapy; fitness for **3 hours** of general anaesthesia; previous implants, stapes or chronic ear surgery or craniotomy; anticoagulants and NSAIDs; and opioid-paracetamol misuse.

Assess communication in clinic, eye contact and syndromic features. Examine the postauricular skin and scars, the canal and drum (intact and dry), nystagmus and gait, facial nerve function and tuning forks.
## Imaging
- **CT** of the temporal bones: cochlear morphology and **ossification after meningitis**, an **enlarged vestibular aqueduct**, and the facial nerve, sigmoid sinus and tegmen.
- **MRI** when cochlear nerve aplasia is suspected (the nerve in the internal auditory canal), and for cochlear patency.

# Anatomy you must see | From the mastoid to the round window.
## Landmarks
- **The facial recess:** the posterior tympanotomy between the **facial nerve** and the **chorda tympani**.
- **The stapedius tendon**, often the first thing seen in the middle ear, runs from the pyramidal process to the stapes in the oval window.
- **The round window niche**, directly **below the oval window**, about **2 mm inferior to the incudostapedial joint**, over the **scala tympani** of the basal turn.
- **The cochleostomy site**, anteroinferior to the round window.

# The operation, stage by stage | Mastoid, recess, window, well, array.
## Incision and flap
Confirm the side at the timeout. Mark the incision **1 cm behind the postauricular crease**, from above the mastoid tip to the superior temporal line, and inject 1% lidocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline. Go down to periosteum, undercutting the edges toward the mastoid tip and zygomatic root. Raise an **anteriorly based periosteal flap** to the spine of Henle, identifying the posterior canal wall without lifting the soft tissue along the canal.
!mf 1 | The incision for a right cochlear implant.
## Mastoidectomy and facial recess
Perform a complete mastoidectomy, thin the posterior canal wall, and open the **facial recess** wide enough to see the incudostapedial joint and the round window niche below it.
!danger The facial nerve and the drill shaft
Heat from the drill shaft while opening the recess can injure the facial nerve, as can direct contact with its mastoid segment. Irrigate, and keep the shaft off the nerve.
!!
## Open the round window
Drill away the **bony lip above the round window** and remove any **fibrous tissue** covering the membrane until it is clearly seen. Alternatively, make a **cochleostomy** anteroinferior to the round window, thinning the bone to eggshell and removing the last layer with a pick or rasp to spare the inner ear. A lubricant such as sodium hyaluronate can be placed over the opening.
!mf 3 | Magnified view of the right facial recess: the incudostapedial joint (white), the round window opened (black), the cochleostomy site (asterisk).
!danger The wrong hole
The array can be pushed into a **hypotympanic air cell** and miss the cochlea entirely. Prevent it with enough exposure through the recess, removal of the round window lip and clearance of fibrous tissue.
!!
## The receiver-stimulator well
Raise a **subperiosteal pocket** posterosuperior to the mastoidectomy, checking its size with the mock-up; the device usually lies **30 to 45 degrees** up from the temporal line. Drill the well **at least 2 cm behind the mastoidectomy** so the device does not abut the external processor, and a channel from the well to the mastoid for the array. Check the fit with the silicone mock-up. Irrigate away bone dust and **take the monopolar off the field**.
## Insert the electrode
Place the device in the pocket and well, with the self-retaining retractor already in place to avoid damaging it. Under the microscope, orient the contacts **upward, toward the modiolus**. Grasp the array just proximal to the contacts with smooth forceps and insert it **slowly**. Technique depends on the design: with the **advance-off-stylet** technique, insert partway with the stylet, then advance the array off the stationary stylet, and withdraw the stylet once fully in. If insertion is difficult, rotate gently or back out slightly and readvance. Insert to the manufacturer's marker rings.
!mf 4 | Insertion in a right ear: the array at the round window with its contacts facing up toward the modiolus (white); the incus (green).
## Seal and close
Pack a small piece of fascia or periosteum around the array at the opening to seal it, and another in the facial recess. Coil any excess array in the mastoid so it does not pull out. Place the ground electrode, if present, under temporalis. Close the periosteum over the array and the soft tissue in three layers; apply a mastoid dressing.

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Facial palsy on waking
Think | heat from the drill shaft at the recess, or direct injury to the mastoid segment
Concern for direct injury | return to theatre and explore
Delayed or partial | conservative management
!!
!path Wound infection
Signs | pus, swelling or redness over the device
Act | antibiotics promptly ; if not rapidly better, a limited incision and drainage, often without removing the device
!!
!path Swelling or bleeding from the wound
Act | a haematoma goes back to theatre for evacuation and haemostasis
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- A narrow facial recess: the round window is hard to find and the array hard to release from the forceps.
- A receiver-stimulator placed too far forward to keep the incision short.
- Rapid insertion, which damages the cochlea.
!!

# Aftercare | Dressing for a day.
## After the operation
Mastoid dressing for 24 hours; showering without scrubbing the wound after 48 hours. Mild pain is expected. Anticoagulants can restart on day 1.
## Complications
!table Complications
Complication | Notes
Misplaced array | Hypotympanic cell; prevent with exposure
Facial nerve injury | Explore if direct injury is suspected
Chorda tympani injury | Metallic taste for months, then gradual recovery
Annulus breach | Entry into the medial ear canal
Tegmen injury | CSF leak with a low tegmen
Haematoma | Theatre
Infection | Antibiotics; drainage often without removing the device
Meningitis | Slightly increased risk; vaccinate
!!
## Alternatives
Hearing aids; sign language and lip-reading.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Does it work?
Yes: one study showed open-set word recognition rising from a mean of **16%** (preoperative HINT) to **61%** (CNC) in the implanted ear. The editor notes that candidacy now includes people with good low-frequency hearing (hybrid or electroacoustic devices, which still beat aids if that hearing is lost), that testing has moved to harder sentence tests in noise (AzBio at +5 dB signal-to-noise ratio), and that bilateral implants improve localisation and hearing in noise, though most adults are content with one implant and an aid in the other ear.
!note A misprint in the paediatric criteria
The chapter gives the threshold for children over 2 years as a pure tone average "over 0 dB", which cannot be right; a digit appears to be missing. The intended criterion is severe to profound loss, or failure to progress with aids. Check current national candidacy criteria.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- Candidacy for adults, hybrid devices and children.
- Contraindications: cochlear or nerve aplasia, active infection.
- Pneumococcal vaccination and why.
- CT findings that matter (ossification, enlarged vestibular aqueduct, facial nerve, sigmoid, tegmen); MRI for the nerve.
- The facial recess, the round window 2 mm below the incudostapedial joint, scala tympani.
- Round window versus cochleostomy.
- Contacts toward the modiolus; slow insertion; advance off stylet.
- The well 2 cm behind the mastoid; monopolar off the field.
- Managing infection and facial palsy.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Missing an ossified cochlea after meningitis.
- Implanting with an active middle ear infection.
- Leaving fibrous tissue over the round window.
- Using monopolar diathermy with the device in the field.
!!
