@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand The central compartment, level VI, holds the prelaryngeal, pretracheal and paratracheal nodes; the superior mediastinal and retro-oesophageal nodes below it are often included as level VII. It is the first nodal basin of the thyroid and parathyroid and can be involved by cancers of the larynx and trachea, but its commonest disease is metastatic thyroid cancer: well-differentiated thyroid cancer reaches it in 20 to 50% of cases, and micrometastases may be present in up to 90%. Clinically involved nodes must be cleared; whether to clear the clinically negative central neck remains one of the field's lasting debates. The operation is a small field crowded with structures that matter: the recurrent laryngeal nerve on each side, four parathyroid glands with fragile blood supplies, the trachea and oesophagus, and the carotids and innominate vessels at its borders. Often it is a reoperation through scar. This chapter covers selection and work-up, the anatomy of the nerves and glands, the dissection from carotid to carotid, and the management of low calcium, a cut nerve and a haematoma.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Central compartment neck dissection
 | Summary
Boundaries | Carotids laterally; the innominate artery and vein below; the prelaryngeal, pretracheal and paratracheal nodes (level VI), often with superior mediastinal nodes (level VII)
Thyroid cancer | At initial surgery: **clinically involved central nodes**; BRAF-positive disease; gross extrathyroidal extension; suspicious paratracheal nodes (for example microcalcifications); lateral neck metastasis at presentation. Also listed: a contralateral nodule or distant metastasis, multinodular goitre with previous head and neck irradiation, first-degree relatives with thyroid cancer
Other uses | **Medullary thyroid carcinoma** (with or without nodal disease; may need lateral dissection too); **parathyroid carcinoma** (with ipsilateral lobectomy); squamous cancer involving the central viscera; recurrence
Caution | Uncontrolled medical disease; pregnancy (postpone to after delivery, or operate in the second trimester for substantial growth); significant distant metastasis unresponsive to suppression; **contralateral vocal fold paralysis**
Never | Unresectable mediastinal disease; intracranial metastasis
Anaesthesia | General, endotracheal
Position | Supine, shoulder roll, head in a ring; table moved away or turned 180 degrees; head and back up 10 to 30 degrees; prepare neck and upper chest (whole chest if a sternotomy is possible); arms tucked and padded; face and eyes protected
Antibiotics | A single preincision dose (cefazolin or clindamycin); none afterwards in clean surgery
Monitoring | Laryngeal nerve monitoring with a surface-electrode tube is optional; probably most useful in reoperations
Tray | Head and neck set; bipolar and monopolar diathermy, ultrasonic device if thyroidectomy is planned; clips in the room; Mahorner, Lone Star or Weitlaner retractors; peanut sponges; Babcock forceps; McCabe dissector
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to remove the lymph nodes around the windpipe that may hold cancer, usually with the thyroid.
- **Low calcium** from damage to the parathyroid glands: temporary or, less often, lifelong tablets.
- **Voice change** from injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve; if both are injured, breathing difficulty needing a tube or tracheostomy.
- **Loss of power and pitch** in the voice from the superior laryngeal nerve.
- **Bleeding** under the wound, which can press on the airway and needs urgent return to theatre.
- **Injury to the windpipe or gullet.**
- A scar in a neck crease.
!!
## Before you start
Is this primary or recurrent disease? Central dissection is often a **reoperation** for regional recurrence of thyroid cancer, so obtain earlier operation and pathology reports. Ask about COPD, cardiovascular disease and **gastric bypass or malabsorption** (which may need a calcium challenge if PTH is raised, to confirm absorption). Ask about haemoptysis, skin fixation and voice change, which suggest invasion and call for laryngoscopy and imaging. Risk factors for serious morbidity are **an existing vocal fold paralysis** (injury to the other side could mean tracheostomy), previous neck surgery or radiotherapy, and haemoptysis or cough. Note well-differentiated or medullary thyroid cancer and MEN.

Palpate the thyroid bed and paratracheal region (site of masses, movement with swallowing, fixation to skin or deep structures) and the lateral neck. Listen for hoarseness and stridor, and note a low larynx or cricoid. **Laryngoscopy is essential**: the voice can be normal despite a paralysed fold.
## Tests and imaging
- **Bloods:** TSH (the patient should be euthyroid); thyroglobulin and its antibodies (disease burden, especially in reoperation); calcitonin with a family history of medullary cancer or MEN 2, or medullary cytology; PTH.
- **Ultrasound:** cheap, no radiation, reliable in experienced hands; microcalcifications suggest malignancy, cystic and spongiform nodules suggest benign; but the thyroid shadows the central neck, so sensitivity for central nodes is low.
- **CT and MRI:** retrosternal extension, cervical and mediastinal nodes, invasion of trachea and oesophagus, distant disease; sensitive, specific and less operator-dependent. Iodinated contrast may delay radioiodine for 1 to 3 months.
- **Radionuclide scanning** for rising thyroglobulin with negative imaging; **PET** for radioiodine-negative, thyroglobulin-positive patients.
- **FNA** is essential before reoperation: sensitivity 65 to 98%, specificity 72 to 100%, better under ultrasound. Sample suspicious nodes; with several nodules, the largest or most suspicious, and more than one if needed.
- **Molecular markers** (BRAF, RAS, RET/PTC, PAX8-PPARγ, galectin-3 and others) may refine indeterminate cytology, but their value is uncertain.
Document vocal fold movement, ensure the patient is euthyroid, and map the disease and plan.

# Anatomy you must see | Nerves, glands and vessels.
## Vessels
- **Superior thyroid artery:** first branch of the external carotid (from the common carotid in 16%).
- **Inferior thyroid artery:** from the thyrocervical trunk.
- **Thyroidea ima artery** in the thyrothymic fat, in 2 to 12%, from the innominate, subclavian, right common carotid, internal thoracic or the arch, supplying the lower isthmus.
- **The carotids** form the lateral borders; **the innominate artery** is the lower limit (the right common carotid may arise early); the **innominate vein** lies below and the **jugular** system laterally.
## The recurrent laryngeal nerve
- **Left:** loops under the aortic arch behind the ligamentum arteriosum and rises in the tracheoesophageal groove.
- **Right:** leaves the vagus higher, loops round the subclavian and rises more laterally.
- It passes beneath the inferior constrictor, **behind the cricothyroid joint**, and enters the larynx below the cricoid, above the first ring.
- **Simon's triangle** (common carotid laterally, oesophagus medially, inferior thyroid artery above) contains it.
- It lies behind or between the branches of the inferior thyroid artery in 80%, and the relationship differs between sides in about half of patients. It is usually near the inferior parathyroid, mostly within **3 mm of Berry's ligament** (often lateral to it, sometimes through it), may pass through the thyroid capsule, and lies **deep to the tubercle of Zuckerkandl in 98%**.
- A **non-recurrent nerve** occurs in up to 0.7%, almost always on the **right** with an aberrant subclavian artery (or on the left in situs inversus), running directly into the larynx from the level of the thyroid cartilage or upper pole.
- **40 to 65%** divide into two or three branches, more than 5 mm below the cricoid.
## The superior laryngeal nerve
It leaves the nodose ganglion of the vagus about 4 cm above the bifurcation, runs down beside the pharynx behind the carotid sheath, and divides about 2 cm above the upper pole. The **internal branch** pierces the thyrohyoid membrane and gives sensation and secretomotor supply to the supraglottis, epiglottis, tongue base, pyriform sinus and upper sphincter. The **external branch** supplies cricothyroid and inferior constrictor; it lies in the **sternothyrolaryngeal (Joll's) triangle** (midline, upper pole and strap attachments), deep to the superior thyroid artery in 82 to 86%, and lies below or up to 1 cm above the upper pole in 37%.
## The parathyroids
- **Superior:** from the fourth branchial pouch, supplied by the superior or inferior thyroid vessels, usually above the cricoid; these can often be preserved.
- **Inferior:** from the third, variable in position, usually below the cricoid; the ones most often needing autotransplantation.
- Most receive blood from the **inferior thyroid artery**.

# The operation, stage by stage | From carotid to carotid.
## Mark and incise
Mark a **5 to 6 cm** horizontal incision between cricoid and sternal notch, usually 2 to 3 cm above the notch, in a crease that could be extended for a lateral dissection. Place it **higher in young patients**, because scars migrate down and one below the clavicle looks poor; if a sternotomy may be needed, at the clavicles or lower. Inject 0.25% bupivacaine with 1:200,000 adrenaline 1 to 2 cm above and below. Cut the dermis with a No. 15 blade and the platysma with diathermy, sparing the communicating anterior jugular veins.
!see What you should see
- An incision in a crease, symmetrical, with the platysma divided.
!!
## Raise and protect the flaps
Raise subplatysmal flaps just above the investing fascia and anterior jugular veins, up to the thyroid notch and down to the sternal notch, tying large vessels with 2-0 silk. Protect the skin edges with occlusive material such as Steri-Strips sewn on with a running locked 2-0 silk, to prevent heat and instrument injury.
!mf 1 | Occlusive skin protection with Steri-Strips held by 2-0 silk.
!see What you should see
- Flaps up to the thyroid notch and down to the sternal notch, with the skin edges covered.
!!
## Split the straps
Open the median raphe from thyroid notch to sternal notch and lift the straps off the thyroid (if present) and trachea with a Freer, a peanut or diathermy. Take the straps with a margin if tumour infiltrates them. In reoperations, or when the straps are stuck to the thyroid or trachea, find the **carotid lateral to the straps** first, and divide the straps horizontally if needed to reach the paratracheal compartment.
!see What you should see
- The trachea and thyroid bed in the midline, or the carotid lateral to stuck straps in a reoperation.
!!
## Find the carotid and the nerve
Rotate the thyroid and trachea medially and skeletonise the carotid. With a McCabe dissector, dissect near the **cricothyroid joint** and in the tracheoesophageal groove to find the **recurrent laryngeal nerve**; never close the clamp with its tips buried. Once found, dissect along and **directly on its sheath**, tracing it from the paratracheal region to cricothyroid, protecting both anterior and posterior branches. In reoperations, start laterally near the carotid.
!see What you should see
- The recurrent nerve in the tracheo-oesophageal groove entering beneath the inferior constrictor near the cricothyroid joint, stimulating.
!!
!danger Find the nerve before you clear the trachea
See the nerve before the thyroid or nodes are lifted off the trachea and oesophagus, and keep it in sight. That is the best protection against injury.
!!
## Remove the paratracheal packet
Hold the paratracheal tissue with a Babcock and pull it up and medially, away from the nerve. Remove all fibrofatty tissue and nodes over the trachea and medial to the carotid, down into the superior mediastinum.
!mf 2 | The paratracheal packet drawn medially away from the recurrent laryngeal nerve.
!see What you should see
- The trachea and oesophagus bare medially, the carotid laterally, the nerve skeletonised throughout, and the innominate artery at the lower limit on the right.
!!
## Save the parathyroids
Dissect each parathyroid **laterally with its blood supply**. Glands above the cricoid can often be kept. If an inferior gland is devascularised, confirm it on frozen section and **autotransplant** it; keep a low threshold.
!mf 3 | Paratracheal dissection with a lateral neck dissection, a parathyroid preserved medial to the recurrent laryngeal nerve.
!mf 4 | Bilateral paratracheal dissection, carotid to carotid, with superior parathyroid tissue preserved.
!see What you should see
- A trachea cleared from carotid to carotid and down to the innominate artery, with both nerves traced and intact and the superior parathyroids pink on their pedicles.
!!
## Haemostasis and closure
Irrigate and ask for a Valsalva; use bipolar diathermy and agents such as Surgicel. A drain is optional. Close the straps with 3-0 Vicryl, leaving a gap below so a haematoma can drain forward; close the platysma carefully and the skin with subcuticular 4-0 Monocryl, with an adhesive and Steri-Strips.
!see What you should see
- A dry bed on Valsalva, a stimulating nerve, and parathyroids that are pink or have been reimplanted.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Haematoma after surgery
Recognise | usually within 2 hours: breathlessness, difficulty swallowing, an expanding neck
Act at once | check the drain ; open the wound at the bedside and evacuate the clot to free the airway
Then | back to theatre to find and stop the bleeding
!!
!path Recurrent laryngeal nerve divided
Repair | immediate reanastomosis may give tone and reduce atrophy
Later | early medialisation of the fold
Both nerves | stridor and distress at extubation: reintubate, and tracheostomy may be needed
!!
!path Low calcium
Severe symptoms | intravenous calcium gluconate with cardiac monitoring
Then | oral calcium carbonate and vitamin D, temporarily or for life
!!
!path Trachea or oesophagus injured
During surgery | usually at Berry's ligament or the subcricoid trachea ; primary repair usually suffices
After surgery | surgical emphysema or increased drainage, often with wound infection ; usually self-limiting ; explore for persistent or large output
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Failing to save or to recognise devascularised parathyroids.
- Failing to identify the recurrent laryngeal nerve and preserve both branches.
- Leaving tissue over the trachea in the superior mediastinum.
- Poor haemostasis, which puts nerves and glands at risk.
- Stretching and burning the skin edges with hot instruments.
!!

# Aftercare | Calcium, voice, wound.
## After the operation
After total or completion thyroidectomy, check **PTH at 1 hour** in recovery; keep the patient in overnight if it is **under 15 pg/mL**, remembering that calcium may take over 12 hours to follow. Ibuprofen or paracetamol with an opioid for breakthrough pain. Keep the wound dry for 48 hours; review at a week and renew the Steri-Strips. Start thyroid replacement after total thyroidectomy; for radioiodine, withdraw thyroxine (liothyronine can bridge and be stopped 2 weeks before). Radioiodine depends on pathology and metastasis.
## Complications
!table Complications of central compartment dissection
Complication | Notes
Hypoparathyroidism | Risk with total or completion thyroidectomy and previous parathyroid surgery; IV calcium for severe symptoms, oral calcium and vitamin D
Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury | Weak, hoarse voice with the fold paramedian, possible aspiration; bilateral injury threatens the airway
Superior laryngeal nerve injury | Poor projection and high-pitch control; voice therapy
Haematoma | Within 2 hours; open the wound and return to theatre
Tracheo-oesophageal injury | Usually at Berry's ligament or the subcricoid trachea
Bleeding during surgery | Anterior jugular veins, the carotid (blunt dissection, know your depth), thyroid surface vessels
!!
## Alternatives
- Radioiodine.
- External beam radiotherapy.
- Tyrosine kinase inhibitors.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Prophylactic central dissection
For clinically involved central nodes, dissection is clearly indicated. For the **clinically negative** central neck there is no proven survival benefit and the effect on local control is equivocal; prophylactic dissection with total thyroidectomy raises **hypocalcaemia**, but may improve local control, avoid riskier reoperation and stage the disease for radioiodine decisions. A 2013 meta-analysis of 11 studies (2318 papillary cancers) found recurrence of **7.9%** after thyroidectomy alone and **4.7%** with prophylactic dissection, not statistically significant; high-volume surgeons' cohorts had 3.8%, and the number needed to treat was 31. Because high-volume surgeons have the fewest complications, prophylactic dissection deserves stronger consideration in their hands. Medullary cancer is always an indication.

## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Not checking the vocal folds before a reoperation.
- Operating without FNA proof of recurrence.
- Clearing the trachea before seeing the nerve.
- Taking an inferior parathyroid without reimplanting it.
