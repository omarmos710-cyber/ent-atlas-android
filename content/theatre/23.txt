@stand When a tracheostomy tube stays in for a long time, the skin can grow down the track until it meets the lining of the trachea. Once skin and mucosa have joined, the track is lined all the way and will not close by itself: a tracheocutaneous fistula. Even without a fistula, the skin often heals straight onto the trachea, leaving a hollow scar that rides up and down with every swallow. The cause is the same in both: the strap muscles were pushed aside for the tube and scarred in that position. So the cure is simple in principle: restore the anatomy. This chapter covers who needs closure and who must not have it, the adult repair that rebuilds the lining and brings the straps back to the midline, the modification for large defects, the simpler approach in children, and the air leak you must watch for afterwards.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Closure of a tracheocutaneous fistula and revision of a depressed scar
 | Summary
Indications | A fistula causing symptoms (weak voice, productive cough, saliva or secretions leaking through it, aspiration, pneumonia, infection at the site, an ineffective cough) or cosmetic concern, present for **at least 3 months after decannulation**; a depressed, tethered scar alone
Contraindications | Cannot tolerate the fistula being occluded, or airway obstruction on endoscopy; active infection at the site; chronic cough; the need for positive pressure ventilation (relative)
Anaesthesia | Adults: local anaesthetic with intravenous sedation preferred (avoids positive pressure during closure and coughing at emergence); general for a difficult airway. Children: general. Keep inspired oxygen low because of the fire risk with diathermy near the airway
Position | Supine, shoulder roll for maximal neck extension
Antibiotics | First-generation cephalosporin before the incision (the track is colonised)
Monitoring | Standard; overnight observation for adults, intensive care for children
Imaging | Non-contrast CT of the neck if endoscopy cannot confirm an adequate tracheal lumen
Tray | Tracheotomy or neck dissection set; diathermy and suction; a cuffed endotracheal or tracheostomy tube ready; size 3 or 4 metal (Pilling) tracheostomy tube for large defects; Penrose drain or rubber band drain; absorbable sutures
Before theatre | Flexible laryngoscopy and tracheoscopy to the carina; a 24-hour trial of occluding the fistula; sleep study with the fistula occluded if sleep apnoea is known or suspected
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **What it is:** removing the skin-lined track and the scar, lining the windpipe again, and bringing the neck muscles back together in the middle so the skin no longer sticks to the windpipe.
- **Air leak:** air can escape under the skin, around the lung or into the chest in up to about 3 in 100; you will be watched overnight for this.
- **Breathing difficulty**, which can mean reopening the wound or, rarely, a tracheostomy again; children are watched in intensive care.
- **The fistula can come back** if the closure is not airtight.
- **Infection** or the wound coming apart.
- **The scar** will still be there, but flatter and usually much better.
- **Sleep apnoea:** if you need a CPAP machine, closing the fistula may mean using it long term; it will usually be restarted after about two weeks.
!!
## Before you start
### The history
- Why and how long the tracheostomy was in place; was it an emergency (often a vertical incision) or planned?
- How long since decannulation, and any previous attempts to close it (more scar, lower chance of success).
- **Symptoms:** breathlessness, a weak voice, productive cough, saliva coming through the hole, swallowing trouble, pneumonia, infections at the site, cosmetic concern.
- **Heart and lung disease**, and any need for CPAP or other positive pressure, including for sleep apnoea.
- Neck radiation, diabetes and vascular disease (poor healing); other neck surgery; **smoking** (poor healing, and coughing at emergence raises the risk of air leak).
### The examination
- The site: a horizontal or vertical scar, the **size of the fistula**, any keloid or granulation tissue.
- Other neck scars; thyromental distance; mouth and pharynx for signs of a difficult airway or sleep apnoea (high arched palate, retracted tongue, Mallampati grade).
- **Flexible laryngoscopy and tracheoscopy down to the carina.** Look hardest at the **trachea just above the stoma and the subglottis**, the commonest sites of granulation and stenosis in children. Awake transnasal tracheoscopy in clinic under nebulised and topical lidocaine shows the airway moving; children who will not tolerate it have formal airway endoscopy at the start of the repair.
!flag The fistula that is keeping the patient alive
A fistula sometimes persists because the airway above it is too narrow and the hole is acting as a second airway. Close it and the patient may not be able to breathe. Prove the airway is adequate on endoscopy, and make sure the patient tolerates the fistula being occluded for **24 hours**, before you operate.
!!

# Anatomy you must see | Why the scar sinks.
## How the fistula forms
During tracheostomy the strap muscles are retracted laterally. With a tube in place for months, infection and fibrosis fix them in that lateral position. When the tube comes out and the stoma heals, the skin heals **directly onto the trachea**, with nothing between them: the scar sinks and moves with swallowing. If skin has grown down the track and met the tracheal mucosa, a **lined fistula** forms that cannot close on its own.
## Risk factors
- **Duration of cannulation:** about half of patients with a tube for over a year develop a persistent fistula.
- Previous neck radiation or previous tracheostomy.
- Infection, poor nutrition and weight loss (loss of subcutaneous tissue).
- A matured (skin-sutured) stoma has not been shown to increase the risk.
## Landmarks
- The **thyroid notch**, **cricoid cartilage** and **sternum**.
- The **strap muscles**, found scarred onto the sides of the trachea.

# The operation: adult repair | Lining, muscles, skin.
## Incision and flaps
Make a transverse elliptical incision around the fistula, extending a little to each side. Undermine the skin flaps widely and release the scar holding the skin to the deeper tissues.
!mf 2 | The tracheostomy scar excised almost down to the fistulous track.
!see What you should see
- Mobile skin flaps above and below, free of the scar.
!!
## Turn in the lining
Follow the track down to the trachea. Excise it, but keep just enough of the skin around the opening to turn inward like a **trapdoor**, so its epithelium forms the inner lining of the front wall of the trachea, and close it airtight.
!mf 3 | The skin of the track used as a trapdoor flap, turned in to restore the inner lining of the front wall of the trachea.
!danger An incomplete seal
Without an airtight closure and an inner epithelial lining, the fistula comes back. Check the closure: with the anaesthetist's help, look for bubbles under saline.
!!
!see What you should see
- The track followed to the tracheal wall, and a rim of skin turned in and sutured so that no air bubbles through saline when the anaesthetist gives a breath.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Before you plan a closure, be sure the patient no longer needs the stoma: endoscope the airway and confirm they tolerated a capped tube or occluded fistula. A closed fistula over an obstructed airway is an emergency.
!!
## Bring the straps back to the midline
Find the strap muscles, which lie buried in dense scar and stuck to the sides of the trachea. Dissect them **completely** free of the scar and the trachea, bring them back over the trachea, and sew them together in the midline with absorbable sutures.
!mf 4 | The strap muscles repositioned in the midline and sutured together; then the subcutaneous tissue and skin are closed.
!see What you should see
- Muscle lying between the trachea and the skin across the whole defect.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
The midline strap repair is the heart of this operation. It separates the skin from the trachea, fills the hollow, stops the scar riding with swallowing and reinforces the closure; with it, muscle flaps and Z-plasties are rarely needed. Half-freed straps spring back and the hollow returns.
!!
## Drain and close
Leave a small rubber band or Penrose drain just deep to the straps, brought out at the side of the incision, so any air leaking from the trachea can escape. Close the subcutaneous tissue without tension and the skin with fine absorbable sutures; a Z-plasty or a geometric broken-line closure makes a smaller, smoother scar if needed. Apply strips to take the tension.
!see What you should see
- A flat closure with no tension, and the drain sitting deep to the muscles.
!!
### A depressed scar without a fistula
The same operation, minus the fistula excision: excise the scar, release the skin, free the straps and bring them together in the midline. Returning the straps to their place removes the hollow and stops it coming back.
!mf 5 | Planned excision of a depressed tracheostomy scar, and its appearance a week after repair.

# The operation: large defects and children | Two modifications.
## A defect larger than 4 mm
Closing a large hole in the front of the trachea in one stage risks air leak, pneumothorax and respiratory failure. Instead, dissect out the fistula and scar through a horizontal ellipse and excise the track down to the trachea. If the defect is **over 4 mm**, place a size 3 or 4 metal tracheostomy tube in the old site. Raise the straps and close them in the midline **around the tube**, undermine the skin widely, and close it around the tube. Cap the tube in recovery or the next day, decannulate on **day 2**, and let the small central area heal by secondary intention.
!see What you should see
- A tube sitting snugly in the trachea with the straps already reunited around it.
!!
## Children
Use the **least complex** method: under general anaesthetic, excise the epithelium lining the track and let it close by secondary intention. It closes quickly and watertight, so the child can swim and bathe, and avoids a long anaesthetic and handling of a small trachea that could swell and obstruct. Revise the scar later if needed. Systematic review found no difference in closure between primary and secondary closure in children (about 96% and 92%), with similar airway complication rates (under 4%), so secondary closure is preferred.
!danger Swelling in a small airway
In a child, oedema after handling the trachea can obstruct the airway. Keep the operation small, and monitor the child in intensive care overnight.
!!
!see What you should see
- A raw, de-epithelialised track with no skin left in its depth, and a small occlusive dressing over it.
!!
!senior Senior's pearl
Endoscope the child's airway first: suprastomal granulation or collapse is the usual reason a stoma has not closed on its own.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Swelling, crackling skin or breathlessness after closure
Recognise | surgical emphysema, pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum from air escaping through the tracheal closure (up to about 3%), worse with coughing or positive pressure
Act | open the wound at the bedside and pass a cuffed tracheostomy tube through the stoma ; give positive pressure through that tube if needed
Never | give positive pressure by face mask, which forces more air into the tissues
Then | chest X-ray ; chest drain for a significant pneumothorax
!!
!path Airway obstruction after closure
Act | keep a tracheostomy set at the bedside ; reopen the stoma and reinsert a tube
Then | reassess the airway endoscopically before any further attempt
!!
!path The fistula comes back
Cause | an incomplete seal or no inner lining
Act | repeat repair once healed, ensuring a trapdoor lining and an airtight closure
!!

# Aftercare | One night of watching.
## After the operation
- **Adults:** admit to a monitored bed overnight; home the next day.
- **Children:** intensive care overnight.
- Drain out on **day 1**; a tracheostomy tube, if used, out after a capping trial, usually on **day 2**.
- Wound care is minimal; strips off at 7 to 10 days.
- A tracheostomy set at the bedside.
- **Positive pressure (CPAP)** restarted after 2 weeks, unless sleep apnoea is severe, when it is restarted the same evening, accepting a higher risk of airway complications.
- For patients with chronic cough or lung disease, excising the track and letting it heal by secondary intention lowers the risk of air leak.
## Complications
!table Complications of fistula closure
Complication | Notes
Subcutaneous emphysema, pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum | Up to about 3%; coughing and positive pressure; mostly after single-stage closure of large defects
Respiratory compromise | Inadequate airway above the stoma; oedema in children
Recurrent fistula | Incomplete seal, no lining
Persistent depressed scar | Straps not fully freed or not reunited
Infection, dehiscence | Colonised track; smoking, diabetes, radiation
!!
## Alternatives
Observation; excision of the track with healing by secondary intention; chemical or diathermy cautery of a very small track where appearance does not matter; and older techniques such as bipedicled or turnover flaps, sternocleidomastoid or sternohyoid muscle flaps, subcutaneous Z-plasty and V-Y advancement. In adults there is little evidence favouring one technique, but primary closure gives the better cosmetic result.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Where the books differ
!differ
Dhingra: covers this from the tracheostomy side: the tube should not stay longer than needed, because prolonged cannulation causes infection, ulceration, granulation, stenosis and unsightly scars; decannulation follows 24 hours of tolerating a plugged tube (a smaller tube in children), with children watched closely afterwards; and failed decannulation is explained by persistent disease, granulation at or below the stoma, tracheal oedema or subglottic stenosis, an infolded tracheal wall at the stoma, tracheomalacia, or psychological dependence.
Lee: lists a persistent tracheocutaneous fistula after decannulation among the delayed complications of tracheostomy, with granulation, stomal infection, stenosis, tracheomalacia, tracheo-oesophageal and tracheo-innominate fistulae and tube displacement.
Myers gives the repair: 3 months' wait after decannulation, airway endoscopy and a 24-hour occlusion trial first, the trapdoor epithelial lining, the midline strap muscle repair, a drain for air, the tube-based method for defects over 4 mm, secondary closure in children, and the bedside drill for air leak.
!!
## Viva essentials
!exam What the examiner is looking for
- **Why** a tracheocutaneous fistula forms and persists (an epithelium-lined track).
- **Why the scar is depressed** (strap muscles scarred laterally).
- **Before closing:** airway endoscopy and a 24-hour occlusion trial, and why.
- The **two key steps**: an epithelial trapdoor lining and the midline strap repair.
- The method for a **defect over 4 mm**.
- **Children:** excision with secondary closure, and intensive care monitoring.
- The **air leak drill**: open the wound, cuffed tube through the stoma, never mask ventilation.
- **Decannulation** principles and causes of failed decannulation.
!!
## Common pitfalls
!pit
- Closing a fistula that is serving as an airway.
- Operating before 3 months.
- No inner lining, or a closure that is not airtight.
- Straps left half-freed.
- No drain for air.
- Mask ventilation for an air leak.
- A long, complex repair in a small child.
!!
