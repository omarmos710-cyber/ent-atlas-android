@src Myers, Operative Otolaryngology
@stand The skin and muscle of the face and neck supply three flaps that have returned to favour after the free flap era: the cervicofacial flap, the supraclavicular artery flap and the submental island flap. They match facial skin in colour and texture as no distant tissue can, they lie next to the defect, they need no microsurgery, and they shorten the operation and the hospital stay. The cervicofacial flap is a random-pattern rotation-advancement of cheek and neck skin for the cheek and lateral face; the supraclavicular flap is a thin axial flap from the shoulder, a regional answer to the radial forearm; the submental flap is a versatile island on the submental vessels that can carry muscle and even bone. Each lives or dies by the geometry of its pedicle and by a haematoma that must not form. This chapter covers selection, the anatomy of each pedicle, the three operations, and how to rescue a failing flap.

# Operating note | The case on one page.
## The case at a glance
!table Cervicofacial region flaps
 | Summary
Flap types | **Random pattern:** cervicofacial. **Axial pattern:** cervicopectoral, supraclavicular, deltopectoral, submental
Cervicofacial | Cheek and lateral face defects, especially thin superficial ones; not if its blood supply is doubtful (size, base, radiotherapy, surgery, vascular disease) or the patient cannot accept a revision
Supraclavicular | Neck, lateral skull base, floor of mouth and lower face; pharyngectomy defects; caution after level IV or supraclavicular neck dissection (the perforator may be gone)
Submental island | Facial defects needing colour match and some bulk; oral cavity, oropharynx and laryngectomy defects; lateral and anterior skull base and orbit; not after neck surgery that divided the submental perforators, nor with bulky level Ib nodes or possible extracapsular spread
Anaesthesia | General; paralysis at the surgeon's discretion depending on the nerves at risk; facial nerve monitoring when branches are at risk
Position | Supine, shoulder roll or neck turned away during elevation; remove rolls and return to neutral if the closure is tight
Antibiotics | Depending on the defect and risk factors
Tray | Head and neck set; bipolar; sterile Doppler; fluorescent angiography (SPY) to predict viability
!!
## Consent
!consent What to tell the patient
- **Why:** to close the defect with nearby skin of matching colour and texture, carried on its own blood supply.
- **Part of the flap may fail**, especially its tip; if you smoke, stop for at least 2 weeks beforehand.
- **A fold of skin** (dog ear) may be left and trimmed at a later small operation.
- **A collection of blood or fluid** under the flap, which may need draining.
- **Facial weakness** (lower lip or face) if a facial nerve branch is injured; **shoulder weakness** if the accessory nerve is injured during a muscle flap.
- **Infection**, especially from a saliva leak.
- **Scars** on the neck, chest or shoulder.
!!
## Before you start
Define the defect and its tissues: replace like with like, skin with skin of similar colour, thickness and texture, multiple layers with a flap carrying the same layers. Assess swallowing and breathing, and the possible need for a tracheostomy and nasogastric feeding. Ask about previous head and neck surgery and radiotherapy, diabetes (especially if poorly controlled), peripheral vascular disease, coagulopathy (also in the family), drugs that increase bleeding (a haematoma threatens the flap), and **nicotine**, which constricts arteries and must stop around surgery.

Examine the face and neck for scars or earlier surgery that may have cut the blood supply, and measure the defect. For a **submental flap**, do the **pinch test** with the neck slightly extended to see how much skin can be taken while still closing the donor site without limiting head extension. For a **supraclavicular flap**, a **Doppler** must trace the pedicle from the supraclavicular fossa over the clavicle to the deltoid; mark the signals. Contrast CT can show whether pedicle vessels survive after previous surgery; CT angiography or angiography is rarely needed.

# Anatomy you must see | Three blood supplies.
## Cervicofacial flap
A **random-pattern** fasciocutaneous flap of facial and cervical skin, better called a local than a regional flap. It moves by **rotation as well as advancement**, needs a **broad base** to recruit enough vessels, and is weakest at its distal edges.
## Supraclavicular flap
Its cutaneous perforator, from the transverse cervical artery, lies in the **supraclavicular triangle**, bounded by the **anterior border of trapezius** behind, the **posterior border of sternomastoid** in front and the **clavicle** below. The perforator is the fulcrum of rotation; beyond the deltoid tip the supply becomes random. The **cephalic vein** lies on its deep surface.
## Submental island flap
The **submental artery** arises from the facial artery deep to or within the submandibular gland, runs forward over the gland on the superficial surface of **mylohyoid**, and sends perforators up on both sides of the **anterior belly of digastric**. The **submental vein** is more variable: usually a branch of the facial vein, less often draining to the external jugular, and always **superficial and lateral to the artery**. Losing the vein during exposure can doom the flap. The **marginal mandibular nerve** lies over the facial vessels and gland.

# The operation: cervicofacial flap | Rotation and advancement.
## Design
Start the design at the **posterior edge of the defect**. Carry the upper limb back to the preauricular region and around the lobule into the postauricular tissue (to increase the arc), down to the mastoid tip, then down the neck about **2 cm behind the anterior edge of trapezius**, turning forward in a natural crease at the level of the clavicle. Size the flap to the defect, reassessing during surgery until it rotates without tension.
!mf 1 | A right anterior cheek defect after skin cancer excision: (A) the design, continued around the lobule and down to the clavicle; (B) the incisions; (C) elevation in the subcutaneous plane to avoid the facial nerve; (D) the result, with the postauricular limb and the dog ear excised and a drain in place.
!see What you should see
- A flap outline that begins at the defect's posterior edge and reaches it when rotated on paper.
!!
## Raise and rotate
Raise the flap **below the platysma in the neck** and **above the SMAS in the face**, protecting the facial nerve branches. Rotate and advance it into the defect.
!see What you should see
- The flap in the defect without tension or blanching at its tip, facial nerve branches untouched beneath the SMAS.
!!
!danger The standing cone
Rotation leaves a standing cone at the base. If you have any doubt about the flap's blood supply, **leave it**, and remove it at a later stage once the wound edges have revascularised the flap: excising it now can cut the supply to the distal flap.
!!
## Drain and dress
Almost always place a drain; a haematoma under the flap compromises it. A light compressive dressing keeps the flap on its bed, especially without a drain.
!mf 2 | The final appearance after a cervicofacial rotation-advancement flap: an excellent colour and texture match.
!see What you should see
- The flap lying flat on its bed with the drain working.
!!

# The operation: supraclavicular flap | A thin flap from the shoulder.
## Design
Design an elongated ellipse over the supraclavicular region toward the **deltoid tip**, around the Doppler-mapped pedicle. A width of **6 to 8 cm** usually allows primary closure; **10 to 16 cm** can be raised reliably but the donor site will need a skin graft. It can be up to **26 cm** long, but extending beyond the deltoid tip makes the supply random: extend **no more than 5 cm beyond the last Doppler signal**.
!mf 3 | A right supraclavicular flap for a floor of mouth defect: (A) the pedicle mapped by Doppler from the triangle between sternomastoid, clavicle and trapezius over the shoulder to the deltoid tip; (B) the flap raised, the donor edges held with towel clips for primary closure.
!see What you should see
- A Doppler signal in the triangle between the sternocleidomastoid, the clavicle and the external jugular vein, with the paddle axis running from it to the deltoid.
!!
## Raise
Raise from **distal to proximal** in the **subfascial** plane, keeping a skin base or making it an island on the perforator for maximum arc. Protect the cephalic vein. Diathermy is fine distally, but release the fibrous attachments to the clavicle and trapezius **sharply**. Close the donor site primarily after undermining in most cases, and drain it.
!see What you should see
- The pedicle entering the flap's deep surface proximally, and a flap that reaches the defect without stretching.
!!

# The operation: submental island flap | Find the vein, keep the fascia.
## Design
Choose fasciocutaneous, myocutaneous (including ipsilateral mylohyoid and anterior digastric, which simplifies the dissection and protects the pedicle on the mylohyoid), more bulk (adding the opposite mylohyoid and digastric), or osteomyocutaneous (with the lower mentum on midline bony perforators, useful for small maxillary bony defects). Find the dominant perforators by Doppler near the digastric insertions. Place the **upper incision over the lower border of the mandible**, not below it (which endangers the perforators), extending as far as needed (it can run angle to angle). Use the pinch test to set the lower limit. Paddles up to about **17 × 9 cm** survive on one pedicle; the average pedicle length of **8 cm** reaches from the zygoma to the clavicle.
!see What you should see
- A paddle whose lower edge can be pinched closed, with a Doppler signal beneath it.
!!
## Raise
1. Open the medial upper incision onto the mandible in the midline; free the flap subperiosteally with a Cottle elevator and release the anterior digastric(s) from the mandible.
2. Open the ipsilateral incision to the lateral limit, down to platysma; divide the posterior platysma to expose the submandibular gland.
3. Find the **marginal mandibular nerve** over the facial vessels and gland, and keeping it in view divide the platysma above to join the two dissections; sweep the nerve up off the gland fascia and out of the field.
4. Find the pedicle at the deep posterior part of the gland, the vein superficial and lateral to the artery, draining to the common facial or external jugular vein.
5. Trace the vessels through the gland, dividing the branches to the gland, keeping a **broad fascial cuff** from pedicle to skin that carries the perforators.
6. Keep or remove the gland (if removing it, free it from the hypoglossal and lingual nerves and divide the duct).
7. Follow the pedicle over mylohyoid, clearing level I nodes and fat in cancer surgery; if level I clearance is not needed, leave the fat around the pedicle. To include mylohyoid, release it from the mylohyoid line and the hyoid.
8. Release the digastric from the hyoid and free the opposite side, above the platysma if no bulk is needed (protecting that side's nerves), or including platysma, mylohyoid and digastric for bulk.
9. Inset; ligate the **facial artery beyond the submental origin** to raise perfusion pressure. Drain the submental area, where seromas are common.
!mf 4 | A submental island flap for a lateral temporal bone defect: (A) the tumour and the flap drawn, the upper incision on the lower border of the mandible; (B) the paddle raised on the facial-submental pedicle and rotated; (C) inset, the donor site closed along the mandible.
!see What you should see
- The submental artery and vein running on mylohyoid into the flap, the marginal mandibular nerve above them, and the facial vessels in continuity.
!!
!danger Thinning the fascia
Thinning the fascia between pedicle and skin, especially over the digastric, destroys the perforators. When neck dissections are also planned, **raise the flap first** so its vessels are found and preserved.
!!

# When it goes wrong | Drills.
## Crisis drills
!path Arterial compromise
Cause | compression from poor vessel geometry, a stretched pedicle or a haematoma
Act | return to theatre to explore the pedicle and restore flow
!!
!path Venous compromise (commoner)
Cause | kinking or compression from geometry or haematoma
Act | return to theatre to explore and restore outflow
If flow cannot be restored | leeches and heparin scrub may salvage the flap
!!
!path Haematoma or seroma
Haematoma | find and evacuate it quickly, and secure haemostasis
Seroma | usually needle aspiration in clinic ; an undrained seroma can cause infection and flap loss
!!
## Common errors in technique
!pit
- Excising the standing cone when the flap's supply is in doubt.
- An upper submental incision placed below the mandible.
- Thinning the fascia over the pedicle.
- Doing the neck dissection before raising the submental flap.
- Extending a supraclavicular flap more than 5 cm beyond the Doppler signal.
!!

# Aftercare | Drains and watchfulness.
## After the operation
Close monitoring of pedicled flaps is debatable, since pedicle compromise is unlikely; check Doppler, capillary refill, colour and warmth as you judge. Keep drains until under **30 mL in 24 hours** and clear. No activity limits; analgesia as needed.
## Complications
!table Complications
Complication | Notes
Arterial or venous compromise | Venous is commoner; explore early
Infection | Usually late (after day 5), from a salivary leak; nil by mouth and oral care help
Poor microcirculation | Obesity, diabetes, vascular disease, radiotherapy
Haematoma | Evacuate quickly
Seroma | Aspirate
Nerve injury | Facial branches (cervicofacial, submental), accessory nerve (sternomastoid flap)
!!
## Alternatives
Secondary healing; skin grafts; other regional flaps (pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi); free flaps for larger, complex defects, at the cost of longer operations.

# For the examination | What the examiner wants to hear.
## Regional flap or free flap?
Comparisons of the submental flap with radial forearm and anterolateral thigh free flaps for glossectomy and lateral skull base defects found **shorter operations and hospital stays** with the submental flap, with **no difference** in function or flap survival. The supraclavicular flap has likewise been compared with free flaps for hemiglossectomy and lateral skull base defects. The editor notes that these flaps serve especially well in vessel-depleted necks and patients who cannot tolerate long anaesthetics.

!pit
- Operating on a smoker without a nicotine-free fortnight.
- Missing the effect of previous level IV or level Ib surgery on the pedicle.
- Closing a donor site under tension instead of grafting it.
