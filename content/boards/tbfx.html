TEMPORAL BONE FRACTURE

and CSF otorrhoea: is the face moving, can they hear, is CSF leaking? ✎

1Who and why it matters

  • Road traffic accidents > assaults, falls · young men (second to fourth decades), ~three times women
  • Temporal bone involved in 30–70% of head injuries; a true fracture in only 14–22% of these
  • Holds cochlea, labyrinth, facial nerve, ossicles, carotid, dura → document face + hearing early
  • Trauma care first, the ear second

Modern: the otic capsule

Sparing = most · violating ~6% → far more facial palsy, SNHL, CSF leak, meningitis · predicts outcome better; mixed fractures don't fit the old scheme · know both for exams

3Look, listen, test the face

  • Base of skull: Battle's sign (mastoid bruising after a day or two), periorbital bruising, ↓ consciousness
  • Ear: bleeding + torn drum / canal step (longitudinal) · blue-black haemotympanum behind intact drum (transverse)
  • Conductive: haemotympanum (commonest early) · perforation · ossicles
  • Sensorineural: through cochlea or labyrinthine concussion; often permanent · mixed common
  • Vertigo: severe + nystagmus (transverse) · milder positional (concussion, BPPV)
  • CSF: clear fluid from the ear through a torn drum, or from the nose via the tube (intact drum)
IS joint separation→incus dislocation→stapes crura fracture
ossicular injury by frequency · IS joint ~80% · gap ≥30 dB
Facial nerve: record immediate or delayed, complete or not · immediate complete → transection / bone spicule · delayed → oedema, usually recovers

4Image the bone, confirm the fluid

  • HRCT temporal bones: fracture line, capsule, ossicles, facial canal, tegmen · CT angiography if it crosses the carotid canal
  • Beta-2 transferrin confirms CSF
  • Audiogram as soon as practical (two to three days, once awake) and again at six to eight weeks
  • ENoG for a complete palsy

5Trauma care first

trauma protocol, neuro→face before sedation→haemotympanum, step, CSF, nystagmus→HRCT (± CTA)
Don't syringe, probe or pack the ear deeply
ProblemPlan
CSF leakmost stop in a week or two: head up, bed rest, stool softeners, no nose blowing / straining · no routine prophylactic antibiotics · persists or meningitis → lumbar drain / repair
Facial palsyimmediate complete + nerve injury → explore, decompress / repair · delayed → steroids, observe, serial ENoG

6The figures

  • Facial palsy in about a quarter: half of transverse, a tenth to a fifth of longitudinal
  • ~three-quarters delayed (one to sixteen days) → good recovery
  • CSF leak ~17%
  • Sunnybrook records synkinesis better than House–Brackmann in recovery

7Early and late complications

  • Meningitis, early or years later
  • Permanent SNHL · facial palsy
  • Late cholesteatoma (skin trapped in the line)
  • Chronic vertigo, perilymph fistula
  • Carotid injury, dissection
The capsule heals only by fibrous union → leaks recur with coughing / straining, meningitis years later, meningo- / encephalocele (conductive loss, effusion, meningitis) → pneumococcal vaccine + advice to seek help for fever + headache

8Evidence

Brodie & Thompson 1997 (820 fractures): capsule-violating → far more palsy, CSF leak, SNHL · Little & Kesser 2006: capsule scheme predicts better · Cochrane 2015: no evidence prophylactic antibiotics prevent meningitis · decompression for immediate complete palsy rests on case series (ENoG >90% within two weeks)

9Exam traps ✗

  • No facial record before sedation
  • Syringing or packing a bleeding ear
  • Calling a conductive loss permanent before the blood clears
  • Missing late meningitis or cholesteatoma
