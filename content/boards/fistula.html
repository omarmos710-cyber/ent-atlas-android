2How infection gets in

  • Middle ear: cholesteatoma eroding the lateral canal (commonest) · oval / round window in AOM or COM · surgery, trauma
  • Meninges: via cochlear aqueduct or IAC · pneumococcus, H. influenzae, meningococcus · pneumococcus: highest deafness risk ~20%
  • Blood: viral, syphilitic

5The eroded lateral canal

Cholesteatoma in most · tumours, trauma, surgery · 7–10% of cholesteatomas (5–10% in some reviews) · 90% lateral canal
Brief vertigo on pressure: tragus, cleaning, nose blowing · hearing often kept

6Fistula test & tests

Fistula test

tragal pressure / Siegle → vertigo + nystagmus · +ve pressure: fast phase usually towards the ear; −ve: away
Specific, insensitive: positive in only 30–50% · negative in a dead labyrinth or when cholesteatoma seals it
Assume a fistula in every cholesteatoma · HRCT shows only ~60% before surgery → expect one with vertigo or a +ve test
  • Bedside: fistula test, nystagmus direction, tuning forks · every patient: pure-tone audiogram
  • HRCT temporal bones = gold standard for a bony fistula · MRI with contrast + heavy T2: labyrinthitis, meningitis, fibrosis / ossification before implant
  • Also +ve test: Ménière's (Hennebert), SCD, perilymph fistula, congenital syphilis

7Fistula surgery

  • Small: matrix removed carefully at the end of the operation, defect sealed (fascia, bone pâté)
  • Large / only hearing ear: leave the matrix, exteriorise the ear
  • Remove at once vs leave for a second stage = debated · in experienced hands removal + sealing keeps hearing in most · steroids often given

8Serous · suppurative

  • Serous: vertigo, nausea, nystagmus to affected ear, some SNHL that recovers if treated · Rx: bed rest, affected ear up, full-dose antibiotics, labyrinthine sedatives, myringotomy if bulging · mastoidectomy for a chronic source
  • Suppurative: severe vertigo + vomiting, nystagmus to healthy ear, total deafness, toxic · vertigo settles over three to six weeks (compensation) · Rx: IV antibiotics, remove source, rarely drain labyrinth
  • Complications: meningitis along the IAC · labyrinthitis ossificans

9Labyrinthitis ossificans

suppurative (esp. meningitic)→fibrous tissue→new bone, basal turn first (round window)
within weeks to months
Deafness after meningitis: test hearing in every patient · bilateral profound → urgent MRI + CT → early cochlear implant · steroids reduce loss in H. influenzae + pneumococcal meningitis

10Perilymph fistula

  • After: stapes surgery, barotrauma, diving, lifting, straining, coughing, head injury · unoperated ear: annular ligament or round window · spontaneous = controversial
  • Fluctuating / sudden loss, roaring tinnitus, disequilibrium, Hennebert's sign
  • Penetrating ear injury + vertigo + loss → stapes subluxation into the vestibule
  • Rx: bed rest 7–10 days, head up, no straining, stool softeners → explore + patch if hearing worsens or symptoms persist

11Viral & syphilitic

  • Mumps: usually one-sided profound loss · measles, rubella
  • CMV = commonest non-syndromic cause of congenital SNHL; may progress after a normal newborn screen
  • Late congenital syphilis at 8–20 years: bilateral sudden / fluctuating loss + vertigo + Hennebert
  • Tests: VDRL / RPR + FTA-ABS (treponemal EIA) · lumbar puncture with GUM

Treat as neurosyphilis

procaine penicillin 1.8–2.4 million units IM daily + probenecid 500 mg four times a day × 14 days · allergic: doxycycline 200 mg twice a day × 28 days · prednisolone 40–60 mg daily × 3 days, starting 24 h before penicillin

12Viva & traps ✗

Viva: three types (circumscribed, serous, suppurative) · fistula test: direction, why it can be negative · nystagmus in serous vs suppurative · ossificans → early implant · perilymph fistula: causes, Rx
  • Reassured by a negative fistula test
  • No hearing test after meningitis
  • Missing late congenital syphilis in a young adult with fluctuating bilateral loss
